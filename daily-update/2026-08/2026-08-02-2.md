### 🎯 Strategy

*Today's batch: 30 Farnam Street mental-model notes, plus one applied strategy piece.*

- **[Critical mass: the 10% threshold that makes ideas self-sustaining](../../wiki/strategy/critical-mass-and-tipping-points.md)**: Once committed opinion-holders exceed ~10% of a population, ideas spread rapidly and become self-reinforcing. Borrowed from nuclear physics; applies to product adoption, culture change, and social movements.

- **[Leverage: why small inputs at the right fulcrum produce outsized results](../../wiki/strategy/leverage-gaining-disproportionate-strength.md)**: Archimedes' lever generalized to strategy — finding the fulcrum point where minimal effort yields maximum effect. The practical skill is identifying where leverage exists before applying force.

- **[Circle of competence: the boundary matters more than the size](../../wiki/strategy/circle-of-competence.md)**: Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger built Berkshire by staying ruthlessly inside domains of deep, earned knowledge and refusing everything outside. Operating inside your circle gives an edge; straying outside transfers it to better-informed competitors.

- **[Occam's Razor: prefer the hypothesis with the fewest assumptions](../../wiki/strategy/occams-razor.md)**: The law of parsimony is a starting heuristic, not a final verdict — simpler explanations are easier to test and execute before complete information exists. The limit: confirmation bias can distort which explanation you perceive as simpler.

- **[Black Swan: why unknown unknowns dominate history](../../wiki/strategy/what-is-a-black-swan.md)**: Nassim Taleb's three-property definition — outside regular expectations, extreme impact, rationalized only in hindsight — captures why prediction anchored in past patterns systematically misses the most consequential events. The implication is designing for robustness, not optimization.

- **[Emergence: novel properties that can't be predicted from parts](../../wiki/strategy/emergence-and-power.md)**: The 13th-century Provence case study — four sisters who became queens of four kingdoms through mutually reinforcing marriages — illustrates collective political power no individual held. Emergence is why systems behave differently at scale than their components suggest.

- **[Probabilistic thinking: Bayesian reasoning + fat tails + metaprobability](../../wiki/strategy/probabilistic-thinking.md)**: Shane Parrish's three-component framework — estimating outcome likelihoods, accounting for fat-tailed distributions, and tracking uncertainty about your own probability estimates. WWII spy Vera Atkins demonstrates that rigorous probabilistic reasoning improves decision quality without guaranteeing outcomes.

- **[First principles: dismantle to confirmed truths, rebuild from scratch](../../wiki/strategy/first-principles-thinking.md)**: The method Elon Musk used to cut SpaceX's rocket costs by 10× — question every default assumption and reason upward from bedrock rather than iterating on existing solutions. The difficulty of the approach is precisely what makes it a durable competitive advantage.

- **[Hanlon's Razor: assume negligence before malice](../../wiki/strategy/hanlons-razor.md)**: Incompetence and inattention account for far more harmful outcomes than deliberate bad acts. The recommended default is broad good faith — with ruthlessness reserved for confirmed cases of malice. Predates its naming; appears in Goethe and Napoleon.

- **[Margin of safety: design for extremes, not averages](../../wiki/strategy/margin-of-safety.md)**: The buffer between current conditions and failure must be sized by the cost of failure, the cost of the buffer, and the degree of uncertainty. Unglamorous but foundational — the gap between long-term survivors and those undone by surprise.

- **[Redundancy: multiple independent paths reduce failure probability multiplicatively](../../wiki/strategy/mental-model-redundancy.md)**: Redundant systems are powerful — but the Challenger disaster shows how overconfidence in redundant safety components can paradoxically increase catastrophic risk by encouraging riskier behavior and adding complexity. Redundancy works when the failure modes of backup systems are truly independent.

- **[The map is not the territory: every model is a simplification with a cost](../../wiki/strategy/map-is-not-the-territory.md)**: Maps compress messy reality into something manageable, but that compression strips detail and reflects the values of their creators. The lesson: engage with the world as it is, not as your models describe it — and choose your sources of abstraction wisely.

- **[Signaling theory: costly actions are credible proxies for unobservable qualities](../../wiki/strategy/signaling-theory.md)**: Peacock tails, gazelle stotting, and Veblen goods all operate on the same logic — honest signals are hard to fake because they require real expenditure of time, energy, or resources. Easy-to-fake signals quickly lose credibility as they spread.

- **[Anchoring: the first number heard pulls all subsequent estimates toward it](../../wiki/strategy/mental-model-anchoring.md)**: Tversky and Kahneman's finding — even arbitrary anchors distort estimates through selective accessibility, not insufficient adjustment. Both experts and novices are susceptible; greater domain knowledge reduces vulnerability to irrelevant anchors but not to relevant ones.

- **[Inversion: solve hard problems by thinking backward from failure](../../wiki/strategy/inversion-avoiding-stupidity.md)**: Charlie Munger's application of Jacobi's "invert, always invert" — ask what would cause failure, then eliminate those conditions. Avoiding stupidity is easier and more reliable than seeking brilliance, and produces a different, complementary decision-making lens.

- **[Dunning-Kruger: the skills needed to perform are the skills needed to evaluate](../../wiki/strategy/dunning-kruger-effect.md)**: The least skilled are the most confident because they lack the metacognitive capacity to recognize their own incompetence — a self-reinforcing trap. The correction requires external feedback or a deliberate effort to build the metacognitive layer alongside domain skill.

- **[Island biogeography: equilibrium via immigration vs. extinction dynamics](../../wiki/strategy/island-equilibrium-immigration-extinction.md)**: MacArthur and Wilson's model — species counts on islands stabilize where immigration rate equals local extinction rate; size and remoteness set the equilibrium level. The model generalizes: isolation, immigration pressure, and extinction dynamics describe businesses, ideas, and any bounded system under competitive pressure.

- **[Power laws: Y = MX^B, and why the exponent changes everything](../../wiki/strategy/power-laws-nonlinear-relationships.md)**: When B > 1, small input increases produce accelerating output returns — creating leverage points that reward concentrated effort over evenly distributed effort. Power-law thinking is the antidote to linear intuition, which systematically underestimates both tails.

- **[Survivorship bias: invisible failures distort our model of what works](../../wiki/strategy/survivorship-bias.md)**: Focusing on visible successes while ignoring the far more numerous failures makes outliers like Bill Gates or the Beatles look like templates when timing, luck, and context were decisive. The fix: actively seek out the failures that never made it into the dataset.

- **[Reciprocity: actions produce reactions, and giving compounds over time](../../wiki/strategy/reciprocity-getting-what-you-give.md)**: From Newton's Third Law through evolutionary biology to social psychology — positive reciprocal behavior compounds, and win-win relationships are strategically superior to zero-sum ones. Indirect reciprocity (reputation effects) multiplies the return beyond direct exchange.

- **[Activation energy: the starting barrier determines whether momentum forms](../../wiki/strategy/activation-energy-mental-model.md)**: The minimum energy required to begin a reaction — borrowed from chemistry — explains why starting is the hardest part of any behavioral change. Catalysts lower the threshold; designing your environment to reduce the activation barrier for good behaviors is more reliable than relying on willpower.

- **[Inertia: objects (and minds) resist changes to their current state](../../wiki/strategy/inertia-the-force-that-holds-the-universe-together.md)**: Cognitive inertia keeps beliefs and habits anchored to existing states even when they no longer serve — manifesting as status quo bias and confirmation bias. Overcoming inertia requires only an initial push; once momentum begins, progress becomes self-sustaining.

- **[Second-order thinking is the new systems thinking — because AI commoditized the first](../../wiki/strategy/second-order-thinking-is-the-new-systems-thinking.md)**: As AI maps system architecture in seconds, the scarce skill shifts to reasoning through consequence chains beyond the obvious first outcome. The proposed method: before any significant decision, ask what happens directly, who changes behavior as a result, and what that behavioral change makes more or less likely downstream.

- **[Second-order thinking: "and then what?" is the most underused question in decisions](../../wiki/strategy/second-order-thinking-farnam-street.md)**: Howard Marks and Ray Dalio both identify failure to consider downstream consequences as a primary source of bad decisions. First-order thinking is fast and widely shared; second-order thinking is slower, less common, and creates durable edge.

- **[Entropy: disorder increases without deliberate energy expenditure](../../wiki/strategy/entropy-why-life-always-seems-to-get-more-complicated.md)**: James Clear connects Murphy's Law to the Second Law of Thermodynamics — disordered states vastly outnumber ordered ones, so everything decays by default. Optimal outcomes must be designed and maintained; mismatch and complexity are the statistical default, not the exception.

- **[Scarcity imposes a cognitive tax that worsens the decisions needed to escape it](../../wiki/strategy/scarcity-why-having-too-little-means-so-much.md)**: Mullainathan and Shafir's finding — whether scarcity is of time, money, or any resource, it captures mental bandwidth and creates tunneling that crowds out consideration of everything else. This creates a vicious cycle: the scarcer a resource feels, the worse your decisions about managing it become.

- **[Regression toward the mean: extreme outcomes trend back toward average](../../wiki/strategy/regression-toward-the-mean.md)**: Francis Galton's statistical insight — extreme performance contains a random-error component that reverts regardless of intervention, so improvements after a corrective action may reflect natural regression rather than the action's effect. The corrective is a control group or historical benchmark rather than a causal story.

- **[Evolution is blind — and that's the feature, not the bug](../../wiki/strategy/evolution-is-blind-but-we-are-not.md)**: Because conditions are never identical, no mistake is ever truly repeated — which means punishing repeated mistakes misunderstands failure. Evolution retries without memory of past failure because a trait that failed before may succeed in a changed environment; the human advantage is being able to reconstruct decision context deliberately.

- **[Speed is the moat: velocity compounds while static assets depreciate](../../wiki/strategy/speed-is-the-moat-why-faster-companies-win.md)**: In markets with fast replication, rate of learning and shipping — the Velocity Moat — outlasts brand, IP, or data accumulation because it compounds each cycle. Time to Value (not deploy frequency) is the metric that actually determines whether velocity becomes advantage; structural choices like small reversible batches and composable systems enable durable speed.

- **[Confirmation bias: we filter evidence through a pre-existing belief lens](../../wiki/strategy/confirmation-bias.md)**: The distinction between wanting to be right (truth-seeking) and wanting to have been right (ego protection) is the core obstacle to genuine learning. The antidote is actively seeking disconfirming evidence and auditing which parts of an argument you skimmed or uncritically accepted.
