## Summary
The IMF and the US Federal Reserve both reported that AI is already reshaping where employers hire, while a KPMG CEO survey pointed the other way with most still planning to add workers. Oyster, Selby Jennings, and ManpowerGroup add evidence that AI skill requirements are rising fast and are now tied to pay and scarcity. Together the six reports show slower hiring in exposed roles alongside a rising premium on demonstrated AI proficiency.

## Core Concepts
- **[[IMF]] Regional Economic Outlook (Asia and Pacific)**: about 40 million online job postings across 19 economies show demand falling in AI-exposed occupations.
- **[[Federal Reserve]] [[Beige Book]]**: anecdotal district reports tie slower, mostly entry-level hiring to [[AI Automation]].
- **[[KPMG]] CEO Outlook**: executives expect to add headcount while redesigning roles for human and AI collaboration.
- **[[Selby Jennings]] AI Talent Pulse**: financial firms linking raises, promotions, and bonuses to [[AI Proficiency]].
- **[[Oyster]] Global AI Talent Report**: explicit AI requirements in job postings tripled from 2022 to 2026.
- **[[ManpowerGroup]] Talent Shortage research**: AI skills now top the hardest-to-fill list worldwide.
- **[[Entry-Level Jobs]]**: the bottom rung is being reset as skill requirements change.

## Key Takeaways
- **IMF Demand Drop**: Demand fell nearly 10% in more AI-exposed occupations; the effect appears stabilized.
- **Beige Book**: Dallas, Cleveland, Minneapolis firms delayed entry-level hiring; overall employment still rose modestly.
- **CEO Optimism**: 89% of KPMG-surveyed CEOs expect higher headcount within three years.
- **Entry-Level Reset**: 75% of CEOs say AI changed entry-level skill requirements.
- **Pay Tied to AI**: A quarter of surveyed financial firms link pay and promotions to AI proficiency.
- **Requirement Tripling**: Postings requiring AI skills rose from about 5% to 15% since 2022.
- **Informal Reskilling**: 95% of HR leaders say staff do AI work not in job descriptions.
- **Train Over Hire**: Firms build AI capability via internal training (53%) more than hiring (14%).
- **Scarcity**: ManpowerGroup ranks AI skills above IT skills as hardest to find.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Slowdown, not destruction** — AI's labor effect often appears first as slower hiring in exposed roles rather than outright layoffs, so aggregate employment can keep growing while entry points shrink. · *model:* [[Leading vs. Lagging Indicators]]
→ explains: IMF Demand Drop, Beige Book, Entry-Level Reset

**T2. What you're asked vs. what you do** — Stated executive intentions and actual hiring behavior can diverge, because timing and respondent differ; triangulate surveys against posting data. · *model:* [[Base Rate Neglect]]
→ explains: CEO Optimism, IMF Demand Drop

**T3. Scarce skills get priced** — When a skill is hard to buy, employers train internally and tie pay to it. · *model:* [[Supply and Demand]]
→ explains: Pay Tied to AI, Train Over Hire, Scarcity

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central picture the six reports paint about AI and hiring?
**A**: AI is already damping hiring in exposed roles (IMF, Fed Beige Book) while executives still plan to add workers, and AI skills are increasingly required, rewarded, and scarce.

**Q2**: What did the IMF analysis find, and what data did it use?
**A**: Using about 40 million online postings across 19 Asian economies, it found employer demand fell nearly 10% in more AI-exposed occupations relative to less-exposed ones, an effect that seems to have stabilized.

**Q3**: What does the Oyster finding imply for how employers build AI capability?
**A**: With explicit AI requirements tripling and 53% of firms relying on internal training versus 14% on hiring, employers are growing capability they cannot reliably buy, so workers who can demonstrate AI skills concretely have leverage.
