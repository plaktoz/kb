## Summary
The US government's small AI safety division, CAISSI, has no permanent leader, has stopped publishing research, and was recently asked to informally report to a White House office. Critics fear politicized or muted findings, while the industry largely polices itself. Government capacity to independently test AI systems, especially agents, is not keeping pace with the technology.

## Core Concepts
- **[[CAISSI]]**: The Center for Advancing Innovation and Standards for Super Intelligence, a Commerce Department division formerly called the U.S. Center for AI Standards and Innovation, funded at $10 million per year.
- **[[AI Safety Evaluation]]**: Government testing of AI systems, compared in the article with [[NHTSA]] crash testing for cars.
- **[[AI Self-Regulation]]**: Voluntary company commitments under [[Joe Biden]] and [[Donald Trump]], including Trump's September call for "tremendous self-policing".
- **[[AI Agents]]**: Systems that plan multi-step actions in real-world settings without human prompting, with no standard process for testing real-world safety.
- **[[Super Intelligence Force]]**: Trump's new body, led by [[Jay Clayton]] with [[Emil Michael]], framing model safety as a national security matter.
- **[[Sayash Kapoor]]**: Co-author of a paper on agent evaluations who argues final-answer checks miss mistakes in intermediate steps.
- **[[OpenAI]] and [[Anthropic]]**: Labs promoting independent evaluation firms whose access they can limit.

## Key Takeaways
- **Leadership Vacuum**: The center has had no permanent director since a hire was fired within days in April.
- **Reporting Line**: Staff were asked to informally report to a White House office, per two former officials.
- **Budget Gap**: $10 million per year, about one-eighth the budget of the UK counterpart.
- **Silent Research**: The center stopped publishing reports on AI system safety; Congress asked why in August.
- **Self-Policing Model**: Trump asked companies for voluntary self-policing without saying how it will be monitored.
- **Agent Testing Gap**: No standard process exists for testing how safely AI agents act in the real world.
- **Process Over Outcome**: Checking only the final result can hide a huge mistake in an agent's steps.
- **Unclear Government Role**: Labs building internal and hired evaluators leaves government testing's purpose ambiguous.
- **National Security Framing**: Safety framed as national security may overshadow consumer protection, privacy, and civil rights.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Evaluators lose credibility when dependent on those they evaluate** — An independent check only works if its findings cannot be altered, muted, or withheld by the party with a stake in the outcome. Placing evaluators under political oversight or letting labs control access and drop evaluations weakens that independence. · *model:* [[Regulatory Capture]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central concern about the US center for AI safety?
**A**: Its independence is in doubt: it lacks a permanent leader, has stopped publishing research, and was asked to informally report to a White House office. Critics worry its findings could be politicized or muted in deference to the tech industry.

**Q2**: What evidence shows government capacity lags behind AI development?
**A**: The center gets $10 million per year, about one-eighth the UK counterpart's budget, and there is no standard process for testing AI agents' real-world safety. Kapoor notes older methods check only the final answer, missing mistakes in an agent's steps.

**Q3**: What does the article imply about relying on labs to evaluate themselves?
**A**: Labs can limit evaluators' access and dispense with their evaluations, which weakens independent checks on systems that can cause real-world harm. It also leaves the role of government testing unclear.
