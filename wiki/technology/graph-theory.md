## Summary

Graph theory is the mathematical study of structures that model pairwise relations between objects using [[Vertices]] (nodes) connected by [[Edges]] (arcs). Founded by [[Leonhard Euler]] in 1736 through his analysis of the [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg]], the field spans subareas including topological, algebraic, geometric, extremal, and probabilistic graph theory. Its structures are foundational to modern computer science, biology, chemistry, and social network analysis.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Graph]] (data structure)**: A set of vertices and edges; may be undirected, directed, simple, multigraph, or weighted.
- **[[Vertices]] and [[Edges]]**: The atomic building blocks; vertices represent objects, edges represent relations between them.
- **[[Directed Graph]] (Digraph)**: A graph where edges have orientation, indicating asymmetric relationships.
- **[[Weighted Graph]]**: A graph where edges carry numerical values (e.g., distances, costs).
- **[[Topological Graph Theory]]**: Studies graph embeddings, crossing numbers, and map coloring problems.
- **[[Algebraic Graph Theory]]**: Uses spectral methods and [[Group Theory]] to analyze graph symmetry.
- **[[Geometric Graph Theory]]**: Covers planar graphs, intersection graphs, and visibility graphs.
- **[[Extremal Graph Theory]]**: Examines maximum edge counts and Turán-type problems.
- **[[Random Graphs]]**: Applies probabilistic methods; key model is the [[Erdős–Rényi Model]].
- **[[Four-Color Theorem]]**: First major theorem proved with computer assistance (Appel and Haken, 1976).
- **[[Kirchhoff's Laws]]**: Early application of graph-like reasoning to electrical circuits (1845).

## Key Takeaways

- **Founding Work**: [[Leonhard Euler]]'s 1736 paper on [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg]] originated graph theory.
- **Terminology Origin**: The term "graph" was coined by [[James Joseph Sylvester]] in 1878.
- **Milestone Textbook**: [[Dénes Kőnig]] wrote the first graph theory textbook in 1936.
- **Computer-Aided Proof**: [[Four-Color Theorem]] proved via computer in 1976 by Appel and Haken.
- **Universal Applicability**: Graphs model neural networks, molecules, social networks, and biological systems.
- **Graph Types**: Undirected, directed, simple, multigraph, and weighted graphs cover most relational structures.
- **Subareas**: Topological, algebraic, geometric, extremal, and random graph theory each address distinct problem classes.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Abstraction Ladder]]**: Graph theory succeeds by stripping real systems (roads, molecules, networks) to their essential relational skeleton — a pure representation of "what connects to what" — enabling universal algorithms to apply across entirely different domains.
- **[[Reductionism]]**: Complex systems like the internet or a metabolic pathway are fully representable as vertices and edges, demonstrating that pairwise relations are a sufficient primitive for modeling many real-world phenomena.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of graph theory as a mathematical field?
**A**: Graph theory studies structures (graphs) consisting of vertices connected by edges to model pairwise relations between objects, with applications spanning nearly every quantitative discipline.

**Q2**: What historical event is considered the founding moment of graph theory, and who is responsible?
**A**: [[Leonhard Euler]]'s 1736 paper analyzing the [[Seven Bridges of Königsberg]] — asking whether one could walk through the city crossing each bridge exactly once — is the founding work of graph theory.

**Q3**: How does graph theory apply to modern computing and science?
**A**: Graphs model neural networks, molecular structures, social networks, and circuit layouts; the same algorithmic framework applies across computer science, chemistry, biology, linguistics, and physics.
