# The 5 Forces Creating Urgency Around Ethical AI Adoption

## Summary

In an excerpt from his book "Boundaries of Tolerance", MIT Sloan senior lecturer Jeffrey Saviano argues that AI governance is a leadership question that belongs in the boardroom, not just with technologists or regulators. He identifies five forces pushing companies toward an ethical AI strategy: competitive pressure, changed risk behavior, investor expectations, internal employee pressure, and rising moral expectations. Leaders must define what their organizations will tolerate from AI systems and cannot defer that to regulators.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Jeffrey Saviano]]**: MIT Sloan senior lecturer and former EY leader; author of "[[Boundaries of Tolerance]]".
- **[[Ethical AI]]**: Saviano's preferred term over "[[Responsible AI]]", because it stresses leadership judgment in how systems are designed and deployed.
- **[[AI Governance]]**: Treated as a boardroom and C-suite responsibility rather than a technical or regulatory one.
- **[[Enterprise Risk Management]]**: Traditional frameworks are described as no longer sufficient for AI.
- **[[Competitive Pressure]]**: Fear of falling behind pushes AI into production before governance is clarified.
- **[[Investor Scrutiny]]**: Questions about AI oversight resemble earlier scrutiny of [[Cybersecurity]] and internal controls.
- **[[Boundaries of Tolerance]]**: The idea that leaders must define what their organizations will tolerate from intelligent systems.

## Key Takeaways

- **Competitive pressure**: Fear of falling behind outweighs concern about systems not fully understood.
- **Risk behaves differently**: Minor AI issues can escalate rapidly once deployed enterprise-wide.
- **Investor scrutiny**: Investors ask who oversees AI risk and how failures are addressed.
- **Internal pressure**: Employees raise ethical questions in real time; unclear authority weakens control.
- **Rising moral expectations**: Compliance alone no longer answers whether AI aligns with organizational values.
- **Non-delegable duty**: Leaders cannot defer tolerance decisions to regulators; judgment outweighs technical expertise.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Competitive pressure makes deployment outrun governance** — When rivals move fast, the fear of falling behind outweighs caution, so systems reach production before oversight is defined and early choices lock in long-term exposure. · *model:* [[Arms Race Dynamics]]
→ explains: Competitive pressure

**T2. Fast-propagating failures break proportionality-based oversight** — Controls built on the assumption that small problems stay small fail when a system can escalate a minor issue organization-wide within hours.
→ explains: Risk behaves differently

**T3. Undefined decision authority degrades control** — When leadership sets no boundaries, people either stop raising concerns or decide alone, and both outcomes weaken organizational control.
→ explains: Internal pressure, Non-delegable duty

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Saviano's central claim about AI governance?
**A**: It is a leadership question that reaches the boardroom and C-suite, because leaders must define what their organizations will tolerate from AI and cannot delegate that to regulators.

**Q2**: Why do traditional risk frameworks fall short for AI?
**A**: With AI, minor issues can escalate rapidly once deployed, a biased outcome can trigger public backlash within hours, and a model that tests well can behave differently in real use, undermining assumptions about materiality and proportionality.

**Q3**: What happens inside an organization when leadership hasn't defined AI boundaries?
**A**: Employees already using AI tools either stop raising ethical concerns or make decisions on their own, and both outcomes weaken organizational control.
