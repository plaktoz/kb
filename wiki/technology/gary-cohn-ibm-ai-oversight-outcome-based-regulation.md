## Summary

IBM Vice Chair [[Gary Cohn]] argues the U.S. should regulate AI outcomes rather than the technology itself, framing oversight as a way to "inspire creativity" rather than constrain it. His central concern is regulatory fragmentation: 50 independent state AI laws would harm U.S. competitiveness against China. He advocates routing AI accountability through existing sector-specific regulators — financial AI under financial regulators, medical AI under the [[FDA]] — rather than creating a new dedicated AI oversight body.

## Core Concepts

- **Outcome-Based Regulation**: Cohn's thesis — govern what AI *does*, not how it works. "You're not going to regulate AI, you're going to inspire creativity. You're going to regulate the outcomes."
- **Regulatory Fragmentation Risk**: 50 state-level AI frameworks would create compliance chaos and disadvantage U.S. innovation relative to [[China]], which can move with unified national direction.
- **Sector-Specific Oversight**: Existing regulators (financial, medical, etc.) should absorb AI accountability within their domains rather than a new AI-specific agency being stood up.
- **U.S.-China AI Competition**: Cohn frames the regulatory design question as geopolitically consequential — fragmented rules weaken the U.S. position in the AI race.
- **No New Dedicated Body**: Cohn explicitly declines to endorse a standalone AI regulator, instead preferring domain-routing for accountability.

## Key Takeaways

- **Core stance**: Regulate AI outcomes, not the underlying technology itself.
- **Fragmentation concern**: 50-state patchwork would damage U.S. competitive standing vs. China.
- **Domain routing**: Financial AI → financial regulators; medical AI → FDA; no new agency needed.
- **Innovation framing**: Oversight should "inspire creativity," not stifle startups and incumbents alike.
- **Implicit premise**: Technology-layer regulation is too blunt — outcome-layer regulation preserves flexibility for builders.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Regulatory Capture vs. Domain Expertise]]**: Routing AI accountability through existing sector regulators assumes domain knowledge produces better oversight than a generalist AI body — the first-principles argument is that regulators who already understand a sector's risk profile will set more proportionate rules than a new agency learning both AI and the domain simultaneously.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: Regulating the technology itself risks optimizing for proxy metrics (model architecture, parameter counts) rather than actual harm reduction — Cohn's outcome-based framing directly counters this by anchoring rules to the results that matter.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Gary Cohn's central argument about how AI should be regulated?
**A**: Cohn argues the U.S. should regulate AI outcomes and end products rather than the technology itself, with the goal of inspiring creativity while holding deployers accountable for results.

**Q2**: Why does Cohn oppose 50 state-level AI regulations?
**A**: Fragmented state rules would create compliance complexity that disadvantages U.S. companies, particularly in the context of direct competition with China, which can coordinate AI policy nationally.

**Q3**: How should AI accountability be structured according to Cohn, and what does this imply for existing regulators?
**A**: Existing sector-specific regulators should absorb AI oversight within their domains — financial regulators for financial AI, the FDA for medical AI — making a new dedicated AI agency unnecessary.
