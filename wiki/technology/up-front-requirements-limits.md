## Summary

James Shore recounts a project where stakeholders signed off on detailed requirements — including prototypes and screenshots — yet the team still delivered the wrong product. His diagnosis is that most customers cannot mentally simulate software from written descriptions, so sign-offs are illusory. The reliable fix is delivering real, working, non-prototype software early with an explicit expectation of iteration and change.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Up-Front Requirements]]** — the practice of thoroughly documenting what software should do before building it; Shore argues this process has inherent limits because stakeholders cannot genuinely evaluate written specifications.
- **[[Cognitive Simulation Gap]]** — the inability of non-technical stakeholders to mentally run software from documentation; they appear engaged ("smile and nod") without truly understanding what they are approving.
- **[[Iterative Delivery]]** — the practice of shipping real, working software incrementally so stakeholders can react to actual behavior rather than descriptions; Shore's preferred alternative to upfront requirements.
- **[[Waterfall]]** — traditional sequential development where requirements are frozen before implementation; the implicit target of Shore's critique.
- **[[James Shore]]** — agile practitioner and author; early proponent of [[Extreme Programming]] and iterative development practices.

## Key Takeaways

- **Stakeholder sign-offs are unreliable**: customers approve specs they cannot truly evaluate.
- **Mental simulation gap**: non-technical stakeholders cannot model software from text descriptions.
- **Documentation is insufficient**: even prototypes and screenshots did not prevent misalignment.
- **Critical features deprioritized**: access control and reporting were cut due to poor stakeholder judgment.
- **Platform mismatch example**: one stakeholder expected a web app but received a Windows app.
- **Solution — ship early**: deliver real, working software fast with iteration baked in as the norm.
- **Iteration over documentation**: change expectations upfront; the process must accommodate discovery.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Illusion of Explanatory Depth]]**: Stakeholders believe they understand the software from written specs, but their comprehension collapses when they encounter the real system — exactly the dynamic Shore documents where sign-offs masked a total misalignment.
- **[[Feedback Loops]]**: The fundamental problem with upfront requirements is a long feedback loop; delivering working software early compresses the loop so misunderstandings surface cheaply and early rather than expensively at delivery.
