## Summary

Kieran Klaassen updates his year-old [[Compound Engineering]] methodology, expanding the original four-step loop ("brainstorm → work → review → compound → repeat") into eight steps as AI models made the "work" phase less dependent on constant human attention. Collaborator [[Trevin Chow]]'s "sandwich" analogy frames the change: AI increasingly owns the middle of the process, while humans provide the "bread" — judgment at the start and finishing polish at the end. The new loop is "ideate → brainstorm → plan → work → review → polish → compound → repeat," with "compound" still central since each shipped feature should make future work easier.

## Key Takeaways

- **Original loop**: compound engineering began as "brainstorm → work → review → compound → repeat."
- **Work phase shrinks**: better-context agents now write and fix code correctly with less oversight.
- **Sandwich analogy**: AI owns the middle; humans provide judgment "bread" on both ends.
- **New loop**: expanded to "ideate → brainstorm → plan → work → review → polish → compound → repeat."
- **Front end matters**: ideation and brainstorming decide what's worth building and for whom.
- **Back end matters**: polish checks design, copy, and feel even when code "technically works."
- **Compound stays central**: each feature should make subsequent work easier, not just ship.
- **Guide updated**: Klaassen revised his compound engineering guide to reflect the fuller system.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Comparative Advantage]]**: As AI absorbs more of the execution-heavy "work" phase, humans concentrate at the judgment-heavy bookends (ideation and polish) — each actor doing what it does relatively best.
- **[[Compounding Returns]]**: The "compound" step encodes the idea that each feature should reduce the cost of the next one, turning individual work sessions into an accumulating asset rather than one-off output.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of Klaassen's updated compound engineering framework?
**A**: As AI models improve, the "work" phase needs less human attention, so the human role concentrates at the start (ideation/planning) and end (polish) of the loop rather than throughout.

**Q2**: What is the "sandwich" analogy and who proposed it?
**A**: Trevin Chow's analogy describes AI as handling the "middle" of development work while humans provide the "bread" — judgment and framing at the start, polish and quality checks at the end.

**Q3**: How does the loop's structure change what a practitioner should focus on day to day?
**A**: Instead of monitoring the "work" step closely, practitioners should invest more time upfront (ideate, brainstorm, plan) and at the finish (polish), since "compound" — making future work easier — remains the throughline.
