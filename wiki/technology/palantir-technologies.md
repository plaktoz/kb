## Summary

Palantir Technologies is an American data integration and analytics software company founded in 2003 by Peter Thiel, Alex Karp, and others, primarily serving U.S. government intelligence agencies and commercial clients. It achieved GAAP profitability for the first time in Q1 2023 after nearly two decades of losses, and by 2025 had a market cap exceeding $400B with $4.48B in annual revenue. The company has expanded from intelligence-focused tools like Gotham into commercial AI platforms including AIP, which integrates large language models into private enterprise networks.

## Key Takeaways

- **Founded 2003**: Bankrolled by Peter Thiel; CIA's In-Q-Tel invested ~$2M early on.
- **Revenue 2025**: $4.48B with $1.63B net income on ~4,400 employees.
- **Went public 2020**: Via direct listing on NYSE; moved to Nasdaq after S&P 500 inclusion in 2024.
- **GAAP profitability**: Achieved Q1 2023 ($31M net income) after ~20 years of losses.
- **Market cap 2025**: Exceeded $400B; The Economist flagged it as possibly "most over-valued firm of all time."
- **Ukraine deployment**: Gotham processes drone video and satellite imagery to shorten battlefield kill chains.
- **AIP launch 2023**: LLM integration into private networks positioned Palantir for enterprise AI wave.
- **Government contracts**: ~$908M in contracts under second Trump administration as of May 2025.
- **ICE controversy**: ELITE tool uses HHS/Medicaid data for deportation targeting; partnership since 2014.
- **NHS contract**: £330M Federated Data Platform drew protests over privacy and IDF ties.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Dual-Use Technology]]**: Palantir's tools serve both intelligence/military and commercial ends — the same data fusion capability that tracks terrorists can track hospital patients, creating both enormous value and ethical tension that cannot be resolved by product design alone.
- **[[Network Effects in Data]]**: Each new government agency or corporate customer adds data linkages that make Palantir's platforms harder to replace, explaining why long unprofitability was strategically acceptable — switching costs compound over time.
