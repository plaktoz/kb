## Summary

[[Daron Acemoglu]] (MIT, 2024 Nobel Prize in Economics) argues that 40 years of digital automation increased productivity while deepening inequality, and that generative AI risks supercharging those same inequities unless deliberately redirected. He proposes "pro-worker AI" — models that expand workers' capabilities and create new tasks rather than replace labor entirely. Realizing this vision requires policy reform, tax code changes, and a cultural shift away from automation-as-default.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Pro-Worker AI]]**: AI designed to augment workers — expanding their capabilities, giving them new tasks, and raising wages — rather than replacing them with software or machines.
- **[[Automation Bias in AI Investment]]**: [[Silicon Valley]] funding overwhelmingly targets labor-replacing automation because it reduces payroll and dependence on organized labor, making "reaching human parity" the dominant success metric.
- **[[Human-Computer Symbiosis]]**: J.C.R. [[Licklider]]'s 1960 vision of computers augmenting human cognition is now technically achievable with [[Generative AI]], forming the technological foundation for pro-worker models.
- **[[Labor-Capital Tax Asymmetry]]**: U.S. tax code imposes up to a 30% burden on $100 of wages versus under 5% on $100 of automation equipment — a structural incentive that makes automating profitable even when it is less productive than human labor.
- **[[Artificial General Intelligence]] Race**: The global AGI race prioritizes total labor replacement across sectors, threatening skilled blue-collar and craft work alongside knowledge work.
- **[[Sectoral AI Applications]]**: Pro-worker AI applied in healthcare, education, and skilled trades (electricians, technicians) could uplift lower-skilled workers without displacing them.
- **[[Technology Monopolies]]**: Concentrated tech market power crowds out alternative AI development paths; antitrust enforcement could open space for pro-worker AI ventures.

## Key Takeaways

- **Automation Default**: Silicon Valley incentives push AI toward replacement, not augmentation.
- **Tax Asymmetry**: Labor is taxed ~30% vs. <5% for automation equipment, distorting investment.
- **Licklider Fulfilled**: Generative AI finally realizes the 1960 vision of machine-augmented cognition.
- **Sectoral Uplift**: Electricians, teachers, and health aides could gain capability — not lose jobs.
- **Policy Levers**: Public AI grants, competitions, and antitrust enforcement needed to enable pro-worker AI.
- **Worker Training**: Adaptive training must build on existing expertise, not retrain across industries.
- **Union Strategy**: Unions should advocate for pro-worker AI design, not just resist automation.
- **Stakes**: The link between productivity growth and good jobs — broken for 40 years — could be restored.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: "Reaching human parity" became the dominant AI benchmark, so developers optimize for it — making replacement the north star and crowding out pro-worker alternatives that don't fit the metric.
- **[[Incentive Asymmetry]]**: The tax code structurally favors capital over labor, meaning even a less-productive automation investment can outperform keeping humans — corporations follow incentives, not social outcomes.
- **[[Comparative Advantage]]**: Genuine productivity gains come from pairing human judgment, physical dexterity, and social intelligence with AI information processing — not from eliminating the human entirely.
