## Summary

Former NEA partner [[Vanessa Larco]] explains why she left a multi-billion-dollar fund to co-found [[Premise VC]], a specialized pre-seed/seed fund, after the [[Silicon Valley Bank]] collapse permanently shifted founder preference toward investors whose small check sizes make founders a top priority. She evaluates founders against seven attributes rather than initial ideas, and judges AI "wrapper" startups by whether they meaningfully change a cost structure — not by whether they own a proprietary model.

## Core Concepts

- [[Vanessa Larco]] — co-founder of Premise VC; former NEA partner and board observer at Robinhood
- [[Premise VC]] — pre-seed/seed fund with $500K–$3M checks, founded 2025
- [[Silicon Valley Bank]] collapse — catalyzed founder preference for specialized, smaller funds over mega-funds
- [[AI Wrapper]] — a startup built atop existing foundation models; Larco compares the "no moat" critique to early skepticism of building on [[AWS]]
- [[Do It For Me]] AI — the shift from AI-as-advisor to AI-as-executor, and the trust problem this creates

## Key Takeaways

- **Founder priority signal**: founders now judge investors by whether "the check size hurt" — a proxy for being a top priority in a crisis
- **Seven founder attributes**: Premise looks for world-class strength in at least two, not a single archetype
- **Surprising trait**: "urgently dissatisfied" founders — disagreeable, relentless, push collaborators past what they thought possible
- **Wrapper defensibility test**: faster, cheaper, or easier than the legacy alternative — not model ownership — is what matters
- **Moat is unchanged**: workflow stickiness, data accumulation, and integrations remain the real moat even as founders swap underlying models
- **Consumer AI contrarian bet**: Larco calls the VC retreat from consumer tech "short-sighted" given AI-driven behavior change

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Signaling Theory]]**: A founder demanding a check size that "hurts" the investor is using financial commitment as a costly, hard-to-fake signal of investor priority — the same logic behind why expensive guarantees are more credible than cheap promises.
