## Summary

[[Dario Amodei]] published an essay calling for deliberate AI development slowdowns, arguing that advances are outpacing researchers' ability to control them — a position endorsed by [[Sam Altman]] and [[Elon Musk]]. The Every.to team's internal discussion was measured rather than panicked, with perspectives ranging from historical skepticism about AI danger cycles to the view that a decade without catastrophe is insufficient reassurance when odds are ~10%. Separately, [[OpenAI]]'s proposed solution to the [[Navier-Stokes]] Millennium Prize has led skeptics to reconsider AGI timelines, with contributors cautioning that belief in near-term AGI doesn't improve one's ability to anticipate it.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Dario Amodei]]'s slowdown call**: Anthropic's CEO argued AI advances are outpacing alignment researchers' control — unusually, [[Sam Altman]] and [[Elon Musk]] both endorsed slowing down.
- **[[Jacob Coxon]] resignation**: Former Anthropic engineer who resigned warning AI could threaten humanity; warning seconded by [[Evan Hubinger]], Anthropic's alignment science lead, who placed odds above 10%.
- **[[Responsible Scaling Policy]]**: Anthropic's internal framework for calibrated AI development — Kieran Klaassen noted that public resignations are a feature of this system, not a sign of malfunction.
- **[[Navier-Stokes]] Millennium Problem**: [[OpenAI]] proposed a solution exposing conditions where equations predict physically impossible infinite fluid speeds — a fundamental limit in fluid-motion modeling and a landmark AI capability demonstration.
- **[[AGI Timelines]]**: The Navier-Stokes breakthrough has prompted even skeptics to revise their AGI estimates upward, though contributor Ashwin Sharma warns that shortened timelines don't translate into better anticipatory capacity.

## Key Takeaways

- Altman and Musk both endorsed Amodei's AI slowdown call — a rare cross-lab consensus.
- Evan Hubinger (Anthropic alignment lead) puts extinction-level AI odds above 10%.
- Brian Dell: a decade without catastrophe isn't reassuring if base odds are ~10%.
- Mike Taylor: labs recruit "worriers" by design — historical AI danger cycles are over-hyped.
- Kieran Klaassen: Anthropic's policy expects public resignations as a healthy signal.
- OpenAI's Navier-Stokes proof reveals physically impossible infinite-speed predictions in existing models.
- Ashwin Sharma: believing AGI is near doesn't make you better at anticipating its arrival.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Brian Dell's rebuttal captures it precisely — ten years without catastrophe is weak evidence against a ~10% per-decade risk; survival bias from a short track record should not update priors much against a low-frequency, high-consequence event.
- **[[Chesterton's Fence]]**: Klaassen's reading of Anthropic's responsible scaling policy applies this model: public resignations look alarming only if you don't understand the structure they were designed to trigger — the system is functioning as intended, not breaking down.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim behind Dario Amodei's slowdown essay, and who endorsed it?
**A**: Amodei argued AI advances are outpacing researchers' ability to control them and called for deliberate slowdowns; both Sam Altman and Elon Musk publicly endorsed the idea.

**Q2**: What did OpenAI's Navier-Stokes proof reveal, and why does it matter for AGI timelines?
**A**: The proof exposes conditions where the equations predict physically impossible infinite fluid speeds, revealing a fundamental limit in fluid-motion modeling; the result has led skeptics to reconsider shortened AGI timelines, though contributor Ashwin Sharma notes that belief alone doesn't improve anticipatory capacity.

**Q3**: How does Brian Dell's perspective challenge the "nothing bad has happened yet" framing?
**A**: Dell argues that a decade without catastrophe provides little reassurance when the stated odds are around 10% — the danger may already be unfolding undetected, and a short incident-free window shouldn't significantly revise a non-trivial base-rate estimate downward.
