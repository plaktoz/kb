## Summary

Crusoe, the Denver-based AI data center startup that recently raised $3.9 billion, has terminated its $1.25 billion agreement to purchase 29 natural gas turbine units from [[Boom Supersonic]]'s "Superpower" stationary power business. Crusoe cited a desire for flexibility in choosing energy solutions site-by-site, while Boom CEO Blake Scholl confirmed the split publicly and said Boom still has other customers targeting ~250MW of deliveries in 2027 and 1GW by 2028. The deal's collapse is a significant setback for Boom, which raised $300 million partly to fund the turbine business, intending those revenues to cross-subsidize development of its Overture supersonic jet.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Crusoe Energy]]** — AI data center startup; recently raised $3.9B; operates flexible energy strategy across grid, renewables, batteries, and on-site turbines
- **[[Boom Supersonic]]** — primarily known for the Overture supersonic passenger jet; launched "Superpower" stationary turbine side-business sharing ~80% components with its Symphony engine
- **[[Superpower Turbines]]** — Boom's 42-megawatt natural gas-fired stationary turbines designed for large-scale on-site power generation at industrial sites
- **[[AI Data Center Energy]]** — growing need for dedicated, reliable power at hyperscale AI compute facilities; Crusoe's Abilene campus (serving [[OpenAI]]) uses grid power; a 900MW [[Microsoft]] facility nearby will use on-site gas turbines
- **[[Vertical Power Generation]]** — trend of AI data center operators securing dedicated on-site power rather than relying solely on the grid

## Key Takeaways

- **Deal size**: $1.25B for 29 x 42-megawatt Superpower turbines; first deliveries planned 2027.
- **Crusoe's stated reason**: Flexibility to pick the right energy solution per site (grid, renewables, batteries, turbines).
- **Boom's exposure**: Raised $300M specifically to fund turbine business; revenues were meant to fund Overture jet development.
- **Boom's pipeline**: Still targeting ~250MW deliveries in 2027 and 1GW by 2028 with other customers.
- **Crusoe's Abilene campus**: Serves OpenAI; runs on grid power — no on-site turbines needed there.
- **Microsoft contrast**: Separate 900MW facility in Abilene will use on-site gas turbines.
- **Component overlap**: Boom's Superpower turbine shares ~80% of components with its airborne Symphony engine.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Single Point of Dependency Risk]]**: Boom's business model concentrated turbine revenue on one anchor customer (Crusoe), making its Overture jet roadmap financially contingent on a single deal — a classic fragility of relying on one large buyer to fund an unrelated core product.
- **[[Optionality]]**: Crusoe's "flexible, site-by-site" energy strategy preserves optionality rather than committing to one technology — rational when grid infrastructure, renewable availability, and turbine costs vary dramatically across data center locations.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why did Crusoe abandon the $1.25B Boom turbine deal?
**A**: Crusoe said it wants to stay flexible, choosing the energy solution that best fits each site — including turbines, renewables, batteries, and grid power — rather than committing to a single technology.

**Q2**: How does the collapse of the Crusoe deal affect Boom Supersonic's broader business?
**A**: Boom raised $300 million largely to fund the Superpower turbine business, intending those profits to support development of its Overture supersonic jet; losing its largest customer is a significant financial setback, though Boom says it has other customers targeting 250MW in 2027 and 1GW by 2028.

**Q3**: What does this deal collapse illustrate about energy strategy for AI data center operators?
**A**: It shows that large operators like Crusoe prefer flexible, site-specific energy sourcing over long-term single-technology commitments, as power needs, grid access, and cost economics differ significantly across locations.
