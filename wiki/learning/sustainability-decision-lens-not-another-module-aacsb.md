## Summary

Jambulingam proposes a five-dimensional Sustainability Decision Lens (environment, economy, society, resilience, strategy) that adds questions to existing business assignments instead of new modules. The aim is "sustainable managerial judgment": weighing competing objectives, anticipating consequences, and defending decisions under uncertainty. Faculty can start with one assignment and keep its primary disciplinary objective.

## Core Concepts

- [[Sustainability Decision Lens]]: five questions added to a business decision on environment, economy, society, resilience, and strategy.
- [[Sustainable Managerial Judgment]]: recognizing competing objectives, evaluating trade-offs, and making defensible choices under uncertainty.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: students state what a recommendation gives up.
- [[Second-Order Thinking]]: asking what consequences a decision creates beyond its primary objective.
- [[Redundancy]]: protection against disruption that requires added capital and resources.
- [[Experiential Learning]]: assignments that add ambiguity, feedback, and accountability for decisions.
- [[Erik Foley]]: author of "Sustainability Across the Curriculum" (AACSB Insights, March 2022), whose approach this article builds on.

## Key Takeaways

- **Change Questions**: Widen an existing assignment's questions instead of adding sustainability modules.
- **Five Dimensions**: Environment, economy, society, resilience, and strategy frame each business decision.
- **Trade-offs Over Scores**: The lens reveals trade-offs; it does not produce a single "sustainable" answer.
- **Who Pays**: Students identify who benefits, who pays, and who assumes risk.
- **Fragile Assumptions**: Decisions judged only under expected conditions hide vulnerability when expectations fail.
- **Defend the Cost**: Students must explain what they give up by choosing a recommendation.
- **Start Small**: Begin with one existing assignment and keep its disciplinary objective.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Trade-offs must be stated** — A recommendation is defensible only when the objectives it sacrifices are named. · *model:* [[Opportunity Cost]]
→ explains: Trade-offs Over Scores, Defend the Cost

**T2. Consequences reach past the primary objective** — A decision optimized for one criterion still produces environmental, social, and strategic effects. · *model:* [[Second-Order Thinking]]
→ explains: Change Questions, Five Dimensions, Who Pays

**T3. Expected conditions are not guaranteed** — A decision that works under expected conditions can fail when conditions change, so its assumptions must be tested.
→ explains: Fragile Assumptions

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central argument about teaching sustainability in business school?
**A**: Rather than adding another sustainability module, faculty can change the questions students answer in existing assignments, widening the analysis without replacing disciplinary objectives.

**Q2**: What do the five dimensions of the lens ask students to examine?
**A**: They ask about environmental consequences, long-term economic value, who benefits and who bears risk, how the decision holds up when conditions change, and whether it strengthens long-term competitive advantage.

**Q3**: How could a faculty member apply the lens right away?
**A**: Take one assignment that already works, keep its primary objective, and ask three questions: what consequence is missing, which assumption is most vulnerable, and who benefits and who bears extra cost or risk.
