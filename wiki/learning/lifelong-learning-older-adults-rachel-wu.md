## Summary

Psychologist [[Rachel Wu]] studies how lessons from child development can help older adults keep learning, finding that when placed in supportive, child-like learning environments, older adults can significantly improve cognitive abilities while mastering practical skills like new technology. Her own practice of deliberately learning a new skill every few years — including ice skating alongside her daughter — illustrates her core finding: the biggest obstacle to adult learning isn't age, but the belief that it's too late to learn.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Supportive Learning Environments]]** — child-like conditions (patient coaching, tolerance for mistakes) that help older adults improve cognitive health
- **[[Deliberate Novice Practice]]** — Wu's personal habit of repeatedly becoming a beginner at new skills throughout adulthood
- **[[Attribution to Talent vs. Effort]]** — the tendency to credit visible skill improvement to innate talent rather than practice, which Wu experienced firsthand
- **[[Cognitive Aging]]** — the relationship between continued skill acquisition and mental health, resilience, and independence in older adults

## Key Takeaways

- **Older adults in supportive environments show real cognitive gains**, not just hobby enjoyment.
- **Adults expect fast fluency**: unlike infants given a year to say one word, adults expect language fluency in weeks.
- **Fast learners make the most mistakes**: mistake tolerance correlates with faster skill acquisition (e.g., ice skating falls).
- **Continued learning correlates with better mental health**: less loneliness and depression among older adults who keep learning.
- **The core barrier is belief, not age**: many older learners discover they were more capable than they assumed.
