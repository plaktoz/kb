## Summary

A new audit by Patrick O'Neill finds that 14 meta-analyses reporting positive effects of AI on learning fail basic standards, pooling mismatched studies and outcome measures. These papers have still been cited over 3,000 times, driven by demand for evidence and incentives to publish fast. Panelists argue the field needs well-defined research questions and high-quality small studies before any broad meta-analysis.

## Core Concepts

- [[Meta-Analysis]]: pooling results from many studies into an average effect size.
- [[Construct Validity]]: having a clear sense of what is actually being studied; the audit's central failure.
- [[Publication Bias]]: journals favoring positive findings over null or negative ones.
- [[Peer Review]]: quality-control mechanism that missed serious errors across 11 reputable journals.
- [[Patrick O'Neill]]: Ivy Tech Community College professor and author of the audit.
- [[Justin Reich]], [[Elizabeth Tipton]], [[Susanna Loeb]]: webinar panelists on research incentives, construct validity, and the evidence base.
- [[Stanford AI Hub for Learning]]: reviewed 800+ studies; only 20 had a strong cause-and-effect design, none with K-12 students.
- [[AI in Education]]: the broad, poorly defined topic being studied.

## Key Takeaways

- **Audit Verdict**: All 14 audited AI-learning meta-analyses failed basic standards for this research type.
- **Influence**: The flawed papers were cited over 3,000 times; one Nature-journal paper was retracted.
- **Mixed Inputs**: Studies from elementary PE and medical simulations were pooled; some used no AI.
- **Outcome Bucketing**: Knowledge, motivation, engagement, mental health, and perceived learning were lumped together.
- **Bad Source Studies**: Some included studies had "impossible" statistics or likely hallucinated references.
- **Incentives**: Demand for evidence and pressure to publish quickly fuel overstated results.
- **Thin Evidence Base**: Stanford found only 20 of 800+ studies had strong causal designs.
- **Path Forward**: Define discrete research questions and gather evidence before broad meta-analyses.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the O'Neill audit conclude about AI-in-education meta-analyses?
**A**: The 14 audited meta-analyses fail basic standards for the method and are too unreliable to guide decisions, despite showing uniformly positive effects and being cited over 3,000 times.

**Q2**: What specific flaws did the audit find in how the meta-analyses were built?
**A**: They pooled different populations and loosely related outcomes (knowledge alongside motivation and mental health), never defined the AI use they studied, ignored publication bias, and included studies with impossible statistics or hallucinated references.

**Q3**: How should the field proceed before drawing broad conclusions about AI in education?
**A**: Researchers should agree on discrete, well-defined research questions and generate high-quality evidence on each before attempting broad meta-analyses, with honesty about what studies can and cannot show.
