# Scott Young: Recent Reading (Eight Books)

## Summary

Scott H. Young shares brief reflections on eight books spanning social psychology, mathematics history, moral philosophy, genetics, existentialism, precision engineering, economics, and satire. Across the reflections, a recurring thread is how ideas from one domain — envy, marginal cost, moral tragedy, migration — reshape assumptions people take for granted. Young treats each book as a distinct intellectual encounter rather than trying to force a unifying thesis.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Envy]]** as a socially corrosive force, per [[Helmut Schoeck]]'s *Envy: A Theory of Social Behaviour* — envy targets nearby peers more than distant elites, a pattern Young connects to both the [[Cultural Revolution]] and modern [[Right-Wing Populism]].
- **History of [[Calculus]]**, covered in [[Steven Strogatz]]'s *Infinite Powers*.
- **[[Moral Dilemmas]]** and [[Greek Tragedy]] as explored by [[Martha Nussbaum]] in *The Fragility of Goodness*, challenging the project (from [[Plato]] through [[Jeremy Bentham]]) of designing frameworks that eliminate genuine ethical conflict.
- **Ancient [[DNA]] research and human migration**, per [[David Reich]]'s *Who We Are and How We Got Here* — prehistory involved far more mixing than the "populations stayed put" assumption.
- **[[Absurdism]]** and the primacy of the question of [[Suicide]], from [[Albert Camus]]'s *The Myth of Sisyphus* — Camus's proposed response of embracing experiential abundance.
- **History of [[Precision Engineering]]**, in [[Simon Winchester]]'s *The Perfectionists*, contrasting [[Rolls-Royce]] hand-fitting with [[Ford Model T]] interchangeable parts.
- **[[Marginalism]]** in economics, per [[Tyler Cowen]]'s *The Marginal Revolution* — decisions hinge on incremental rather than average costs, a mode of thinking Cowen argues is losing ground to [[Econometrics]].
- **Satire and the purpose of fiction**, via [[Joseph Heller]]'s *Catch-22*.

## Key Takeaways

- **Envy's target**: Schoeck argues envy strikes nearby peers, not distant elites.
- **Historical echo**: Cultural Revolution violence fell hardest on local teachers, small landowners.
- **Calculus's audience**: Strogatz's justifications for calculus feel unnecessary to its likely readers.
- **No dilemma-free ethics**: Nussbaum argues genuine moral dilemmas resist elimination by any framework.
- **Migration was constant**: Reich's ancient DNA evidence overturns the "populations stayed put" view.
- **Camus's answer unconvincing**: Young finds pure absurdism inadequate — humans need reformable meaning.
- **Precision paradox**: Ford's interchangeable parts required *more* precision than Rolls-Royce's handcraft.
- **Marginal vs. average**: Cowen's marginalism is losing ground to econometrics in economics.
- **Fiction as thinking**: Young says *Catch-22* shows fiction generates a distinctive kind of thought.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Marginal Thinking]]**: Cowen's marginalism holds that good decisions weigh the next incremental unit of cost or benefit, not the average — a first-principles corrective to reasoning that implicitly anchors on aggregates.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central format and purpose of this piece?
**A**: Scott Young shares brief personal reflections on eight books he found interesting, ranging across psychology, philosophy, science, and economics.

**Q2**: What surprising engineering fact does Young highlight from *The Perfectionists*?
**A**: Ford's Model T, despite mass production, actually demanded greater precision than Rolls-Royce's handcrafted cars because interchangeable parts left no room for hand-fitting adjustments.

**Q3**: How does Young push back on Camus's *Myth of Sisyphus*?
**A**: He finds Camus's embrace of absurdity psychologically unconvincing, arguing humans are fundamentally goal-directed and need meaning that can be reformed rather than abandoned.
