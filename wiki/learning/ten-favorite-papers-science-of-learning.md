## Summary

Scott Young curates ten research papers that shaped his thinking on learning, drawn from roughly 500 papers he read while researching a book — spanning direct instruction versus discovery learning, expertise, transfer, self-efficacy, working memory, and self-explanation.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Direct Instruction]] vs. [[Constructivist Learning]]**: Kirschner, Sweller & Clark's paper argues research favors strongly-guided instruction over discovery/problem-based learning, despite constructivism's intuitive appeal.
- **[[Far Transfer]]**: Barnett & Ceci's taxonomy frames transfer — applying learning in a new setting — as the central unresolved issue in education, since most studies report transfer failures rather than successes.
- **[[Self-Efficacy]]**: Bandura's theory adds a second variable to motivation beyond valuing an outcome — believing you *can* execute the action needed to get there.
- **[[Long-Term Working Memory]]**: Ericsson & Kintsch argue experts learn to use long-term memory as a form of working memory, expanding effective capacity for familiar tasks beyond simple [[Chunking]].
- **[[The Feynman Technique]]** (self-explanation): Chi et al.'s research shows prompting students to explain material to themselves increases understanding by surfacing gaps that passive reading hides.

## Key Takeaways

- **The 2 Sigma Problem** (Bloom): one-on-one tutoring can produce a 2-standard-deviation improvement over group instruction; mastery learning (interim tests + targeted re-teaching) gets roughly halfway there (~0.5 SD).
- **Intuitive expertise** (Kahneman & Klein): despite opposing research traditions, both agree expert intuition only develops with highly valid environmental cues and fast feedback.
- **ATI mismatch** (Clark): low-ability students learn better with structure, high-ability students with open-ended challenge — yet students tend to *prefer* the method that works less well for them, likely because it feels less effortful.
- **Reading and intelligence** (Ritchie, Bates, Plomin): a twin study finds higher early reading ability predicts greater intelligence gains over time, supporting reading as a driver — not just a marker — of general cognitive ability.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Feedback Loops]]**: Several papers in this list (self-explanation, deliberate practice's expert-feedback dependency, mastery learning's interim testing) converge on the same underlying mechanism — learning accelerates specifically when there's a fast, accurate signal about what you don't yet know, not just repeated exposure.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Scott Young curates ten research papers he found most influential on how learning and thinking actually work, covering direct instruction, expertise, transfer, motivation, memory, and self-explanation.

**Q2**: What's the key mechanism behind the "2 Sigma Problem"?
**A**: One-on-one tutoring can produce roughly two standard deviations of improvement over classroom instruction by giving immediate, individualized feedback; mastery learning (interim tests plus targeted re-teaching) approximates part of that effect at classroom scale.

**Q3**: How would you apply the self-explanation (Feynman Technique) finding in practice?
**A**: After reading or studying new material, explain it in your own words as if teaching someone with no background — the act of generating an explanation (not just reading one) reveals specific gaps in understanding that you can then go back and close.
