## Summary
Three major U.S. studies of school cellphone restrictions, including a new Harvard study, find teeny-tiny or zero academic gains. The Harvard estimate of 0.0087 standard deviations was framed as one to two extra weeks of learning, but critics say it is effectively zero and that researchers overstate what results support. Researchers still favor keeping bans and ask social media companies for data to test whether out-of-school phone use is the real driver.

## Core Concepts
- [[School Cellphone Bans]]: restricted or banned in at least 40 states and D.C. since 2023.
- [[Harvard CEPR Study]] ([[Tom Kane]]): locked pouches raised combined math and reading scores by 0.0087 standard deviations; 0.0068 under the originally planned method, not statistically significant.
- [[Effect Size]]: even 0.1 standard deviation is considered quite small.
- [[Statistical Significance]]: the 0.0068 estimate could not rule out a true effect of zero.
- Earlier studies: a Florida district study (Oct 2025, [[David Figlio]]) found a 0.6 percentile point gain; a national study (Apr 2026, Stanford, Duke, Penn, Michigan; [[Tom Dee]]) found little to none.
- [[Research Overinterpretation]]: [[Brendan Bartanen]] and [[Candice Odgers]] criticize claims beyond what results support.
- [[Social Media Data Access]]: researchers want platform data on in-school and out-of-school use.
- [[Cost-Effectiveness]]: pouches cost about $25, versus thousands per student for tutoring.

## Key Takeaways
- **Tiny Effect**: Harvard's 0.0087 SD is under one-hundredth of a standard deviation.
- **Spin vs. Data**: Researchers translated it into one to two weeks of learning; critics call it negligible.
- **Not Significant**: Under the original method, the 0.0068 estimate could not rule out zero.
- **Behavior Changed**: Phone use fell; suspensions rose initially, then subsided after year one.
- **Florida Result**: A 0.6 percentile point gain, roughly 50th to just under 51st percentile.
- **Cost Argument**: Kane calls $25 pouches better "bang for the buck" than tutoring.
- **Continue Anyway**: Even skeptics like Dee want bans to continue, but not claim proof.
- **Missing Data**: Out-of-school scrolling may drive harm; platforms hold the data.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Small estimates are not evidence of an effect** — An effect indistinguishable from zero cannot be reported as a win; a policy can be worth continuing without evidence that it works. · *model:* [[Statistical Significance]]
→ explains: Not Significant, Spin vs. Data, Continue Anyway

**T2. Measuring only one exposure can miss the real driver** — If harm comes mainly from out-of-school phone use, restricting school-day use will show little effect. · *model:* [[Confounding Variable]]
→ explains: Missing Data, Tiny Effect

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about school cellphone bans?
**A**: Three major U.S. studies show tiny or zero academic gains, so the evidence does not show bans work, even though researchers often favor keeping them.

**Q2**: What specific evidence shows how small the Harvard result is?
**A**: The gain was 0.0087 standard deviations, and 0.0068 under the originally planned method, which was not statistically significant and could not rule out zero.

**Q3**: What does the article suggest researchers and policymakers do next?
**A**: Keep trying bans without claiming proof, wait to see if annual gains accumulate, and get social media data to test whether out-of-school phone use matters more.
