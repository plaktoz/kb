## Summary

A [[California Competes]] study of 27 "comebacker" graduates — adults who stopped out of college and later returned and finished — argues that earnings-only measures capture just part of a degree's [[Return on Investment]]. Comebackers described financial, personal, and social benefits weighed against financial, opportunity, and personal costs, with personal and social gains often feeding back into career outcomes. The report recommends that California policymakers and institutions raise the benefits of a degree and cut its costs, especially time to degree, to make college a worthwhile investment for [[Adult Learners]].

## Core Concepts

- **Comebackers**: Students who attended college, stopped out for any length of time, and re-enrolled; they overlap with [[Adult Learners]] (ages 25–64 or with adult responsibilities) and the "some college, no credential" population.
- **Multidimensional ROI framework**: ROI is a function of economic, personal, and social benefits minus financial, opportunity, and personal costs, extending the series' earlier earnings-minus-costs formula.
- **Financial benefits**: Credentials that unlock promotion or new career pathways; uneven by region, with rural graduates facing limited job prospects and overqualification concerns.
- **[[Durable Skills]]**: Foundational, transferable competencies (writing, public speaking, time management, problem-solving, discipline) that employers increasingly screen for.
- **Self-confidence**: Completing a degree reframed graduates' sense of their abilities and gave them courage to apply for promotions or better jobs.
- **Social benefits**: Broadened worldviews through contact with different perspectives, and [[Intergenerational Mobility]] as parents model and support their children's education.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: Forgone wages, extra childcare costs, and missed family time from attending class and studying, plus long commutes for rural students.
- **Credit loss and time to degree**: Courses that don't count toward a major or transfer extend enrollment, exhaust financial aid, and push students into loans (some $80,000–$100,000).
- **Personal costs**: Stress and mental toll from juggling work, family, and school, crowding out sleep, socializing, and exercise.
- **Cost-reducing levers**: High-quality [[Online Learning]], [[Competency-Based Education]], [[Credit for Prior Learning]], basic-needs support, and reformed [[Cal Grant]] eligibility.
- **Benefit-raising levers**: Early career advising, richer local labor market data in the [[Cradle-to-Career Data System]], embedded durable-skill curricula, holistic ROI measures, and [[Andragogy]]-centered teaching.

## Key Takeaways

- **Target Population**: 6M+ Californians aged 25–54 have a diploma but no degree.
- **Study Design**: 27 comebackers interviewed in 2023 and again in 2025.
- **Earnings Gains**: About half saw a raise, promotion, or better job within three years.
- **Clear Goals Pay**: Graduates enrolling with specific career goals were likelier to earn more.
- **Kevin's Path**: Library wage rose from $15 to nearly $50 an hour via degrees.
- **Rural Gap**: Rural bachelor's holders less likely to see positive economic ROI.
- **Benefits Interlock**: Personal and social gains often directly improved career and financial outcomes.
- **Confidence Effect**: Self-confidence prompted graduates to pursue promotions they'd otherwise skip.
- **Hidden Costs**: Forgone wages, childcare, and family time are part of college's true cost.
- **Credit Loss**: Non-counting courses lengthened time to degree and drove borrowing.
- **Debt Deterrent**: Fear of debt stopped some after an associate's degree.
- **Measure What Matters**: Institutions steer resources toward whatever indicators get measured.
- **Shorten Time to Degree**: CBE and credit for prior learning cut direct and opportunity costs.
- **Aid Restrictions**: Cal Grant age and time-out-of-school limits exclude many adult learners.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. The true cost of a choice includes the alternatives it displaces** — For adults, every hour spent in class or studying is an hour not spent earning or caregiving, so the price of college exceeds tuition and fees. · *model:* [[Opportunity Cost]]
→ explains: Hidden Costs, Debt Deterrent, Shorten Time to Degree

**T2. Total cost scales with time to completion** — Because tuition, living expenses, and forgone time all accrue per term, anything that extends the path to a credential multiplies its cost, and anything that shortens it improves the return.
→ explains: Credit Loss, Shorten Time to Degree, Debt Deterrent

**T3. Institutions optimize what they measure** — Organizations direct resources and programming toward measured indicators, so an earnings-only ROI metric leaves non-financial value unmanaged even when it drives outcomes.
→ explains: Measure What Matters, Benefits Interlock, Confidence Effect

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the report's central claim about college ROI for adult learners?
**A**: Earnings-based ROI is only a partial picture; comebackers experience a multidimensional return combining financial, personal, and social benefits against financial, opportunity, and personal costs.

**Q2**: How do extra credits raise the cost of a degree for comebackers?
**A**: Taking courses that don't count toward a major or transfer extends time to degree, which can exhaust financial aid and lead most affected comebackers to take out loans, sometimes $80,000–$100,000.

**Q3**: How could a college apply these findings to improve ROI for returning adults?
**A**: It could raise benefits through early career advising and embedded durable-skill teaching, and cut costs through flexible deadlines, quality online courses, competency-based education, and credit for prior learning.
