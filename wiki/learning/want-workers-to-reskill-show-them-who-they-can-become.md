## Summary
A study of 1,100 unemployed Italian workers found low uptake of free reskilling programs was driven more by identity concerns than income — only 38% wanted to retrain even for better-paying roles, fearing loss of status. Shifting professional identity, more than providing wage data, drove reskilling interest.

## Core Concepts
- [[Professional Identity]] as a stronger driver of reskilling decisions than income data.
- [[Status Loss Aversion]] as a barrier to workforce retraining.
- Government-sponsored [[Reskilling Programs]] and their low observed uptake.

## Key Takeaways
- **Low Uptake**: Only 38% of surveyed workers wanted to retrain, even for better pay.
- **Identity Over Income**: Fear of status loss outweighed financial incentives in retraining decisions.
- **Wage Data Helps Modestly**: Concrete wage/job data increased interest, but less than identity-shifting interventions.
- **Bigger Lever**: Helping workers envision themselves in new roles had the largest effect on reskilling interest.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models
- **[[Identity-Based Motivation]]**: People act to preserve or express their self-concept; retraining is resisted less for financial reasons than because it threatens who someone believes themselves to be.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's core finding?
**A**: Reskilling uptake among unemployed workers is driven more by identity and status concerns than by income potential.

**Q2**: What data supports this?
**A**: Among 1,100 surveyed Italian workers, only 38% wanted to retrain even when new roles paid better, and identity-shifting interventions outperformed wage-data interventions at increasing interest.

**Q3**: How should employers or policymakers apply this?
**A**: Design reskilling programs that help workers envision a new professional identity and connect training explicitly to career advancement, not just show wage comparisons.
