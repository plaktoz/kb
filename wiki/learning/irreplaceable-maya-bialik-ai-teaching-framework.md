## Summary

[[Maya Bialik]] argues that the question is not whether to embrace or ban AI in education but when and how to use it. Her book *Irreplaceable*, co-written with [[Peter Nilsson]], gives teachers a framework for telling when AI enhances learning and when it short-circuits it. The book is organized around three lasting contexts: teacher preparation, student learning, and teacher-student collaboration. It stresses using AI to enhance teacher agency while guarding against [[Cognitive Offloading]].

## Core Concepts

- **[[Irreplaceable (Book)]]**: *Irreplaceable: How AI Changes Everything and Nothing in Teaching and Learning* (February 2026), by Bialik and Nilsson.
- **Three educational contexts**: Teachers preparing for class, students learning, and teachers and students working together.
- **AI as teacher aide**: AI helps with research and planning (studying curricular requirements, drafting lesson plans) and with feedback (grading, immediate comments on student work).
- **[[Teacher Agency]]**: Bialik wants AI that elevates teachers' ingenuity rather than adding "slop." She suggests treating it as a collaborator or agent, not just a tool.
- **Learning assistant vs. doing assistant**: For students, AI can act as an on-demand tutor (learning assistant) or can complete tasks that are not central to the learning goal (doing assistant).
- **[[Calculator Analogy]]**: Once students master basic skills, AI can free them for higher-order challenges, the way calculators did for math.
- **Classroom roles**: AI can handle administrative work such as attendance and data tracking, and can act as an instructional coach during and after lessons.
- **[[Cognitive Offloading]]**: Brains try to conserve energy, so using AI for offloading is tempting. Bialik's countermeasures are student accountability and reflection on AI use.
- **[[QuestionWell]]**: A tool Bialik co-created that generates instructional materials and exports them to Moodle, Kahoot! and Quizlet.
- **[[Socrates]] and writing**: Socrates feared writing would weaken memory. Bialik uses the example to show that every technology involves tradeoffs.

## Key Takeaways

- **Reframe the debate**: Ask when and how to use AI, not pro versus anti.
- **Planning prompts**: Ask AI what challenge would naturally require students to use a skill.
- **Personalization**: Ask AI which parts of a goal students could personalize to their interests.
- **Distinguish uses**: Learning assistant (tutor) versus doing assistant (completing non-core tasks).
- **Skills first**: Let AI handle higher-order work only after basics are mastered, as with calculators.
- **Guardrail**: Make students accountable and have them reflect on their AI use choices.
- **QuestionWell reach**: About 8,400 questions generated for more than 5,000 teachers in 185 countries.
- **Nuance**: Every technology comes with tradeoffs, as Socrates' worry about writing shows.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Desirable Difficulties]]**: Learning depends on effortful processing, so AI used as a "doing assistant" for the core learning goal removes the struggle that builds skill. This explains why Bialik limits offloading to tasks that are not central to the goal.
- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: Bialik's Socrates example looks past the immediate cost of a technology (writing did weaken memory) to its downstream payoff (the information age). She asks the same of AI.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Bialik's central argument about AI in education?
**A**: The pro-versus-anti binary is the wrong frame. Educators should judge when and how AI enhances learning rather than short-circuiting it.

**Q2**: How does Bialik distinguish between student uses of AI?
**A**: A "learning assistant" works like an on-demand tutor that supports learning. A "doing assistant" completes tasks that are not central to the learning goal, much as calculators handle computation once basics are mastered.

**Q3**: How should a teacher guard against AI undermining learning?
**A**: Teachers should hold students accountable and have them reflect on when and how they use AI. This counters the natural pull toward cognitive offloading.
