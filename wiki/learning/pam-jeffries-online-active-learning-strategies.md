## Summary

Vanderbilt Nursing Dean Pam Jeffries outlines concrete active-learning techniques for online courses — 1-minute papers, learning communities, structured debates, and portfolios — built on Chickering and Gamson's seven principles of good practice, with prompt feedback identified as the single strongest driver of course quality.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Chickering and Gamson's Seven Principles]]**: A foundational framework (active learning, prompt feedback, student-faculty interaction, high expectations, among others) that applies equally to online and in-person teaching quality.
- **[[1-Minute Paper]]**: A rapid formative-assessment technique where students write a concise page linking a concept to practice, scored against a 3-5 criterion rubric for fast, low-friction grading.
- **[[Structured Online Debate]]**: Students are split into pro, con, and questioning/summary groups, developing arguments asynchronously so students in different time zones or with work obligations can participate.
- **[[Asynchronous Learning Design]]**: Jeffries deliberately favors asynchronous formats over synchronous ones because fixed meeting times create barriers for working students, undermining the flexibility that motivates online learning in the first place.

## Key Takeaways

- **Prompt feedback matters most**: Even a brief acknowledgment ("I'll get back to you by end of week") keeps students engaged and signals faculty care.
- **Rubrics remove ambiguity**: Clear 3-5 criterion rubrics prevent grading from feeling like a surprise to students.
- **Community sizing**: Splitting a 50-student class into 3 sub-communities (with TAs) preserves quality feedback while managing faculty workload.
- **Evaluation diversity**: Jeffries mixes rubrics, timed multiple-choice quizzes, portfolios, and reflection papers rather than relying on one assessment type.
- **Evidence base**: Over a decade of research shows online learning can match face-to-face learning outcomes when designed around these principles.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Effective online teaching depends on applying established active-learning best practices — especially prompt feedback — not on the medium itself.

**Q2**: What is a concrete example of an active-learning technique described?
**A**: The 1-minute paper, where students concisely link a concept to practice in a short timed writing exercise graded against a simple rubric.

**Q3**: Why does Jeffries prefer asynchronous over synchronous online formats?
**A**: Synchronous formats impose fixed meeting times that create barriers for working students, undermining the flexibility and educational mobility that draw students to online programs.
