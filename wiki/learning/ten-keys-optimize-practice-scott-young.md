## Summary

Getting good at a skill requires practice, but not all practice produces equal gains — Scott Young distills ten research-backed strategies (starting from examples, retrieval over rereading, spaced sessions, interleaving, side-by-side comparison of confusions, self-explanation, recorded self-review, the 85% difficulty rule, layered feedback, and matching practice to the real target skill) for optimizing the rate of improvement.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Practice vs. Problem Solving]]**: Practice applies a known method; problem solving is used when the correct method is unknown. Starting with worked examples avoids practicing a poor self-discovered strategy or missing the underlying pattern.
- **[[Retrieval Practice]]**: Testing yourself with a closed book (free recall, flashcards, practice questions) builds stronger memory than rereading; shift emphasis from studying examples toward independent retrieval as competence grows.
- **[[Spacing Effect]]**: The same total study time produces far more durable memory when spread across days rather than crammed into one session.
- **[[Interleaving]]**: Mixing problem types instead of practicing them in predictable blocks forces the brain to identify a problem's type from its features — closer to how problems actually appear outside of school.
- **[[85% Rule]]**: For tasks with a clear correct/incorrect distinction, aim for roughly 85% accuracy — hard enough to challenge, not so hard that learning becomes inefficient or frustrating.

## Key Takeaways

- When confused between two similar cases (e.g., two easily-mixed characters or concepts), place them side-by-side rather than hoping repetition alone resolves the confusion.
- Self-explanation — walking through why each step of a method works — builds the understanding that lets skills generalize when a question's format changes.
- For skills at the edge of ability, record and review the attempt afterward rather than trying to monitor and perform simultaneously.
- Direct feedback (the actual outcome) is more accurate; indirect feedback (others' commentary) is more constructive — seek both rather than relying on just one.
- Skill transfer is narrower than assumed: get precise about the real target application, and for ambiguous skills, alternate between deliberate practice and doing the real thing.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the article?
**A**: Improvement rate depends heavily on how you practice, not just how much — ten specific, research-backed adjustments (like retrieval over rereading, spacing, and interleaving) meaningfully accelerate skill acquisition versus generic repetition.

**Q2**: What is the 85% Rule and how should it be applied?
**A**: For tasks with a clear right/wrong answer, calibrate difficulty so you succeed about 85% of the time — high enough to avoid frustration from constant failure, low enough to ensure genuine challenge and learning.

**Q3**: How would someone apply the interleaving principle when studying for an exam covering multiple topics?
**A**: Instead of completing all problems from one unit before moving to the next, mix problem types together during practice sessions so recognizing which method to use becomes part of what's being trained — matching the unpredictability of the real exam.
