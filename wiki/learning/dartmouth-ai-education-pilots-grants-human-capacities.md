## Summary

Dartmouth is launching grants, computing access, curricular initiatives, and campus programming that let faculty both use AI and teach or research without it, while schools draft ethical guidelines. AI Fellow Roopika Risam frames the strategy as an investment in people, with a focus on strengthening students' core human capacities. The measure of success is whether AI improves rather than degrades community members' experiences.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Roopika Risam]]**: Dartmouth AI Fellow leading policy-gap analysis, community conversations, and pilot evaluation.
- **[[AI Research Catalyst Grants]]**: Seed funding for four to eight interdisciplinary projects using or critically studying AI.
- **[[Course Redesign Grants]]**: Support for AI-resilient learning, AI with purpose, or both.
- **[[AI Curricular Design Grants]]** and **[[Pedagogical Innovation Grants]]**: Funding for new AI degrees and cross-disciplinary teaching experiments.
- **[[AI Computing Access]]**: Remote GPUs at the Massachusetts Green High Performance Computing Center.
- **[[AI-Resilient Learning]]**: Assignments designed so students engage in the intellectual struggle without AI.
- **[[AI Governance in Higher Education]]**: School-level ethical guidelines plus shared vocabulary built through community conversations.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dual Approach**: Grants support both using AI and teaching or learning without it.
- **Research Seed Funding**: Four to eight interdisciplinary AI projects funded via the President's office.
- **Teaching Grants**: Course redesign, AI curricular design, and pedagogical innovation tracks.
- **Human Capacities**: Proposals sought to strengthen students' thinking, writing, and evidence evaluation.
- **Policy Clarity**: Schools draft guidelines; aim for consistency despite differing course policies.
- **Success Metric**: Whether AI improves rather than degrades community experiences.
- **Programming**: March 3 lecture by Tressie McMillan Cottom; seminar on AI and language teaching.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Dartmouth's overall approach to AI in education?
**A**: It funds both AI use and AI-free or critical approaches through grants, computing, curriculum, and programming, framed as an investment in people.

**Q2**: What concrete mechanisms support the strategy?
**A**: AI Research Catalyst Grants fund four to eight projects, and teaching grants cover course redesign, AI curricular design, and pedagogical innovation. Remote GPU access and school-level ethical guidelines round it out.

**Q3**: What should faculty proposals aim for, and how is success judged?
**A**: Proposals should strengthen students' core human capacities, such as thinking, writing, and evaluating evidence, including by engaging in learning struggles without AI. Success is judged by whether AI improves rather than declines community members' experiences.
