## Summary
Generative AI can support learning in one context while bypassing it in another, so course design must start from what students should learn and do themselves rather than from the tool. Students should critique, improve, test, apply, or explain AI output instead of merely consuming it. The article offers reflection questions to guide AI-supported course design in asynchronous online learning.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Generative AI]]**: Tools that explain concepts, summarize readings, give feedback, and draft assignments within seconds.
- **[[Asynchronous Online Learning]]**: Instructors cannot observe students working in real time, which makes evidence of learning harder to gather.
- **[[Adult Learners]]**: Online students balancing career and family, motivated by practical outcomes and application.
- **[[Instructional Design]]**: Course design built around learning outcomes, authentic application, and visible reasoning.
- **[[Productive Struggle]]**: The effortful thinking that AI use may unintentionally remove.
- **[[Authentic Assessment]]**: Assessments that give meaningful evidence of learning.
- **SPS Distance Learning** at [[Northwestern University]]: Source of the collaborative reflection-question initiative.

## Key Takeaways
- **Tool Duality**: AI that supports learning in one context may bypass it in another.
- **Right Question**: Ask what thinking students must still do themselves, not whether they can use AI.
- **Engage, Don't Consume**: Students should critique, improve, test, apply, or explain AI output.
- **Visible Learning**: AI use should make learning more visible, not less.
- **Evidence of Reasoning**: Ask students for process or evidence showing their reasoning.
- **Relevance Test**: Require AI use only when relevant to the discipline, not because it exists.
- **Stable Goal**: Tools change, but the goal of knowledge, judgment, and habits of mind remains.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Learning requires the learner to do the thinking** — When a tool performs the analysis, application, or judgment, the learner's practice is bypassed even if the output looks polished.
→ explains: Tool Duality, Right Question, Engage, Don't Consume

**T2. Learning must be observable to be assessed** — Instructors can only judge whether outcomes are met if students produce evidence of their reasoning; designs should make that evidence more visible, not less.
→ explains: Visible Learning, Evidence of Reasoning

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central argument about AI in course design?
**A**: Course design should begin with the learning outcomes and what students must do independently, not with where an AI tool can be used. AI becomes one instructional tool rather than the driving force.

**Q2**: Why does generative AI complicate assessing student learning?
**A**: It can produce explanations, examples, feedback, and polished drafts in seconds, making it harder to tell whether students are developing the underlying knowledge and skills.

**Q3**: How should faculty design AI-supported activities?
**A**: Ask students to analyze, apply, explain, create, or evaluate AI output and to show their reasoning. Also check whether the activity removes productive struggle or uses AI only because it is available.
