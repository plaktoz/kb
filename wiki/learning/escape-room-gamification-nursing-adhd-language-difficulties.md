## Summary

A quasi-experimental cohort study of 426 nursing students in Israel, published in *Nursing Open*, found that a virtual [[Escape Room Learning|escape room]] had uneven effects depending on the type of learner. Students with [[ADHD]] or learning difficulties scored much higher on multiple-choice questions, while students with difficulties in the language of instruction scored much lower on constructed-response questions and on total scores. The authors argue this undercuts the common assumption that [[Gamification]] benefits everyone: educators should ask not only whether a format works, but for whom.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Escape Room Learning]]**: An individual, self-paced digital activity built on [[Genially]], lasting 30–60 minutes, with eight tasks: classifying nursing theories, matching concepts to definitions, identifying theorists, interpreting images, drag-and-drop and multiple choice.
- **Study design**: 150 traditionally taught students (control) versus 276 escape-room students across the 2023–2025 academic years. Both groups sat the same final exam, and groups were set by academic year, not randomised.
- **Learner classification**: Groups came from institutional support-services records, not self-report. There were 153 students with language difficulties, 32 with learning difficulties including ADHD, and 241 with none recorded.
- **[[Second-Language Learners]]**: Nearly half the sample belonged to Arab society, for whom Hebrew, the language of instruction, was often a second language.
- **Two-way [[ANCOVA]]**: Controlling for age and gender, it found significant interactions between format and learner category on all three outcomes. [[Holm Correction|Holm-corrected]] post hoc tests located the disadvantage in the language-difficulty subgroup.
- **[[Cognitive Load Theory]]**: The authors hypothesise, without measuring it, that the game wrapper adds extraneous load and that text-heavy, multi-layered puzzle instructions burden students who are still decoding the language.
- **[[Bloom's Revised Taxonomy]]**: Escape-room students did worse on lower-level constructed-response items (remembering and understanding) but showed no difference at higher levels. The authors call this tentative because the coding had no inter-rater reliability check.
- **[[Universal Design for Learning]]**: The findings support offering multiple means of representation, engagement and expression for diverse learners.
- **Authors**: Weil-Lotan, Harari, Tannenbaum-Baruchi and Theilla (2026), *Nursing Open* 13(10), e70882.

## Key Takeaways

- **Aggregate result**: Escape-room group scored lower on constructed-response items, with a small effect size.
- **ADHD subgroup**: Multiple-choice scores of 93.33% versus 70.00%, a large but exploratory effect (n=32).
- **ADHD benefit is narrow**: Gains were in recognition only, not in written or total scores.
- **Language difficulties**: Constructed-response scores of 54.20% versus 71.00%, and total scores of 61.68% versus 72.70%.
- **Not just time pressure**: The deficit persisted despite 30 minutes of extra exam time.
- **Limitations**: No randomisation, exams differed across years, one instructor, no engagement data.
- **Not causal**: The authors say the results are associations, not effects of the escape room.
- **Recommendation**: Use escape rooms to complement traditional teaching, with vocabulary and multilingual supports.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Flaw of Averages]]**: The overall result looked like a small, uniform shortfall, but it hid opposite effects in two subgroups. Judging an intervention by its average effect can mislead about who it helps and who it harms.
- **[[Correlation vs. Causation]]**: Allocation by academic year, exams that changed across years and no baseline equivalence mean cohort effects could explain the differences. The authors themselves say the gaps should not be read as caused by the escape room.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about gamified escape-room instruction?
**A**: Its effects differed sharply by learner type. Students with ADHD or learning difficulties scored higher on multiple-choice questions, while students with language difficulties scored lower on constructed-response and total scores, which challenges the idea that gamification benefits everyone.

**Q2**: What evidence suggests the language-difficulty disadvantage was not simply due to time pressure?
**A**: These students scored 54.20% versus 71.00% on constructed-response items and 61.68% versus 72.70% overall, even though they were entitled to 30 minutes of extra exam time. The authors read this as a sign that the problem goes beyond time pressure, possibly extra cognitive load from text-heavy puzzle instructions.

**Q3**: How should an educator apply these findings when adopting a new engaging format?
**A**: Ask "for whom does it work?" and use escape rooms to complement traditional teaching rather than replace it. Add vocabulary support, multimodal and multilingual materials, flexible pacing and structured debriefs, following Universal Design for Learning.
