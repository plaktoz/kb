## Summary

Stanford SCALE Initiative's November 2026 update of The Evidence Base on AI in K-12 now covers 52 causal studies, up from 20 in March. It finds AI is more useful when students use it to understand and practise material than when they use it to produce work for them, though evidence for lasting academic gains remains insufficient. Impact depends on how the technology is designed, introduced, and used.

## Core Concepts

- [[Stanford SCALE Initiative]]: publisher of The Evidence Base on AI in K-12, reviewing randomised controlled trials and quasi-experimental studies.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: students handing academic tasks to AI instead of engaging with the underlying material.
- [[Generative AI in Education]]: student-facing tools (28 studies) and educator-facing tools (4 studies).
- [[Learning Assistant vs. Replacement]]: the distinction between AI that supports thinking and AI that substitutes for independent thinking.
- [[Adaptive Learning]]: interactive formats, adaptive exercises, and clear guidance show promise.
- [[Causal Research Synthesis]]: 52 papers, with five new student-facing studies from US K-12 settings.

## Key Takeaways

- **Evidence Growth**: 52 causal studies, up from 20 in the March 2026 edition.
- **Understand vs. Produce**: AI helps more for understanding and practice than for producing work.
- **Correct Answer Trap**: Producing a correct answer does not prove the concept was understood.
- **Experience Gains**: AI can improve confidence, learning experience, and well-being without raising outcomes.
- **Coding Evidence**: Undergraduates completed programming tasks with AI without stronger independent understanding.
- **Teacher Tools**: AI cut lesson-planning time, but one study showed lower student motivation and confidence.
- **Representativeness Caveat**: Limited, varied studies mean findings do not represent all K-12 schools.
- **Open Question**: Lasting academic improvement from AI remains unestablished.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Offloading the task is not the same as learning it** — Finishing work with AI's help does not mean the learner understood the concepts, so AI helps most when it supports the learner's own thinking. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Understand vs. Produce, Correct Answer Trap, Coding Evidence

**T2. Impact depends on design and use, not the tool itself** — The same technology produces benefits or harm depending on how it is designed, introduced, and used by students and teachers.
→ explains: Teacher Tools, Experience Gains, Open Question

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of Stanford's November 2026 review?
**A**: AI appears more useful when students use it to understand and practise material than when they use it to produce work on their behalf. Evidence that it delivers lasting academic improvement is still insufficient.

**Q2**: How large is the evidence base, and what are its limits?
**A**: It now has 52 causal papers (up from 20), of which 28 examine student-facing tools and four educator-facing tools. The studies are limited in number and varied in setting, so they are not representative of all K-12 schools.

**Q3**: What does this imply for how schools should introduce AI?
**A**: Favour interactive formats, adaptive exercises, and clear guidance that support student thinking and teacher instruction rather than replacing independent learning or professional judgement. Teacher-facing tools also need care, since one study found reduced student motivation and confidence.
