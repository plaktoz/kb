## Summary

A within-subjects Wizard-of-Oz study with 27 introductory computer science students had the same human give feedback while presenting one tutor as human and the other as AI. Students rated both favorably with no significant difference in instructional value, but many doubted an AI could genuinely care about them, matching higher relatedness scores for the "human" tutor. Students also communicated more casually with the human and more explicitly with the AI, suggesting human and AI tutoring play complementary roles.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Self-Determination Theory]]**: Autonomy, competence, and relatedness need satisfaction were measured for each tutor.
- **[[Wizard of Oz Study]]**: The same human tutor provided feedback in both conditions using a common protocol; only the stated identity differed.
- **[[Explain in Plain English]] (EiPE) Problems**: Students describe Python code behavior in English during text-based tutoring sessions.
- **Perceived Tutor Identity**: Belief about who gives feedback shaped how social cues like praise, humor, and emojis were read.
- **[[Help-Seeking]] Pressure**: Some students felt more pressure with a human; others found AI less judgmental.
- **[[Cognitive Offloading]]**: Relying on AI to organize incomplete thoughts may reduce learning from articulating questions.

## Key Takeaways

- **Sample**: 27 introductory CS students; none of four construct comparisons significant after Bonferroni correction.
- **Competence Parity**: Median competence need satisfaction was equal; feedback content was judged similarly.
- **Relatedness Gap**: Largest difference on "The tutor cares about me"; 11 questioned AI care.
- **Pressure Split**: Five felt more anxiety with the human; some were motivated by it.
- **Communication Styles**: Nine were more social with the human; eight were more direct with the AI.
- **Design Implication**: Treat human and AI tutoring as complementary; keep warmth in AI tutors.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Motivation depends on psychological needs beyond instructional quality** — Equal feedback quality can still yield different experiences, because relatedness (feeling cared about) is a separate need from competence. · *model:* [[Self-Determination Theory]]
→ explains: Competence Parity, Relatedness Gap

**T2. Beliefs about the source change how identical behavior is interpreted** — The same feedback and social cues were read as more meaningful when believed to come from a human, and users adapt how they communicate to who they think is listening.
→ explains: Relatedness Gap, Communication Styles, Pressure Split

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the study find about perceived tutor identity?
**A**: Students judged both tutors' feedback similarly, but many questioned whether an AI could genuinely care about them, which matched higher relatedness scores for the tutor believed to be human.

**Q2**: How did the researchers isolate the effect of perceived identity?
**A**: In a Wizard-of-Oz design, the same human gave feedback in both sessions while students were told one tutor was human and the other AI.

**Q3**: What should educators do given these results?
**A**: Treat human and AI tutoring as complementary, use AI as an accessible lower-pressure option, provide pathways to human help, and keep warmth in AI tutors rather than removing it.
