## Summary
The instructional designer role is being redefined by four connected shifts — from course builder to performance consultant, manual to AI-assisted production, completion metrics to business outcomes, and tool expertise to adaptable technology fluency — while classic design models (ADDIE, SAM, Bloom's Taxonomy, Gagné's Nine Events) remain the stable foundation underneath fast-changing tools.

## Core Concepts

- [[Four Shifts in Instructional Design]] — the article's organizing frame: (1) course builder → performance problem-solver, (2) manual → AI-assisted production, (3) completion rates → measurable business outcomes, (4) tool expertise → adaptable technology fluency
- [[ADDIE Model]] — Analysis, Design, Development, Implementation, Evaluation; the linear, thorough model still relevant for compliance training and large-scale, documentation-heavy programs
- [[SAM (Successive Approximation Model)]] — a faster, iterative alternative to ADDIE using short sprints with frequent prototyping and stakeholder feedback, closer to agile software practice
- [[Technology Fluency vs. Tool Expertise]] — the article's central argument: because tools change faster than principles, the durable skill is the ability to quickly evaluate and adapt to new tools, not mastery of any single platform

## Key Takeaways

- **Shift 1**: designers increasingly ask whether a course is even the right solution, versus a job aid, process change, or manager coaching.
- **Shift 2**: AI compresses first-draft production (scripts, storyboards, quiz banks) from days to hours; the key skill becomes evaluating and correcting AI output, not prompting.
- **Shift 3**: L&D teams face growing pressure to show training changed a real metric (error rate, sales, performance), not just completion counts.
- **Adoption data**: per LinkedIn's 2025 Workplace Learning Report, roughly 7 in 10 L&D professionals are already experimenting with or integrating AI into their work.
- **Staying current without burning out**: follow 2–3 curated sources (not everything), join one active community, build one small project per quarter with a new tool, read one research report per year cover-to-cover, and revisit fundamentals annually.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: The instructional designer role is shifting from course production toward performance consulting, with AI-assisted workflows and adaptable technology fluency replacing deep single-tool expertise as the key differentiator.

**Q2**: What specific skill does the article say matters more than knowing how to write a "perfect" AI prompt?
**A**: Knowing how to evaluate what AI produces and fix what it gets wrong — judgment, accuracy, and tone — since AI-assisted first drafts have already compressed the raw production time.

**Q3**: How does the article recommend staying current without burning out on the constant pace of new tools?
**A**: Curate a small set of sources rather than tracking everything, join one active peer community, build a small project each quarter with a new tool, and revisit foundational models (ADDIE, Bloom's) annually with fresh eyes.
