## Summary

The American Psychological Association's Expert Report on Children's and Adolescents' Learning with Educational Technology warns that student engagement with EdTech does not equal learning — the ability to transfer knowledge outside an app is the true measure. Generative AI tools pose particular risks by boosting immediate performance without building underlying skills. The report offers 10 evidence-based recommendations for families, educators, policymakers, and developers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Engagement vs. Learning]]**: Clicking, responding to animations, and collecting rewards signals attention, not knowledge acquisition. Transfer of learning to new contexts is the stronger indicator.
- **[[Generative AI in Education]]**: AI can improve a student's essay output without making them a better writer — separating performance from learning is the new challenge.
- **[[Transfer of Learning]]**: Durable knowledge is defined by whether students can apply it outside the original app or screen environment.
- **[[Evidence-Based EdTech]]**: The APA calls on developers to provide credible, independent evidence of learning outcomes, and on institutions to demand it before adoption.
- **[[Active AI Evaluation]]**: Rather than banning AI, the report recommends teaching students to question chatbot assumptions, identify reasoning gaps, and compare responses against other sources — treating AI as something to test, not an answer machine.
- **[[Adult Involvement in EdTech]]**: Meaningful educator and parent engagement with technology implementation is a key recommendation, not an optional enhancement.

## Key Takeaways

- **Engagement fallacy**: Time on screen or click frequency does not confirm learning has occurred.
- **AI performance gap**: AI-assisted essays look better but don't make students better writers.
- **Transfer test**: Real learning = student can apply knowledge outside the original tech context.
- **Developer accountability**: Companies must demonstrate products improve learning, not just engagement.
- **10 APA recommendations**: Prioritize durable skills, require independent evidence, involve adults, invest in research.
- **Parent heuristic**: If the app involves dressing characters or collecting rewards, question its educational value.
- **AI literacy**: Teach students to probe, challenge, and cross-check AI responses rather than accept them.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When engagement becomes the metric EdTech is optimized for, developers build for clicks and animations rather than actual knowledge transfer — the proxy displaces the goal.
- **[[Cargo Cult Learning]]**: Students (and platforms) replicate the surface behaviors of learning (interacting with content, producing outputs) without the underlying cognitive work that creates durable understanding.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the APA's core argument about educational technology and engagement?
**A**: Student engagement with EdTech — such as frequent clicking or responding to rewards — does not necessarily mean learning is occurring; transfer of knowledge to new contexts is the stronger indicator.

**Q2**: Why do generative AI tools pose a specific risk in learning environments according to the report?
**A**: AI can improve immediate outputs (e.g. a better essay) without building the underlying skill, meaning performance and learning must now be separated just as engagement and learning must be.

**Q3**: What practical approach does the APA recommend for students using AI tools?
**A**: Students should treat AI less as an answer machine and more as something to continuously test and challenge — questioning assumptions, identifying reasoning gaps, and comparing responses against other sources.
