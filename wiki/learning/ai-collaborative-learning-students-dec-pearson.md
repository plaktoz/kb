## Summary
Focus groups with 23 students from 21 institutions, run by the Digital Education Council with Pearson, show that AI is reducing the debate that makes group work collaborative. Whether AI helps or harms depends on when and how it is used, and students cannot enforce informal AI rules among themselves. The report argues institutions need course-level guidance defining AI's role at each stage of group tasks.

## Core Concepts
- [[Collaborative Learning]]: students building understanding together through discussion and debate.
- [[Digital Education Council]] and [[Pearson]]: authors of the report *Designing AI for Collaborative Learning*, with 15 emerging methodologies and six design principles.
- [[AI Policy in Higher Education]]: general policies cover whether AI is allowed, not how groups use it.
- Stage-based AI use: before meeting, during group work, and after task completion.
- [[Idea Homogenization]]: students prepare with the same AI tools and arrive with the same ideas.
- Assessment as reinforcement: presentations or follow-up questions to verify understanding.

## Key Takeaways
- **Adoption**: 88% of students use AI in their studies, per DEC's 2026 survey.
- **Lost Debate**: Groups arrive with similar AI-shaped ideas and have fewer differences to explore.
- **Outsourced Disagreement**: Asking AI to settle disputes skips the reasoning of defending a position.
- **Hidden Effort**: Splitting tasks to AI and combining outputs masks unequal effort among members.
- **Pressure to Use**: One student said "we can't not use it" given instructor expectations.
- **Timing Matters**: AI that challenges developed ideas deepens reasoning; AI generating first ideas homogenizes.
- **Stage Preferences**: Students welcome AI for logistics and criteria feedback, not for settling disputes.
- **Unenforceable Rules**: Peer agreements on AI limits collapse under fatigue and deadlines.
- **Institutional Fixes**: Define AI's role per stage, protect learning goals, assess understanding, seek consent.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Shared inputs shrink the diversity that drives debate** — Collaborative learning depends on differences between members' ideas. When everyone draws first ideas from the same source, there is less to challenge and defend.
→ explains: Lost Debate, Timing Matters, Outsourced Disagreement

**T2. The same tool helps or harms depending on its role in the task** — AI that challenges reasoning someone has already developed supports learning; AI that supplies the first answer or settles disputes replaces it.
→ explains: Timing Matters, Stage Preferences

**T3. Informal rules without structure or enforcement erode under pressure** — Peer-agreed limits depend on everyone complying, and fatigue and deadlines make shortcuts win. Designing the constraint into the activity or assessment is what holds.
→ explains: Unenforceable Rules, Institutional Fixes

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the report's central finding about AI in group work?
**A**: AI is reducing the exchange of ideas and debate that make group work collaborative. General AI policies leave students to manage this themselves, so institutions need course-level guidance.

**Q2**: How does the timing of AI use change its effect on collaborative learning?
**A**: Using AI to generate first ideas risks groups converging on similar conclusions, while AI that challenges ideas students have already developed can encourage deeper reasoning. Students welcomed AI before meetings for organising and after tasks for criteria-based feedback.

**Q3**: What should institutions do in response?
**A**: Define AI's role at each stage, protect the parts of a task requiring students' own reasoning, use assessment such as presentations or follow-up questions, and seek consent before using AI on student work.
