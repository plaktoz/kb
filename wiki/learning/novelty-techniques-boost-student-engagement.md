## Summary

Novelty in the classroom must trigger [[Epistemic Curiosity]] — as defined by Professor Susan Engel — to genuinely deepen learning rather than merely entertain. Brain research supports that this curiosity enhances both learning and memory retention. The article presents 15 concrete techniques organized by function, plus the JOLT framework for evaluating whether a novelty strategy will actually work.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Epistemic Curiosity]]** — the drive to resolve knowledge gaps; the quality that separates productive novelty from distraction
- **[[Retrieval Practice]]** — techniques like Unfair Game and Diagram Labeling prime memory by requiring recall before new content arrives
- **[[Cognitive Conflict]]** — opening with a misconception or startling fact forces students to investigate and reconcile their existing mental models
- **[[JOLT Framework]]** — a four-criteria checklist (Jarring, Open-ended, Laugh-inducing, Tense) for evaluating whether a novelty technique will genuinely engage
- **[[Sensory Anchoring]]** — tying a sound, scent, or object to a concept to aid long-term recall
- **[[Todd Finley]]** — educator and author; contributor to Edutopia

## Key Takeaways

- **Epistemic trigger**: Novelty works only when it creates genuine knowledge-gap tension.
- **Unfair Game**: Quiz values revealed post-answer; keeps stakes uncertain and attention high.
- **Diagram Labeling**: Pre-teach recall by blanking labels before instruction begins.
- **Crack the Redaction**: Pairs reconstruct blacked-out key terms — active sense-making.
- **Classroom CSI**: Students act as detectives to find and correct content errors.
- **Slow Reveal**: Progressively disclosing text reshapes interpretation and sustains curiosity.
- **Cognitive Conflict**: Lead with a misconception to prompt active investigation.
- **Micro-Museum**: Four items (quote, image, question, detail) students connect to the lesson.
- **Would You Bet?**: Teams wager on predictions before learning outcomes — raises stakes.
- **JOLT test**: Jarring + Open-ended + Laugh-inducing + Tense = effective engagement hook.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Curiosity Gap]]**: Each technique deliberately creates an incomplete picture — the brain's discomfort with open loops drives students to fill them, which is the mechanism underlying every strategy in the JOLT framework.
- **[[Desirable Difficulty]]**: Techniques like Diagram Labeling and Crack the Redaction introduce productive friction before instruction, making subsequent encoding stronger — consistent with retrieval-practice research on spacing and interleaving.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core condition that separates productive classroom novelty from mere distraction?
**A**: Novelty must trigger epistemic curiosity — the drive to resolve a knowledge gap — rather than simply provide entertainment; without that tension, brain research shows no learning benefit.

**Q2**: What are the four criteria of the JOLT framework, and what does each represent?
**A**: Jarring (surprise or contradiction), Open-ended (unresolved or incomplete), Laugh-inducing (playful or absurd), and Tense (dilemma or unresolved problem) — together they define whether a novelty technique will genuinely hook student attention.

**Q3**: How could a teacher apply the Cognitive Conflict technique at the start of a lesson?
**A**: Open the lesson with a stated misconception or a startling counterintuitive fact, then invite students to investigate and explain why the initial claim was wrong — turning the correction process itself into the learning activity.
