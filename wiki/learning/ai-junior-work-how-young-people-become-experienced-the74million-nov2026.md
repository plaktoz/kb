## Summary
A school-board member with a finance background argues that junior work produces both an output and a future experienced professional, and that AI automating it could erode the pipeline of experienced workers. Early data show fewer junior postings and weaker outcomes for graduates in AI-exposed majors, though neither study proves AI is the cause. She urges schools and employers to plan now for how beginners will get practice and supervision, especially students without professional connections.

## Core Concepts
- [[Entry-Level Hiring]]: junior employees contribute useful work while learning, offsetting the cost of supervising them.
- [[Generative AI]] and junior work: cheaper initial analysis may reduce employers' need for beginners.
- [[Experience Pipeline]]: companies can automate junior tasks and hire experienced staff later, but collectively no one trains them.
- [[Judgment]] built through practice and feedback: a correct calculation can still lead to a poor decision.
- [[Learning Retention]]: a 2025 randomized study of university students using ChatGPT.
- [[Internships]] and [[Apprenticeships]]: need to give meaningful practice and supervision.
- [[Social Capital]] and access: students without professional connections depend on accessible programs.

## Key Takeaways
- **Dual Output**: Junior work yields a deliverable and a more experienced person.
- **Hiring Signal**: A 2026 study found almost 15% fewer junior than senior software postings after late 2022.
- **Graduate Outcomes**: A Census Bureau paper found weaker early employment and earnings in AI-exposed majors.
- **Causation Caveat**: Neither study proves AI caused the changes or measures training investment.
- **Retention Gap**: Students studying with ChatGPT scored 57.5% versus 68.5% on a test 45 days later.
- **Small Study**: That experiment was small and measured retention, not critical thinking.
- **Planning Lag**: Labor-market data trails real job changes; education pathways take years.
- **Equity Risk**: Students without connections lose most if first-job opportunities shrink.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Individually rational cuts can deplete a shared supply** — If each employer skips training beginners and hires experienced staff instead, the pool of experienced workers shrinks for everyone. · *model:* [[Tragedy of the Commons]]
→ explains: Dual Output, Hiring Signal

**T2. Judgment is built through practice and feedback** — Competence beyond correct calculation comes from doing the work and having more experienced people review it. · *model:* [[Deliberate Practice]]
→ explains: Dual Output, Retention Gap, Equity Risk

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the author's core worry about AI and junior work?
**A**: Junior tasks produced both useful output and future experienced workers, so automating them may leave too few people able to gain the experience employers will later need.

**Q2**: What evidence does the author cite, and what are its limits?
**A**: A 2026 study found almost 15% fewer junior than senior software postings, and a Census Bureau paper found weaker outcomes for AI-exposed majors. Neither proves AI caused the changes or measures employer training investment.

**Q3**: What should schools and employers do in response?
**A**: Look further ahead on how entry-level work will change, check that internships and apprenticeships give real practice and supervision, and reconsider how the cost of developing beginners is shared.
