## Summary

[[Jing Liu]] of the University of Maryland released two October studies with opposite results: a K-12 math AI tutor (Goblins) was associated with learning gains, while a university AI course assistant lowered college students' grades and engagement. Liu's point is that [[AI in Education]] is neither "all good" nor "all bad", and that tools must be studied for how students actually respond, including in ways designers never intended.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Goblins]] study**: a math practice site rolled out to 6,000 middle schoolers in Deer Valley, Arizona in 2025-26. Its [[AI Tutor]] offered step-by-step help through text, a virtual whiteboard and speech, and teachers chose which problems to assign.
- **Dosage finding**: students solving at least two problems a week (50+ per year) scored 0.11 standard deviations higher on the year-end test, about three extra months of learning. The study is correlational; [[Accelerate]] hopes to commission a [[Randomized Controlled Trial]].
- **[[Five-Percent Problem]]**: only about 5% of students typically use ed-tech as directed. Half of Goblins students hit the two-problem dose, but only 6% used it for 30+ minutes a week.
- **Engagement drivers**: a talking cartoon avatar of the teacher, support for Spanish and six other languages, and [[Teacher Agency]] over assignments. [[ASSISTments]] showed similar results from teacher-assigned practice with instant feedback.
- **University of Maryland AI assistant study**: in fall 2025, 15 of 30 professors were randomly assigned to add a course-trained AI assistant in [[Canvas]]. About 1,100 students had it and 1,200 did not.
- **Findings**: assistant courses had grades lower by 0.11 points on a four-point scale, less engagement with course materials, and fewer office-hours visits. Effects were stronger for first-generation students. Only 15% ever used it, mostly to get answers.
- **Proposed mechanism**: the assistant's constant presence may have signaled that AI use is encouraged, prompting students to use outside tools like ChatGPT, Claude or Gemini to do assignments. The researchers cannot confirm this.
- **[[Jennifer King Rice]]**: UMD provost, who asked for the study before a 40,000-student rollout and warned AI could exacerbate inequities.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dose Matters**: Two or more Goblins problems weekly linked to about three extra months of learning.
- **Causation Unproven**: Motivated students may simply use the tool more and score higher anyway.
- **Five-Percent Problem**: Most ed-tech students don't use tools as directed; only 6% hit 30 minutes weekly.
- **Engagement Design**: Talking teacher avatar, multilingual support, and teacher-controlled assignments helped reach hard-to-engage students.
- **Maryland Backfire**: AI-assistant students' grades fell 0.11 points; first-generation students were hit hardest.
- **Low Use, Real Harm**: Only 15% used the assistant, yet course-wide outcomes worsened.
- **Unintended Signal**: Presence of the tool may have implied AI use was encouraged.
- **Test Before Rollout**: Maryland tested before campuswide deployment and published the troubling results.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Benefit depends on dose** — A tool can only help the students who use it enough; outcomes track actual usage, not availability. · *model:* [[Dose-Response Relationship]]
→ explains: Dose Matters, Five-Percent Problem

**T2. Usage-outcome links may reflect who chooses to use the tool** — Heavy users may differ (e.g. more motivated) in ways that drive results regardless of the tool, so observational gains can't prove causation. · *model:* [[Selection Bias]]
→ explains: Causation Unproven

**T3. Interventions have effects beyond their intended function** — A tool's mere presence can change behavior in ways its designers never intended, so even a carefully designed tool can backfire. · *model:* [[Unintended Consequences]]
→ explains: Maryland Backfire, Low Use, Real Harm, Unintended Signal, Test Before Rollout

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central contrast between the two studies Jing Liu released?
**A**: A math AI tutor (Goblins) was linked to about three extra months of learning for regular users, while a university AI assistant lowered grades and engagement. Liu says this shows AI in education is neither all good nor all bad.

**Q2**: What evidence showed the Maryland AI assistant backfired, and why is that surprising?
**A**: Students in the 15 AI-assisted classes had grades 0.11 points lower on a four-point scale and engaged less with materials and instructors. Only 15% of them ever used the assistant, so a rarely used tool still seemed to hurt.

**Q3**: How should institutions apply these findings before adopting AI tools?
**A**: Test tools before broad rollout, watch actual usage and dosage, and consider unintended signals such as implying AI use is encouraged. Giving teachers control over assignments and making the tool engaging may help it reach more students.
