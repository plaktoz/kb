## Summary

Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice framework is valid but incomplete for real-world career skills: it was built on domains with clear objectives and objective feedback, neither of which most careers provide. Scott Young argues that practitioners must also solve two underappreciated problems — choosing the right skills to practice (knowing *what* to learn), and demonstrating those skills credibly to others (signaling). The right fix is not to separate practice from performance, but to augment real-world work so it functions more like deliberate practice.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Deliberate Practice]]** — Ericsson's framework of focused, feedback-driven, target-specific practice as the engine of expert performance
- **[[Skill Signaling]]** — the parallel challenge of making hard-to-assess competencies visible via credentials, track records, and social proof
- **[[Career Landscape Mapping]]** — understanding which skills are truly valuable and how they translate to impact before investing practice time
- **[[Performance as Practice]]** — augmenting real-world work (choosing stretch projects, applying constraints) so it yields learning gains like structured practice

## Key Takeaways

- **Talent still matters:** practice is required but who benefits most may depend on fixed traits Ericsson underweighted.
- **What to learn dominates how to learn:** time is scarce; choosing the right skills matters more than optimizing the method.
- **Signaling is half the game:** success is a mix of skill and career assets (credentials, track record, relationships) that signal competence.
- **Real-world skills need real-world context:** project management, legacy codebases, and team collaboration cannot be meaningfully practiced in isolation.
- **Augment performance, don't replace it:** choose stretch projects and apply constraints to make daily work function more like deliberate practice.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Practice What's Valuable, Not What's Measurable** — In domains with fuzzy objectives, the selection of *what* to practice dominates returns from *how* well you practice it; optimizing method before validating direction wastes the largest resource (time). · *model:* [[Deliberate Practice]]
→ explains: career-landscape mapping, skill selection before skill acquisition

Skill and Signal Must Be Co-developed** — Real-world advancement requires not only building competence but accumulating verifiable evidence of it (track record, relationships, credentials); treating these as separate projects leaves practitioners invisible in non-meritocratic contexts. · *model:* [[Deliberate Practice]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Deliberate practice is a sound framework, but applying it to careers requires solving two additional problems Ericsson ignored — choosing the right skills to practice, and demonstrating those skills credibly — and the right practice mode is augmenting real-world work, not isolating practice from performance.

**Q2**: Why does Scott Young say "knowing what to learn" dominates "how to learn it" in career contexts?
**A**: Because practice takes a long time even done efficiently, and skills in career contexts are instruments for impact rather than ends in themselves — investing heavily in the wrong skill yields little return regardless of practice quality.

**Q3**: How should a professional apply deliberate practice principles given that real-world skills require real-world context?
**A**: Rather than inventing artificial drills divorced from work, deliberately choose projects that stretch current abilities and impose constraints that force learning, so that performance itself functions as practice.
