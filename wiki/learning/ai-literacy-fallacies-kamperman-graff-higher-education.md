## Summary

University of Kansas English professor [[Sean Kamperman]] and Ohio State's [[Harvey Graff]] argue that higher education's rush toward "[[AI Literacy]]" programs promotes technology for its own sake. In their view, the term is used without real grounding in what literacy is, and use of the technology is nearly always assumed. Their article, "On 'AI Literacy': The Rhetorical and Historical Fallacies of a Movement and Its Marketing" (in *Change: The Magazine of Higher Learning*), proposes instead an "AI and Literacy" approach. That approach puts reading and writing at the center and draws on the history of earlier writing technologies.

## Core Concepts

- **"AI Literacy" as marketing**: Universities compete to promote AI literacy offerings and integrations of text-generative AI in classrooms, research, and administration. The term stands for learning the basics of fast-changing tech on the premise that AI is inevitable.
- **Narrow definition of AI**: In higher education, "AI" usually means [[Large Language Models]], chatbots, and synthetic text generators. Most programs teach basics, sometimes limitations and failure modes, and include perfunctory ethics, while assuming use.
- **Two historical myths**: The study of literacy history exposes two claims in AI literacy initiatives: (a) using generative AI effectively is a simple, foundational skill, and (b) AI literacy guarantees economic security.
- **Conflation of skills**: AI literacy initiatives blur technology skills with reading and writing, which hides the complex ways the two interact.
- **[[AI and Literacy]] approach**: An alternative framework, informed by the history of past [[Writing Technologies]], that centers reading and writing when teaching about [[Generative AI]].
- **Purpose of assignments**: Now that AI can supposedly write literary analyses or scientific papers, educators must explain why students do these tasks at all.
- **Dependency risk**: Heavy LLM use may bring some social benefits, but it may also erode literacy skills and increase dependence on tech controlled by a few corporations.
- **[[KU AI & Digital Literacy Institute]]**: Kamperman co-directs this professional development program for high school and college humanities instructors, which focuses on ethical and use considerations around AI.

## Key Takeaways

- **Critique**: AI literacy programs lack conceptual grounding in literacy itself.
- **Use is assumed**: most programs treat ethics perfunctorily and take adoption for granted.
- **Myth 1**: effective generative AI use is a simple, foundational skill.
- **Myth 2**: AI literacy is a sure path to economic security.
- **Remedy**: the "AI and Literacy" approach, which centers reading and writing over tool skills.
- **Purpose must be explained**: educators must now justify why students write essays and papers.
- **ChatGPT-native Ph.D. students** will arrive within a couple of years.
- **Expectation ratchet**: if LLM-boosted output is rewarded, the bar rises for everyone.
- **Long-term risk**: eroded literacy skills and dependence on a few powerful corporations.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Chesterton's Fence]]**: Before letting AI replace essays and papers, educators must first state why those assignments exist, which is exactly what Kamperman says they never had to explain before.
- **[[Red Queen Effect]]**: If universities reward sheer output, LLM users raise expectations for everyone, so all must run faster just to keep their place.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Kamperman and Graff's central critique of "AI literacy" in higher education?
**A**: Universities adopt AI literacy frameworks uncritically and for technology's sake, using the term without real grounding in literacy and assuming AI use rather than questioning it.

**Q2**: Which two myths do they say AI literacy initiatives promote?
**A**: First, that using today's generative AI effectively is a foundational skill that is simple to acquire. Second, that AI literacy is a sure path to economic security.

**Q3**: How should an instructor apply the "AI and Literacy" approach when designing coursework?
**A**: The instructor should first state the purpose behind each reading or writing assignment, then ask how AI might enhance or disrupt those specific goals, rather than adding AI by default.
