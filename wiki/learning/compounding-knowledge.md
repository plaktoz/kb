## Summary

Shane Parrish examines how [[Warren Buffett]] achieved extraordinary investment results by deliberately accumulating durable, non-expiring knowledge over 70+ years. Unlike most people who consume information that expires within months, Buffett focused on knowledge that "changes very, very slowly or not at all," allowing it to compound in usefulness over time. The key distinction is between retrieval (looking things up) and retention (having knowledge internalized so it can be used simultaneously under decision pressure).

## Core Concepts

- **[[Compounding Knowledge]]**: Just as money compounds over time, deeply retained knowledge compounds — each new piece builds on prior understanding, forming richer [[Mental Models]] and pattern recognition.
- **[[Warren Buffett]]**: Used as the primary case study; described by biographer [[Alice Schroeder]] as possessing a vast "vertical filing cabinet" in his brain built across 70+ years of reading.
- **Expiring vs. Durable Information**: Most consumed information expires within months; durable information (things that change slowly or not at all) accumulates and compounds in value.
- **[[Pattern Recognition]]**: Independent thinking combined with deep accumulated knowledge enables seeing what others miss — the basis for Buffett's investment edge.
- **Retention vs. Retrieval**: When a decision requires integrating 50 pieces of information simultaneously, internalized knowledge outperforms searchable knowledge; [[Charlie Munger]] and Buffett both prioritize internalization.

## Key Takeaways

- **Durable Knowledge**: Focus on information that won't expire within a year.
- **Mental Filing Cabinet**: Buffett read extensively across industries, including companies he didn't own.
- **Signs of Expiring Info**: Marketed to you; lacks nuance; explains *what*, not *why*.
- **Pattern Matching**: Deep accumulated knowledge enables seeing what others miss.
- **Retention Over Retrieval**: Internalized knowledge beats searchable knowledge under real decision pressure.
- **Cumulative Building**: Each piece of durable knowledge strengthens all prior knowledge.
- **Reflection Test**: Ask — will you care about this reading in a year?

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Compounding]]**: The same exponential-growth logic that makes compound interest powerful applies to knowledge — durable information builds on itself, producing insights unavailable to those who start from scratch each time.
- **[[Circle of Competence]]**: Deliberately focusing on slowly-changing domains is how Buffett maintains a deep, reliable circle of competence rather than spreading attention thin across ephemeral news.
- **[[Inversion]]**: Identifying the signs of expiring information (marketed, no nuance, irrelevant within a year) is the inverted test for what to avoid, clarifying what durable knowledge actually looks like.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument of "Compounding Knowledge"?
**A**: Durable knowledge that changes slowly compounds over time — each new piece builds on prior understanding, producing pattern recognition and decision-making ability unavailable to people who consume only ephemeral information.

**Q2**: What is the key distinction between retrieval and retention, and why does it matter?
**A**: Retrieval means looking information up when needed; retention means having it already internalized. When a decision requires integrating 50 pieces of information simultaneously, internalized knowledge is the only viable option — you cannot pause to search.

**Q3**: How can you apply the expiring-vs-durable framework to your own reading habits?
**A**: Use the self-check questions from the article: Will you care about this in a year? Does it tell you *why*, not just *what*? Is it marketed to you? If the answer flags it as expiring, deprioritize it in favor of books, case studies, or deep-dives on slowly-changing fundamentals.
