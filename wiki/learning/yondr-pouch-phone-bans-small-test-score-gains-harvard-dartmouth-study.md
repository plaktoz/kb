## Summary
A Harvard and Dartmouth study of about 3.3 million students finds strict locked-pouch phone bans raise test scores by roughly 0.01 standard deviations a year, equal to one or two extra weeks of instruction. The effect is small and contested: Stanford's Tom Dee calls the estimates small and sensitive, though the authors argue small annual gains can accumulate, as small annual declines did before the pandemic.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Cell Phone Bans in Schools]]**: Three-quarters of states restrict student phone use; Pennsylvania recently adopted a "bell-to-bell" ban.
- **[[Yondr Pouches]]**: Magnetically locked pouches costing about $25 to $30 per student.
- **[[NWEA MAP Growth]]**: Test data source covering 4,600 schools from 2021-22 to 2025-26.
- **[[Tom Kane]]** (Harvard) and **[[Tom Dee]]** (Stanford): Kane calls bans a low-cost way to add instruction; Dee warns against projecting the small, sometimes insignificant estimates.
- **[[David Figlio]]**: Finds the pouch analysis persuasive but the state-level analysis less so.
- **[[Social Media and Student Outcomes]]**: Proposed next research area, including out-of-school use affecting homework and sleep.

## Key Takeaways
- **Effect Size**: About 0.01 SD yearly in reading and math, equal to 1-2 school weeks.
- **Cost Efficiency**: Cheaper than paying teacher salaries for extra instructional weeks.
- **Sample**: Roughly 3.3 million students at 4,600 schools.
- **Consistency**: Small gains held across school levels and poverty levels.
- **Lowest Achievers**: No extra gain for bottom performers, but fewer large reading losses.
- **State Bans**: Weaker, confounded evidence due to differing pre-ban trajectories.
- **Prior Study**: An earlier national study found "close to zero" test score effects.
- **Reduced Phone Use**: All three U.S. studies found bans cut phone use.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Small consistent effects accumulate over time** — A modest per-period change, positive or negative, compounds into a large cumulative effect; the authors argue this by comparing the gains to the pre-pandemic annual declines. · *model:* [[Compounding]]
→ explains: Effect Size, Cost Efficiency

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Harvard and Dartmouth study find about strict phone bans?
**A**: Locked-pouch bans raised test scores about 0.01 standard deviations a year in reading and math, roughly one to two extra weeks of instruction.

**Q2**: Why do the researchers think such a small effect matters?
**A**: It is about half the annual score decline seen before the pandemic, and small declines added up to a big drop, so small gains could add up too. Tom Dee counters that the estimates are small, sensitive and sometimes insignificant.

**Q3**: How should schools weigh these bans as an intervention?
**A**: Pouches cost about $25 to $30 per student and are a low-cost way to add instruction, but they are not a panacea, and causes like social media and laptops may need separate solutions.
