## Summary

A synthesis of ten evidence-backed learning techniques — anchored in the spacing effect, the testing effect, and cognitive load theory — showing that active, effortful, spaced study consistently beats passive rereading, highlighting, or cramming for long-term retention.

## Core Concepts

- [[Spacing Effect]] — distributing study across multiple sessions rather than cramming, documented by Ebbinghaus (1885) and confirmed as one of learning science's most replicated findings
- [[Testing Effect]] — actively retrieving information from memory (self-quizzing) strengthens retention far more than passive review, per Roediger and Karpicke (2006)
- [[Cognitive Load Theory]] — working memory holds only ~4 items at once, so chunking related information into meaningful units reduces overload (John Sweller)
- [[Interleaving]] — mixing topics or problem types within a study session, rather than drilling one exhaustively, for stronger retention
- [[Feynman Technique]] — explaining a concept in plain language as if teaching a novice, then using the gaps in your own explanation to find what you don't actually understand

## Key Takeaways

- **Forgetting curve**: people forget up to 70% of new information within 24 hours (Ebbinghaus).
- **Test yourself early and often**: quiz within 24 hours of first exposure, then at increasing intervals — tools like Anki/Quizlet automate the scheduling.
- **Deliberate practice**: focused, uncomfortable practice at the edge of ability with immediate feedback (Anders Ericsson) beats longer casual practice.
- **Timed focus blocks**: 25 minutes focused work + 5-minute break (Pomodoro-style), longer break after four blocks.
- **Sleep is part of learning**: memory consolidation happens largely during sleep, not "lost" study time.
- **Sample spaced-review schedule**: day 1 (first exposure) → day 3 (active recall) → day 7 (self-test) → day 14 (flashcards) → day 30 (final review) — each timed just before the memory would fade.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Compounding]]**: Because each spaced review is timed to land right before forgetting would occur, the memory strengthens with each pass — small, consistently-timed reinforcements compound into durable long-term retention, much like reinvested returns compound capital.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim about why cramming and rereading fail?
**A**: They create surface-level familiarity (recognition) rather than genuine retrieval strength (recall), and lack the spacing that lets synaptic connections consolidate — so the material decays within days rather than being retained long-term.

**Q2**: What evidence supports the testing effect over passive review?
**A**: Roediger and Karpicke's 2006 study found students who tested themselves on material retained significantly more of it a week later than students who only reread their notes.

**Q3**: How would you apply the spaced-review schedule to a real study plan?
**A**: Study new material once, then deliberately revisit it via active recall on day 3, self-testing on day 7, flashcards on day 14, and a final review on day 30 — timing each review just before you'd naturally forget, rather than rereading indiscriminately.
