## Summary

A Cambridge research paper, *Shaping AI Policy and Practice in English Language Teaching*, interviewed 15 early-adopter teachers and trainers across 13 countries and found that generative AI should support educators rather than replace them. Teachers reported real gains in lesson preparation, differentiated materials and feedback, but warned that students increasingly turn to AI before attempting tasks, eroding confidence and independent thinking. The report argues that access to tools alone won't improve learning; teacher expertise, sustained professional development, process-focused assessment and critical [[AI Literacy]] must sit at the centre of policy.

## Core Concepts

- **The study**: A [[Cambridge University Press & Assessment|Cambridge]] paper based on interviews conducted in early 2025 with 15 teachers and teacher trainers across 13 countries and six continents, covering primary, secondary and adult [[English Language Teaching]].
- **[[Generative AI in Education]]**: Used for lesson plans, level-adapted materials, quizzes, feedback and marking, and creative resources such as songs, visuals and role plays.
- **Teachers as experts**: [[Gabriel Azpilcueta]] (Adelaide University English Language Centre) stresses that teachers "are still the experts in the classroom" and that their role is presence and encouragement.
- **Objective-first integration**: Effective use starts from the learning objective, not the technology; teachers must judge whether AI genuinely improves an activity or just adds complexity.
- **Differentiation at speed**: German teacher trainer [[Anna Schönbach]] describes adapting materials for different levels shrinking from hours of research to minutes of prompt testing.
- **[[Cognitive Offloading]] risk**: Vietnamese educator Trang describes students losing confidence in their own thinking; AI works best for learners who already have independent study skills.
- **Making AI use visible**: Classroom exercises comparing a student text, a ChatGPT-revised student text and an AI-generated text, and testing AI answers against known facts to catch confident errors.
- **Critical [[AI Literacy]]**: Knowing when to use AI, when not to, and how to evaluate its output, not just how to operate a chatbot.
- **Bias, privacy and inclusion**: Kenyan teacher [[Danson Kimathi]] calls for tools that accommodate local languages such as Swahili; generic AI products weren't built for education.
- **Eight policy recommendations**: Involve teachers and learners in implementation decisions, invest in training, set curriculum expectations for evaluating AI outputs and bias, redesign [[Assessment]] around process, and strengthen privacy, transparency, age-appropriate access and equity.
- **Limitations**: Participants were self-selected early adopters, and the study doesn't measure student outcomes at scale.

## Key Takeaways

- **Support, Not Replace**: Teachers overwhelmingly rejected AI as a substitute for human teachers.
- **Prep Time Savings**: Material adaptation fell from hours of research to minutes of prompting.
- **Objective First**: Start from the learning goal, then ask whether AI improves it.
- **Dependency Risk**: Students use AI before trying tasks, weakening foundational skills.
- **Skills Precede Tools**: AI helps most learners who already study independently.
- **Learning vs. Avoiding**: Distinguishing AI-for-learning from AI-to-avoid-learning must be explicit classroom practice.
- **Critical AI Literacy**: Students must learn when to use AI and how to evaluate it.
- **Process-Based Assessment**: Assess progress, reflection and independent thinking, not just polished products.
- **Teachers as Decision Makers**: Teachers should select and evaluate tools, not merely implement them.
- **Inclusion Gaps**: Generic tools underserve local languages like Swahili and diverse contexts.
- **Access Isn't Enough**: Tool access alone won't deliver better learning without training and policy.
- **Limited Evidence Base**: 15 self-selected early adopters; no large-scale outcome measurement.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Outsourced effort doesn't build the skill** — When a tool supplies answers before the learner attempts the task, the learner's own capability and confidence erode; a tool amplifies learning only for those who already have the underlying skills. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Dependency Risk, Skills Precede Tools, Learning vs. Avoiding, Critical AI Literacy

**T2. When a product becomes cheap to generate, it stops evidencing learning** — If AI can produce polished output effortlessly, the final artifact no longer reliably measures the learning it was meant to reflect, so evaluation must shift to the process. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Process-Based Assessment

**T3. A tool's value depends on the expert judgment applying it** — Generic tools produce benefit only when someone with domain expertise aligns them to the goal and context, so access without that judgment doesn't improve outcomes. · *model:* [[Human-in-the-Loop]]
→ explains: Support, Not Replace, Objective First, Teachers as Decision Makers, Access Isn't Enough, Inclusion Gaps

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the Cambridge study on AI in English language teaching?
**A**: AI should support educators rather than replace them, and effective integration depends on teacher expertise, sustained professional development and classroom-grounded policy, not tool access alone.

**Q2**: What specific risk did teachers report about students' use of AI, and which learners does AI benefit most?
**A**: Students were turning to AI before attempting tasks themselves, losing confidence in their own thinking; AI is most effective for learners who already have independent study skills.

**Q3**: How does the report suggest assessment should change as AI makes polished assignments easy to produce?
**A**: Assessment should recognise the learning process, emphasising evidence of progress, student reflection, feedback literacy and independent thinking alongside conventional measures of achievement.
