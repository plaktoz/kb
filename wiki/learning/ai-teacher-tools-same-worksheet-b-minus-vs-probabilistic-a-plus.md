## Summary
Dan Meyer asked five AI tools (Magic School, Brisk, Diffit, and frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI) to recreate the same fifth-grade fraction worksheet and found each had layout or typesetting flaws that needed fiddling. He argues this explains why Gallup finds only 59% of teachers used AI last year: most teachers want a predictable B- over a probabilistic A+. Several studies he cites find AI tutors and chatbots add little to learning.

## Core Concepts

## Key Takeaways
- **Adoption Gap**: 41% of surveyed teachers used no AI at all.
- **Output Flaws**: Slanted fractions, no work space, and inconsistent fraction layout appeared.
- **Fix Attempt Failed**: Asking Brisk to fix layout produced fractions running off the page.
- **Core Hypothesis**: Teachers prefer a predictable B- to a probabilistic A+.
- **Fiddling Cost**: Prompt iteration feels like a slot machine to techies, stress to teachers.
- **Tutor Evidence**: Eedi's AI tutor matched human tutors short-term, not long-term.
- **Chatbot Harm**: Maryland LMS chatbot lowered final grades; 73.8% of requests sought direct answers.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Predictability beats variance under time scarcity** — People short on time prefer a reliable acceptable output over a tool whose output might be excellent but must be iterated to get there, because iteration costs time and attention they lack.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why does Meyer think teachers are not adopting generative AI more widely?
**A**: He hypothesizes teachers would prefer materials that are a predictable B- to a probabilistic A+. Overworked teachers lack the time to fiddle with LLM output.

**Q2**: What happened when Meyer tested five AI tools on the same worksheet?
**A**: Each produced flawed results, such as slanted fractions, no space for work, or inconsistent fraction order. Asking Brisk to fix the layout made fractions extend beyond the printable page.

**Q3**: What does the supporting research imply for AI in education?
**A**: Eedi's AI tutor did not beat videos and hints on long-term learning, and Maryland's chatbot lowered grades. Added AI does not automatically improve learning.
