## Summary

A new framework called "[[Effort Recalibration]]" proposes that digital media doesn't destroy raw attention capacity but reshapes the brain's cost-benefit valuation of cognitive effort, making low-effort activities like infinite scroll subjectively more rewarding than demanding tasks like studying or deep reading. This explains why lab attention tests show little harm from digital media use, even as real-world sustained focus for [[Exploitation-based Learning]] (mastery of a single topic) appears to erode in favor of constant [[Exploration]] (novelty-seeking).

## Core Concepts

- **[[Effort Recalibration]]** — the brain's internal recalculation of what feels "worth the effort" after repeated exposure to low-friction rewards
- **[[Exploration vs. Exploitation]]** — seeking new information versus sustained engagement with one topic to achieve mastery; digital media shifts the balance toward exploration
- **[[Attention Capacity vs. Willingness]]** — distinguishes whether people *can* concentrate (largely intact) from whether they're *willing* to invest sustained effort (declining)
- **[[Habit Loop Engineering]]** — platforms designed for minimal friction and immediate reward that reinforce low-effort activity preference

## Key Takeaways

- **Lab tests understate real-world harm**: short controlled attention tests miss effects that only appear over long, unconstrained periods.
- **Digital media reshapes valuation, not capacity**: the brain still *can* focus — it becomes less willing to.
- **Learning requires exploitation**: durable knowledge needs prolonged single-task focus, which digital habits can erode.
- **Framework reframes the research question**: from "does digital media harm cognition" to "how does it change willingness to invest effort."
- **Published in Nature Human Behaviour**: by Wisnu Wiradhany, Douglas Parry, and Jaan Aru (2026).
