## Summary
A research-grounded roundup of 21 teaching strategies drawn from [[Rosenshine's Principles of Instruction]], [[John Hattie]]'s Visible Learning meta-analyses, and the UK Education Endowment Foundation, organized around four pillars: direct instruction, effective questioning, feedback, and [[Retrieval Practice]] — finding that the least flashy, most consistent techniques produce the largest effect sizes on student outcomes.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Direct Instruction]]**: Clear modeling and worked examples followed by independent practice, based on Rosenshine's principle of teaching in small, explicit steps before releasing students to work alone.
- **[[Retrieval Practice]]**: Low-stakes quizzing and spaced review that strengthens long-term memory more effectively than passive re-reading, by forcing active recall of prior learning.
- **Effective questioning**: Techniques like cold-calling and deliberate wait time, used to check genuine understanding rather than surface-level recall.
- **[[John Hattie]]**: Education researcher whose Visible Learning meta-analyses rank teaching strategies by effect size on student outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- **Four pillars organize the 21 strategies**: direct instruction, questioning, feedback, and retrieval practice.
- **Feedback works best when specific and process-focused**, targeting the task and the process rather than the student personally.
- **Novice learners benefit most from scaffolding**: breaking complex skills into small steps outperforms discovery-based approaches for students still building foundational knowledge.
- **The biggest effect sizes come from unglamorous, consistent practices** — not novelty-driven teaching methods.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this research roundup?
**A**: The teaching strategies that produce the largest measurable effect on student outcomes are typically the least flashy — consistent formative feedback, retrieval practice, and scaffolded direct instruction — rather than novelty-driven or purely discovery-based methods.

**Q2**: What does "retrieval practice" mean and why does it outperform re-reading?
**A**: It's low-stakes quizzing and spaced review that forces active recall of prior learning; the act of retrieving strengthens the memory trace more than passively re-exposing yourself to the same material.

**Q3**: How should this inform teaching novice versus expert learners?
**A**: Novices, who lack the foundational knowledge to fill gaps on their own, benefit most from explicit, scaffolded direct instruction, while discovery-based approaches tend to work better once foundational knowledge is already in place.
