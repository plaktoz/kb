## Summary

Texas Sports Academy, connected to [[Alpha School]], received $6.5 million through the state's [[Texas Education Freedom Accounts]] program as of October 30, making it the largest school recipient at that point. The article argues that approval for public funds confirms eligibility, not that an AI-centered method improves learning, so families need to see who provides academic help, how learning gains are calculated, and how students who struggle are supported. Schools seeking public money for AI-centered instruction should make their strongest claims checkable.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Texas Education Freedom Accounts]] (TEFA)**: State education savings account program that pays approved educational expenses; requires accreditation, operating history and an annual norm-referenced assessment for grades 3–12.
- **[[Alpha School]]**: Model where students spend about two hours a day on software-based core academics, with adult "guides" focused on mentoring and motivation.
- **[[Learning Gains vs Achievement]]**: High national rankings describe where students start; growth describes change over time, and the two can diverge.
- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: Results limited to students who completed a full year describe those who stayed and leave out students who withdrew or missed testing.
- **[[NWEA]] growth norms**: Typical academic growth varies with age, starting achievement and time between assessments, so "a year's growth" is not a fixed amount.
- **[[Special Education in Private Schools]]**: Children placed in private schools by parents have no individual right to the special education services they would get in public school; some receive services through a services plan.

## Key Takeaways

- **Approval Is Not Efficacy**: TEFA approval establishes eligibility, not that AI use improves learning.
- **$6.5 Million Through October**: Texas Sports Academy led school recipients as of October 30.
- **Two Hours Core Academics**: Alpha's model pairs about two software hours with mentoring and activities.
- **Guides Versus Teachers**: Staffing claims need to show who diagnoses academic problems.
- **Ask Who Helps**: Schools should say who explains material when software fails.
- **Rankings Are Not Growth**: High achievement does not show how much the model caused.
- **Show the Calculation**: Claims need starting and ending scores, test dates, student counts and benchmarks.
- **Count Everyone Enrolled**: Evaluations must explain students who withdrew or missed testing.
- **Support Before Enrollment**: Stalled progress, anxiety and disability services need written plans.
- **Services Plans Replace IEPs**: Private placement does not carry over an existing IEP automatically.
- **Public Data Needs Definitions**: Statewide averages give limited guidance for choosing a school.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. A sample that excludes leavers can overstate results** — Outcomes measured only among students who finished describe a selected group, so any evaluation must account for everyone who entered. · *model:* [[Survivorship Bias]]

**T2. Achievement level and growth measure different things** — A high starting score and a large change over time are separate facts, and neither alone shows how much a method caused the change.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the article say Texas approval of AI-centered schools actually confirms?
**A**: Approval confirms that a school is eligible to receive funds through TEFA, such as meeting accreditation and operating-history requirements, but it does not establish that a particular use of AI improves learning.

**Q2**: Why can a school's reported learning gains be misleading if they cover only students who completed a full year?
**A**: Those results describe students who stayed, and they leave open what happened to students who withdrew, transferred or missed testing, which is a form of [[Survivorship Bias]].

**Q3**: What should a family ask a school that promises personalized AI learning before enrolling a child who struggles?
**A**: Families should ask who makes the academic judgment when software fails, what specialist services exist and how often, what extra charges apply, and how staff decide a child needs more time than the schedule allows.
