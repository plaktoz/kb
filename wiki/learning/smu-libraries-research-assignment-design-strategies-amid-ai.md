## Summary

[[SMU Libraries]] argues that because AI makes it easy to produce something that looks like research, instructors should redesign research assignments around what students must be able to do for themselves rather than around the final paper. Instead of abandoning papers or banning AI outright, assignments should make learning visible, explicitly teach research skills, and give students meaningful reasons to do the work. The post offers four areas of concrete strategies — buy-in, skill-building, process and growth, and accountability — and recommends starting with one change.

## Core Concepts

- **Product vs. understanding**: Finding an answer and knowing why it is credible are different; [[Generative AI]] can supply the former without the latter.
- **Gain buy-in**: Make [[Critical Thinking]] concrete by linking everyday trust decisions to research standards; connect assignments to students' interests; explain the purpose of each requirement; discuss the limits of confident AI-generated summaries.
- **Build skills**: Embed [[Information Literacy]] instruction in class sessions, collaborate with a [[Subject Librarian]], require annotated source PDFs with page-numbered citations, and teach synthesis via a [[Synthesis Matrix]] comparing claims, evidence, methods and conclusions.
- **Process and growth**: Break projects into checkpoints (question, preliminary search, [[Annotated Bibliography]], working argument, outline, draft), build revision into grading, use [[Low-Stakes Assessment]] before major deadlines, schedule peer workshops and instructor conferences, and allow flexible final formats.
- **Accountability**: Specify tasks students must complete independently, include in-class reading and writing, test research knowledge on exams, add firsthand investigation, require a final reflection on a specific AI verification instance, and use version history in [[Google Docs]] or [[Box]].
- **People**: Co-written with [[Megan Heuer]], director of educational initiatives and social sciences librarian; linked to SMU's AI and Research Assignments Cohort.

## Key Takeaways

- **Beyond the Final Paper**: Design for what students can do independently at the end.
- **No Ban Needed**: Neither abandon research papers nor ban AI at every stage.
- **Gain Buy-In**: Students invest more when they understand what research gives them.
- **Explain Requirements**: Don't assume students know why scholarly sources or drafts matter.
- **Teach Skills Explicitly**: Assignments often omit teaching reading, evaluating and synthesizing sources.
- **Annotated Sources**: Require annotated PDFs and page numbers to force direct engagement.
- **Checkpoints**: Stage submissions with deadlines leaving time to use feedback.
- **Revision Grading**: Allow penalty-free revision; grade early work for engagement.
- **Low-Stakes Practice**: Give feedback early so mistakes surface before the final paper.
- **Make Learning Visible**: In-class work, exam questions and reflections reveal actual skill.
- **AI Reflection**: Ask for a specific instance of verifying, revising or rejecting AI output.
- **Start Small**: Begin with one change targeting the most important learning goal.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. A polished product is not evidence of the skill behind it** — When an output can be produced without the underlying reasoning, assessing only the output stops measuring learning, so the reasoning itself must be made observable. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Beyond the Final Paper, Make Learning Visible, AI Reflection, Annotated Sources

**T2. Skill develops through iterative feedback before high-stakes judgment** — Research ability grows by revising questions and arguments in response to feedback, so learning requires feedback cycles early enough to act on. · *model:* [[Feedback Loops]]
→ explains: Checkpoints, Revision Grading, Low-Stakes Practice

**T3. Effort follows perceived purpose** — Learners invest in demanding work when they understand what it builds for them and it connects to questions they care about. · *model:* [[Intrinsic Motivation]]
→ explains: Gain Buy-In, Explain Requirements

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central argument about research assignments in the age of AI?
**A**: Since AI makes it easy to produce something that looks like research, assignments should be redesigned around the skills students must build themselves, making learning visible rather than relying on the final paper or banning AI.

**Q2**: What specific mechanisms does the post suggest for ensuring students engage directly with sources?
**A**: Require annotated PDFs of sources explaining why evidence matters with page-numbered citations, and have students organize sources in a synthesis matrix comparing claims, evidence, methods and conclusions.

**Q3**: How should an instructor assess a student's AI use in a research project?
**A**: Require a final reflection where the student describes a specific instance of verifying, revising, rejecting or building on AI output, and grade the quality of that reflection rather than a generic ethics statement.
