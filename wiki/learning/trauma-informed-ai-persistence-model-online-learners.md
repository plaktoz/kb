## Summary

A peer-reviewed qualitative study of 45 non-traditional online undergraduates at University of Phoenix finds that stress-related regulatory strain increases the cognitive load of course navigation, pacing, and limited instructor presence. Belonging and psychological safety supported engagement. GenAI gave short-term cognitive relief and helped students start tasks, though students worried about dependency and unclear ethical boundaries. The authors propose the Trauma-Informed AI Persistence (TIAIP) Model, which treats student persistence as an outcome of institutional design rather than only of individual motivation or resilience.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Trauma-Informed AI Persistence Model]] (TIAIP)**: A preliminary conceptual model that connects [[Trauma-Informed Pedagogy]], digital course architecture, and guided [[Generative AI]] use.
- **Regulatory strain and [[Cognitive Load]]**: Stresses such as financial pressure, caregiving, and job instability magnify the cognitive demands of unclear instructions, inconsistent course organization, and pacing expectations.
- **[[Psychological Safety]] and [[Sense of Belonging]]**: Both supported engagement. Even a simple instructor acknowledgement made students feel seen.
- **GenAI as a conditional scaffold**: GenAI helps with [[Task Initiation]] and breaking work into steps when students feel overwhelmed. It is not presented as a replacement for human support or student thinking, and it needs clear ethical and instructional boundaries. Related to [[Instructional Scaffolding]].
- **Persistence as an institutional outcome**: [[Student Persistence]] depends partly on how the institution designs and supports learning, not only on learner traits.
- **Method**: [[Colaizzi's Descriptive Phenomenological Method]] applied to open-ended questionnaire responses. Five themes emerged.
- **Authors**: Melinda Kulick, Jessica Sylvester, Chunfu Cheng, Christina Bergren, and Jim Croushore of University of Phoenix's CEITR. Published in *Innovative Higher Education*, July 15, 2026.
- **Related notes**: [[science-of-learning-cognitive-load-gradual-release]], [[adaptive-scaffolding-llm-pedagogical-agents]].

## Key Takeaways

- **Sample**: 45 online working-adult undergraduates, most aged 36–50.
- **Stress raises load**: Regulatory strain magnifies the cognitive cost of poorly organized courses.
- **Human presence matters**: Instructor acknowledgement boosted students' willingness to stay engaged.
- **AI helps start tasks**: GenAI "takes away the panic of a blank page."
- **Risks**: Students raised concerns about AI dependency and unclear ethical boundaries.
- **Design levers**: Clearer course structure, instructor presence, belonging, and guided AI use.
- **Limitations**: One institution, self-reported data, and no assessment of clinical trauma histories.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Cognitive Load Theory]]**: Stress takes up limited working memory, so extraneous load from unclear course design weighs more heavily. Reducing unnecessary demands therefore protects persistence.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of the TIAIP Model?
**A**: Persistence in online higher education is an institutional outcome shaped by trauma-related demands, course design, and guided GenAI use, not just individual motivation or resilience.

**Q2**: How did students describe GenAI's role during periods of overwhelm?
**A**: They saw it as a situational support that reduced the panic of a blank page and broke assignments into manageable steps. They also worried about dependency and unclear ethical boundaries.

**Q3**: What should institutions do to apply the model?
**A**: Design clearer course structures, keep a supportive instructor presence, and attend to belonging and psychological safety. Institutions should also give purposeful guidance on what GenAI can support and where its limits lie.
