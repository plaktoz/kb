## Summary

Adults retain strong neuroplasticity well past middle age, and short, focused sessions with active recall reliably outperform long passive study — the barrier to learning a new skill is method, not brain capacity. A four-stage competence model plus a seven-step practical framework (specific goals, subskill breakdown, deliberate practice, feedback, motivation management) combine with named techniques like the [[20-Hour Rule]] and [[Pareto Principle]] to make skill acquisition tractable within a busy schedule.

## Core Concepts

- [[Four Stages of Competence]] — unconscious incompetence, conscious incompetence (where most learners quit), conscious competence (where the bulk of growth happens), unconscious competence
- [[20-Hour Rule]] — Josh Kaufman's finding that ~20 hours of focused, deliberate practice reaches basic functional competence in most skills, distinct from the 10,000-hour rule for expert mastery
- [[Deliberate Practice]] — Anders Ericsson's concept of focused, feedback-driven, target-specific practice, distinct from mere repetition
- [[Feynman Technique]] — explaining a concept simply, as if to a beginner, to expose gaps in your own understanding
- [[Interleaving]] — mixing different subskills within one practice session rather than mastering one before moving to the next, producing more durable learning

## Key Takeaways

- **Most learners quit at "conscious incompetence"** — the stage where the gap between current and target ability is clearest and most demoralizing
- **20 focused hours, not 10,000**, reaches basic functional competence in most new skills
- **The 80/20 rule applies to subskills**: roughly 20% of a skill's subskills drive 80% of practical results, so identify and prioritize those first
- **Passive consumption (videos, reading) without active application is the most common progress-killer**
- **Spreading attention across too many skills simultaneously guarantees none gets enough repetition to stick** — depth beats breadth

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Feedback Loops]]**: nearly every technique in this framework (deliberate practice, tracking progress, the protégé effect) is a variation on tightening the feedback loop between action and correction — the tighter and faster the loop, the faster the skill compounds, which also explains why [[Speed to Skill]] has become a competitive differentiator at the organizational level.
