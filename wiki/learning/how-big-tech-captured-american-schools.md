## Summary

An adapted excerpt from Natasha Singer's book "Coding Kids: Big Tech's Battle to Remake Public Schools" argues that companies like [[Google]] and [[Microsoft]] have used their money, reach, and influence to shape nearly every step of the US education supply chain, often with little scrutiny compared to how other regulated industries would be treated.

## Core Concepts

- **[[EdTech Capture]]**: The pattern of large technology companies embedding their products and worldview into public education infrastructure — teacher training, curricula, and classroom tools — largely unchallenged.
- **[[Microsoft Copilot]] in schools**: Illustrated via a 2025 Manhattan workshop where ~200 teachers were trained on Copilot for tasks like drafting parent emails and customizing lesson plans, alongside an AI explainer video from Microsoft's Minecraft Education division.
- **Regulatory asymmetry framing**: The article explicitly contrasts ed-tech's free access to schools with how other regulated industries (e.g., an oil major writing environmental science curricula, a pharmaceutical company training biology teachers) would be received.

## Key Takeaways

- **Teacher workshops as entry point**: Microsoft ran hands-on AI-chatbot training for ~200 teachers in summer 2025, including tasks like generating parent emails and customizing lesson plans.
- **Youth-targeted content**: Microsoft's Minecraft Education division produced an animated AI-explainer video featuring a duck mascot, aimed at introducing AI concepts even to children too young to use AI tools directly.
- **Little scrutiny historically**: The book argues schools have embraced tech industry involvement over the last decade "with few questions asked," unlike comparable industry-curriculum entanglements elsewhere.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Regulatory Capture]]**: The article's framing — contrasting ed-tech's unchecked classroom access with how an oil company writing environmental curricula would be treated — is a direct application of regulatory/institutional capture, where an interested industry shapes the very institution meant to oversee outcomes for the public it serves.
