## Summary
Stanford SCALE's November 2026 update grows the causal evidence base on generative AI in education from 20 to 52 papers. Effects depend on design and use: AI helps more when it supports understanding or targeted educator tasks than when it replaces student thinking, though results stay mixed and K-12 evidence remains thin.

## Core Concepts
- [[Generative AI in Education]]: student-facing tools (42 studies) and educator-facing tools (12 studies).
- [[Randomized Controlled Trials]] and quasi-experimental designs, screened with an LLM and then human review against the What Works Clearinghouse standards.
- [[AI for Learning Support]] versus [[AI for Producing Work]]: using AI to understand material versus outsourcing assignments.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: students who outsource tasks perform worse later without AI.
- [[AI Tutoring]]: interactive formats, adaptive sequencing, and guidance versus general-purpose chatbots.
- [[Educator-Facing AI]]: feedback drafts, progress summaries, and teaching-support tools.
- [[Student Motivation and Trust]] and [[AI Well-Being Tools]].
- [[SCALE Initiative]] and the [[AI Hub for Education]] Research Repository.

## Key Takeaways
- **Evidence Growth**: 32 new strong causal papers; 52 total, up from 20 in March 2026.
- **Learning vs. Producing**: AI used to understand material beats AI used to produce work.
- **Offloading Cost**: Outsourced work boosted task performance but hurt later unaided performance in several studies.
- **Experience vs. Outcomes**: Positive student experiences often do not translate into stronger learning outcomes.
- **Design Matters**: Interactive, adaptive, and guided tools show promise; which features matter remains unclear.
- **Not Inherently Better**: Educational AI tools do not consistently outperform general-purpose chatbots.
- **Teacher Use Risk**: One Turkish study found teacher AI use reduced student motivation and confidence.
- **Feedback Gains**: AI drafts roughly doubled TA feedback volume without extra grading time.
- **Remaining Gaps**: Little evidence on equity, long-term gains, or K-12 settings; much evidence is postsecondary preprints.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Reliance that replaces effort erodes learning** — When AI substitutes for the learner's or teacher's own thinking and judgment, performance gains while AI is present do not carry over once it is removed. Support that keeps the human doing the reasoning is more likely to help. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]

**T2. Effects depend on design and use, not the tool category** — The same class of technology produces opposite effects depending on features, task, user expertise, and implementation. Evaluate specific uses in their setting rather than AI access in general.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central conclusion of the November 2026 review?
**A**: The effects of generative AI in education depend on how it is designed and used. It shows more promise when it supports learning and targeted educator tasks than when it replaces student thinking or broad instructional judgment.

**Q2**: What evidence shows that offloading work to AI can hurt learning?
**A**: Brazilian undergraduates finished a presentation faster but did worse on a surprise conceptual test. Canadian beginners who generated full coding solutions understood them poorly. U.S. students who used AI to draft essays lost their gains on a later unaided essay.

**Q3**: How should education leaders act on these findings?
**A**: Evaluate specific AI tools and uses against their intended goals in the local setting. For example, check whether AI-assisted teacher feedback is more useful in the time available and whether students do better on later independent work.
