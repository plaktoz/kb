## Summary

A four-year study of nearly 7,000 survey respondents and 177 tested children found that children who swim before age 5 can be 5-18 months ahead of milestones in literacy, numeracy, and following instructions, regardless of socio-economic background. Researcher [[Robyn Jorgensen]] argues swimming builds transferable skills rather than making children innately smarter. Because cost is the biggest barrier to lessons, she calls for government subsidies.

## Core Concepts

- [[Robyn Jorgensen]]: Emeritus Professor at the University of Canberra, author of *Why Young Children Should Learn to Swim*.
- [[Laurie Lawrence]]: Olympic swim coach whose "industry hunch" that swimmers are more school-ready began the study.
- [[School Readiness]]: literacy, numeracy, and instruction-following measured with the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales and Woodcock-Johnson III.
- [[Transferable Skills]]: counting kicks with an instructor builds number sequencing and one-to-one correspondence.
- [[Educational Equity]]: cost, geography, and income gaps limit access to lessons, and the same groups face the highest drowning risk.
- [[Early Childhood Development]]: cognitive, language, and emotional regulation gains alongside physical fitness.
- [[Government Subsidy]]: proposed investment in early-years swimming for excluded families.

## Key Takeaways

- **Developmental Lead**: Pre-5 swimmers can be 5-18 months ahead on literacy, numeracy, and instruction-following.
- **Study Scale**: Nearly 7,000 survey respondents and 177 individually tested children over four years.
- **Not Innate**: Gains reflect transferable skills and dispositions, not children being inherently smarter.
- **Counting Example**: Counting kicks from one to ten teaches number sequencing and one-to-one correspondence.
- **Income Controlled**: Swimmers outperformed the general population even after accounting for socio-economic background.
- **Access Barrier**: Cost is the biggest barrier; the U.S. and China are hardest for non-affluent families.
- **Double Whammy**: Low-income and rural children are least likely to get lessons and most at risk of drowning.
- **Policy Ask**: Governments already spend billions on attainment gaps and could subsidise early swimming.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's core finding about early swimming?
**A**: Children who learn to swim before age 5 can be 5-18 months ahead in literacy, numeracy, and following instructions, regardless of socio-economic background. Researchers say this reflects transferable skills, not innate intelligence.

**Q2**: How does swimming build skills that matter in school?
**A**: Activities like counting kicks from one to ten with an instructor teach number sequencing and one-to-one correspondence, which have direct classroom value. The study measured outcomes with the Peabody Developmental Motor Scales and Woodcock-Johnson III.

**Q3**: What policy implication does the researcher draw?
**A**: Because cost is the biggest barrier and excluded children are also at higher drowning risk, governments should provide or subsidise early-years swimming. Jorgensen frames it as both morally and financially sensible given existing spending on attainment gaps.
