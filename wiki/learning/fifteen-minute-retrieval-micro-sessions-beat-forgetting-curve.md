## Summary
Learning breaks such as school half-term trigger rapid knowledge decay, but 15 minutes a day of focused active-recall micro-sessions can flatten the [[Forgetting Curve]] while leaving most of the holiday free for rest. Passive habits like rereading and highlighting create familiarity without durable memory, whereas [[Retrieval Practice]] combined with [[Spaced Repetition]] on expanding intervals yields far better long-term retention. Effective review sits in the [[Zone of Proximal Development]] — challenging but achievable — avoiding both comfortable passivity and panicked cramming.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Forgetting Curve]]**: [[Hermann Ebbinghaus]] (1885) mapped exponential decay of unreinforced knowledge — ~50% lost after 1 hour, ~70% after 24 hours, 75–90% after a week.
- **Study-technique utility**: Dunlosky et al. (2013) rated highlighting, rereading and summarising low-utility, and practice testing ([[Active Recall]]) and distributed practice high-utility.
- **[[Testing Effect]] evidence**: Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found 80% one-week retention after retrieval vs 34% after rereading; Karpicke & Blunt (2011) found 50% more correct answers than [[Concept Mapping]].
- **[[Fluency Illusion]]**: Rereading builds visual familiarity — recognising a topic on a page — without retrievable memory traces.
- **[[Cognitive Load Theory]]**: Brief sessions keep working memory within capacity and prevent fatigue; marathon cramming overwhelms it and induces procrastination.
- **Expanding intervals**: Dobson (2012) showed expanding retrieval gaps beat uniform intervals; the article proposes a 1-3-7-14 day schedule.
- **[[Growth Mindset]]**: Drawing on [[Carol Dweck]], forgotten items are diagnostic information, not proof of limited ability.
- **Three learning zones**: Sofa Zone (comfort, no growth), Zone of Proximal Development (challenging but achievable), Panic Zone (overwhelming cramming).
- **Related notes**: [[why-microlearning-sticks-forgetting-curve]], [[retrieval-practice-beats-mind-mapping]], [[science-of-spaced-repetition]].
- **Techniques**: Retrieval sprints, blank-page brain dumps, morning micro-routines, gradual sleep adjustment, low-stakes starter quizzes, and [[Scaffolding (Learning Theory)|scaffolding]] with worked examples.

## Key Takeaways
- **Ebbinghaus Decay**: ~70% of unreinforced knowledge lost within 24 hours.
- **Rereading Illusion**: Rereading builds familiarity, not retrievable memory.
- **Retrieval vs Rereading**: 80% vs 34% retention after one week.
- **Retrieval vs Concept Mapping**: Retrieval gave 50% more correct comprehension answers.
- **Micro-Sessions**: 10–15 minutes needs minimal activation energy and avoids fatigue.
- **Expanding Intervals**: Review at days 1, 3, 7 and 14.
- **Against Cramming**: Marathon sessions overwhelm working memory and induce procrastination.
- **Learning Zones**: Aim for the ZPD, between Sofa Zone and Panic Zone.
- **Errors as Feedback**: Gaps in a brain dump target the next session.
- **Morning Routine**: A fixed early review block frees the rest of the day.
- **Low-Stakes Re-Entry**: Ungraded 5-minute starter quizzes reactivate prior learning.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Unreinforced memory decays rapidly, and each retrieval slows the next decay** — Without review, newly learned information fades exponentially; every deliberate recall makes the trace more resilient, so widening gaps between reviews stays efficient. · *model:* [[Forgetting Curve]]
→ explains: Ebbinghaus Decay, Expanding Intervals, Micro-Sessions

**T2. Effortful retrieval builds durable memory; passive re-exposure builds only familiarity** — Pulling information from memory consolidates it, while recognising material on a page feels like knowing it without making it retrievable. · *model:* [[Testing Effect]]
→ explains: Rereading Illusion, Retrieval vs Rereading, Retrieval vs Concept Mapping, Errors as Feedback, Low-Stakes Re-Entry

**T3. Learning grows under challenge that is achievable, and stalls when load is too low or too high** — Too little challenge produces no growth, while overload swamps working memory, so effective practice is brief and pitched just beyond current ability. · *model:* [[Zone of Proximal Development]]
→ explains: Learning Zones, Against Cramming, Micro-Sessions

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about study breaks?
**A**: Holiday rest and retention need not compete: 15 minutes a day of focused active-recall micro-sessions can flatten the forgetting curve and protect progress while leaving most of the day free.

**Q2**: What evidence shows retrieval beats passive review?
**A**: Roediger & Karpicke (2006) found 80% retention after one week for students who retrieved material versus 34% for those who only reread it, and Karpicke & Blunt (2011) found retrieval gave 50% more correct answers than concept mapping.

**Q3**: How would you structure review over a two-week break?
**A**: Use a fixed 10–15 minute morning block of retrieval sprints or blank-page brain dumps, scheduled on expanding intervals (days 1, 3, 7, 14), treating forgotten items as targets for the next session.
