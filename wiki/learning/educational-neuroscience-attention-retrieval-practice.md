## Summary

Educational neuroscience translates brain research on attention, working memory, and emotion into concrete classroom techniques — active learning raised student participation by 30% in one semester at the University of Edinburgh. The field's core principles (attention first, retrieval practice, spaced feedback, emotional safety) converge on [[Stanislas Dehaene]]'s four pillars of learning, and directly debunk the popular "learning styles" theory.

## Core Concepts

- [[Working Memory]] — the brain's limited-capacity short-term holding space; overloading it with information leaves students confused, especially under stress or fatigue
- [[Retrieval Practice]] — recalling information from memory rather than re-reading it, which strengthens memory each time it's used
- [[Dehaene's Four Pillars of Learning]] — attention, active engagement, feedback, and consolidation, identified by cognitive neuroscientist Stanislas Dehaene
- [[Learning Styles Myth]] — the theory that students should be taught according to a fixed visual/auditory/kinesthetic style; not supported by brain research, since all students benefit from varied teaching channels
- [[New Science of Learning]] — the 2009 framework (Meltzoff, Kuhl, Movellan, Sejnowski) combining cognitive neuroscience, developmental psychology, and education research

## Key Takeaways

- **Active learning improved participation by 30%** in one semester at the University of Edinburgh
- **If the brain doesn't notice something, it can't remember it** — attention must be captured before working memory or retention matter at all
- **Retrieval practice (quick questions, short writing, peer explanation) beats passive re-reading** for durable memory
- **The brain consolidates information during breaks, not during constant teaching** — short pauses are functionally part of the lesson
- **"Learning styles" is a debunked myth**: rich, multi-channel lessons (words, images, examples, practice) serve every learner better than style-matched teaching

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Emotional Safety as a Prerequisite]]**: the finding that fear of being wrong measurably closes off the brain to new information reframes classroom (or any learning) design — psychological safety isn't a soft add-on to instruction, it's a hard precondition for the attention and retrieval mechanisms underneath all the other techniques to function.
