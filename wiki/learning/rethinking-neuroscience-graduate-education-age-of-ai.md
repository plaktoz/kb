## Summary
Neuroscientists will keep steering AI for the foreseeable future, so graduate training should shift from mechanical facts and routines toward "formulate-and-evaluate" problem-solving. Used as a Socratic tutor, AI can scale active learning, but used to hand over answers it stalls the intuition that scientists need to judge AI output. Six commentators agree that skills worth keeping are those needed to spot when a tool is wrong or that underpin deeper understanding.

## Core Concepts
- [[Active Learning]]: repeated problem-solving with rapid feedback, which AI can personalize at scale.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: handing thinking to books, search engines, or [[Large Language Models]]; the central risk for student learning.
- **Formulate-and-evaluate cycles**: posing a problem precisely and judging the plausibility of an AI-assisted solution.
- [[AI Tutoring]] and Socratic tutors: [[Learning by Teaching]] agents that guide students without giving answers.
- [[Intuition]]: the internal model of the world that grows from doing the work and lets scientists choose questions and check agents.
- [[Terence Tao]]'s helicopter analogy, [[Hodgkin-Huxley Model]] teaching example, and Jeff Lichtman's "Genuinely Hard Problems in Science" course.
- [[Ernst Strüngmann Forum]] workshop on cognitive offloading and the NYC schools' reversed LLM ban.

## Key Takeaways
- **Steering AI**: Scientists, not AI, set questions and judge output for the foreseeable future.
- **Lower-level skills**: Like long division, routine skills build common sense even if rarely used later.
- **Formulate-and-Evaluate**: Replace factual detail and mechanical calculation with exercises that pose and check AI solutions.
- **Observation Trap**: Watching AI solve problems does not build lasting reasoning skills.
- **AI as Tutor**: Students using AI as tutor outscored those using it for answers in a randomized study.
- **Visible Failure Test**: Automation can replace a skill only if its failures are obvious.
- **Intuition Stagnation**: When agents do the work, learning-on-the-job intuition stops growing.
- **Faculty Role**: Lecture less; facilitate problem-solving alongside AI tutors.
- **Open Uncertainty**: Experts admit it is unclear which training experiences prove essential.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Skills can be dropped only if failures are visible** — A tool can safely replace a human skill only when its errors surface on their own; otherwise a human who understands the underlying analysis must remain to catch them. Skills that are foundations for deeper understanding stay, often in reduced form.
→ explains: Visible Failure Test, Lower-level skills

**T2. Learning requires the learner to do the cognitive work** — Understanding and error-detection grow from attempt, confusion, and correction, so offloading the work stalls intuition even when output improves. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Observation Trap, Intuition Stagnation, AI as Tutor

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core argument about training future neuroscientists in the age of AI?
**A**: Because scientists will keep steering AI, training should emphasize formulate-and-evaluate problem-solving and retain the foundations needed to judge AI output. AI should be used as a tutor that supports active learning, not as a supplier of answers.

**Q2**: Why does the article say automation can replace some skills, such as machine code, but not others?
**A**: Compilers stop and report when they fail, so the skill is safely automated. An AI that confidently reports a spurious neural finding needs a human who understands the analysis to notice the error.

**Q3**: How should faculty change their teaching given AI tutors?
**A**: They should lecture less and act as facilitators who introduce, motivate, and guide problem-solving lessons alongside AI tutors, and could use a probing AI to sharpen their own lectures.
