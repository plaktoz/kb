## Summary

A joint Office for Students and Advance HE study, drawing on over 300 students and staff from over 120 institutions, finds that AI's effect on learning depends on how it is used. It warns that AI undermines the validity of unsupervised assessment and that AI literacy training and institutional strategy remain uneven. It recommends secure assessment design, subject-specific AI literacy and coordinated leadership.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Office for Students]] and [[Advance HE]]**: Authors of the June–September 2026 research programme on English higher education.
- **[[Generative AI]] in teaching and study**: Students use it to explain concepts, summarise documents, generate practice questions and revise.
- **[[Cognitive Offloading]]**: Replacing reading, research or problem-solving with AI bypasses the productive struggle needed to build knowledge.
- **[[Assessment Validity]]**: Unsupervised assessments can be completed to a high standard with AI without showing the intended skills.
- **[[AI Detection Tools]]**: Evidence suggests they do not reliably identify AI use.
- **[[AI Literacy]]**: Knowledge and capabilities for effective, responsible AI use, for students and staff.
- **Institutional strategy and governance**: AI leads, champions and governance groups, set against staff-led experimentation.
- **[[Regulatory Environment]]**: Participants favoured clarity on existing registration conditions over new AI-specific ones.

## Key Takeaways

- **Use Determines Outcome**: Critical, careful AI use can improve learning; offloading weakens it.
- **Accessibility Benefits**: Possible pedagogical gains for neurodivergent, disabled and non-native English-speaking students.
- **Assessment Risk**: Unsupervised assessments can be passed with AI without demonstrating knowledge.
- **Detection Unreliable**: AI detection tools do not consistently identify AI use.
- **Unclear Policies**: Students are often unsure when AI use is permitted in assessed work.
- **Secure Assessment Works**: Closed-book exams, presentations and controlled settings give the most confidence in student achievement.
- **Uneven Literacy Training**: Many students received limited or no AI training; staff training often has low take-up.
- **Efficiency Not Yet Realised**: Most staff felt AI had not delivered significant efficiency gains.
- **Adoption Barriers**: Low trust in outputs, limited capability, immature data infrastructure and slow approvals.
- **Coordinated Leadership**: Scaled benefits need senior-led coordination alongside local experimentation.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the report's central finding about AI and learning?
**A**: Whether AI improves or weakens learning depends on how it is used. Critical use can reveal gaps in understanding, while using it to replace reading, research or problem-solving circumvents the productive struggle needed to learn.

**Q2**: Why does AI threaten assessment validity, and why can policies not simply solve it?
**A**: In unsupervised assessments students can reach a high standard with AI without demonstrating the intended skills. AI detection tools are unreliable, so it is usually not possible to establish whether a student followed the institution's AI policy.

**Q3**: How should institutions respond on assessment?
**A**: They should favour secure approaches that prevent AI use, such as closed-book exams, presentations and controlled environments, because these give high confidence in students' own knowledge. Innovative assessments that integrate AI can work but need careful design to stay valid and reliable.
