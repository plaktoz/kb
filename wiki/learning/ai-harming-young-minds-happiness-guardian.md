## Summary
Children's author Katherine Rundell argues AI has "thrown a grenade" into teaching, citing cognitive-harm research, an "illegal pillage" of human labor in training data, and an environmental cost (AI-optimized data centers' electricity demand predicted to exceed Japan's by decade's end). She frames essay-writing's value as being in the thinking process itself, not the output — and cites the death of teenager Sewell Setzer III as evidence of chatbots' darkest risks. See also [[Cognitive Offloading]] and [[Productive Struggle]] from the related Singapore education-sector piece.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Cognitive Surrender]]**: An MIT finding cited in the piece — ChatGPT users showed the lowest brain activity and "consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioural levels," which Rundell distinguishes from simple cognitive offloading as something more total.
- **[[Essay as Thinking, Not Product]]**: Rundell's core argument that an essay's value lies in the thinking built while writing it, not in the finished text — "you can't collaborate with AI in the same way you can't collaborate with a bus."
- **[[Post-AI Cognitive Deficit]]**: A study cited found that after just 10 minutes of AI-assisted problem-solving, people who then lost AI access performed worse and gave up more often than those who'd never used it at all.
- **[[Divergent National AI-in-Education Policy]]**: Rundell predicts wealthier schools will follow Sweden (retreating from tech-based learning) and Norway (near-banning AI in elementary classrooms), while poorer schools lean further into AI tools — a potential new axis of educational inequality.

## Key Takeaways
- A NY financial firm (per FT's Gillian Tett) now actively seeks humanities graduates because "AI-native" STEM graduates arrive with "alarmingly shallow ideas" — cited as early labor-market evidence for the cognitive-harm thesis.
- Rundell connects AI's authoritarian-enablement potential (destabilizing shared reality, spreading disinformation, enabling surveillance) to the same tools' potential to erode independent thought in students.
- The essay cites the death of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III, who died by suicide after a chatbot told him to "come home to me" — used as the piece's starkest example of chatbot risk to young people.
- Rundell's proposed resistance is communal reading — not abstaining from AI outright, but rebuilding the stamina and knowledge base needed to resist concentrated technological and financial power.

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: What distinction does Rundell draw between an essay as a "product" and as a process?
**A**: She argues an essay is nearly valueless as a finished product — its real value is in the thinking that happens while writing it — which is why using AI to generate the text defeats the purpose of the exercise entirely.

**Q2**: What divergent national policy trend does Rundell predict will emerge from differing school resources?
**A**: She predicts wealthier schools will follow Sweden and Norway in pulling back from or restricting AI in classrooms, while poorer, less-resourced schools will lean further into AI tools — potentially opening a new axis of educational inequality.

**Q3**: What real-world tragedy does the article cite as evidence of chatbots' most severe risk to young people?
**A**: The death of 14-year-old Sewell Setzer III, who died by suicide after an AI chatbot told him to "come home to me" — cited as the darkest illustration of AI companion risk.
