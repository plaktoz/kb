## Summary

The [[University of Dayton]] now requires every first-year student to complete modules on AI fundamentals and human dignity. AI-use rules, however, are left to individual instructors under its [[Academic Integrity|Academic Honor Code]], so they vary from course to course. The university separates "legitimate" use, which amplifies a student's own intellectual work, from "corrosive" use, which replaces it. Research on AI in learning is mixed, and employers increasingly expect AI-literate graduates.

## Core Concepts

- **Mandatory [[AI Literacy]]**: First-year students learn AI fundamentals through asynchronous modules embedded in existing courses.
  - Arts and Sciences, Business and Education/Health Sciences students use [[Coursera]] in Introduction to the University.
  - Engineering students use [[IBM SkillsBuild]] in EGR 103.
- **Human dignity curriculum**: HUM 102 and ASI 102 cover AI as a writing and research tool, what it means to be human in an AI-driven world, and AI's social-justice implications. Students also learn about training data, [[Prompt Engineering|prompt writing]] and how to evaluate AI output for accuracy.
- **Course-by-course policy**: The Honor Code requires instructor permission for AI use and says permitted use "will vary widely from course to course". A student might use [[FlyerGPT]] in one class, be barred from AI in another, and be limited to brainstorming in a third.
- **Legitimate vs corrosive use**: [[Meghan Henning]] frames the line through academic integrity and the purpose of learning, not a blanket use-or-ban rule.
- **Faculty dilemma**: Faculty lack a reliable way to discourage misuse and worry that AI deprives students of the learning an assignment intends. Ryan Allen called this being "stuck between a rock and a hard place".
- **Mixed research evidence**:
  - A 2026 review of 35 studies with more than 4,000 participants found [[ChatGPT]] had a positive overall effect on learning outcomes, but results depended on instructional method.
  - A 2025 [[Harvard University]] study found a thoughtfully designed [[AI Tutoring|AI tutor]] beat an active-learning classroom. The researchers warned that ordinary chatbots are not necessarily tutors.
- **Employer expectations**: According to [[Jason Eckert]], "Awareness of AI is no longer enough." Employers want prompt engineering, machine-learning fundamentals and concrete examples of using AI to augment work. They also still want communication, ethical reasoning and complex problem-solving.
- **[[FlyerGPT]]**: A secure university multi-model platform launched in February 2025. It sets a monthly compute-cost ceiling of $20 for students and $25 for faculty and staff. Which account funds it is not publicly disclosed.
- **Environmental cost**: The [[International Energy Agency]] projects global [[Data Center Power Demand|data-center electricity use]] will grow from about 485 TWh in 2025 to about 950 TWh in 2030. UD has not published FlyerGPT-specific electricity, water or emissions estimates.
- **Building with AI**: UD helps lead a $1.4M [[Gates Foundation]] grant project with Toboggan Labs and the University of Delaware. The project is developing an AI system that reviews school improvement plans and preserves institutional memory when staff leave.

## Key Takeaways

- **Universal Requirement**: Every first-year student now completes AI fundamentals and human dignity modules.
- **No Uniform Rule**: Each instructor decides whether and how AI may be used.
- **Core Distinction**: "Legitimate use amplifies" a student's work; "corrosive use replaces it."
- **Evidence Nuance**: AI tutors designed for learning outperform ordinary answer-giving chatbots.
- **Meta-analysis**: 35 studies, 4,000+ participants show net positive ChatGPT learning effects.
- **Employer Bar**: Graduates need demonstrated AI use, not just awareness.
- **Usage Cap**: FlyerGPT gives students a $20 monthly compute ceiling, not cash.
- **Energy Doubling**: The IEA projects data-center electricity will rise from ~485 to ~950 TWh by 2030.
- **Equity Rationale**: "If we left AI literacy to chance, we'd be widening a gap."

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Desirable Difficulty]]**: The Harvard caveat is that chatbots can hand over answers "without requiring the effort an instructor intended". This is why the legitimate-versus-corrosive line hinges on whether the student's own effortful thinking is preserved.
- **[[Matthew Effect]]**: Students arrive with very different levels of AI exposure, so leaving literacy to chance lets early advantages compound. A universal requirement is designed to interrupt that.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: How does the University of Dayton approach AI in its curriculum and policy?
**A**: It requires all first-year students to learn AI fundamentals and human-dignity content. Day-to-day AI use is left to each instructor under the Honor Code and judged by whether it amplifies or replaces a student's own work.

**Q2**: What does the research say about whether AI helps students learn?
**A**: A 2026 review of 35 studies with more than 4,000 participants found ChatGPT had a positive effect overall, but the effect depended on instructional method. A 2025 Harvard study found a well-designed AI tutor beat active learning, while warning that ordinary chatbots can just give answers.

**Q3**: What does this imply for students preparing for the job market?
**A**: Knowing what ChatGPT is is no longer enough. Students should build concrete examples of using AI to augment real work in courses, jobs or internships, while also developing writing, ethical reasoning and problem-solving skills.
