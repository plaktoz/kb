## Summary

K-12 districts are rushing out AI workshops, but a workshop alone leaves teachers unsure how to use AI in tomorrow's lesson. Drawing on a Digital Promise higher-education study, the authors argue teachers learn AI best when it is tied to a real problem and worked through with trusted colleagues over time. Districts should support the coaches who support teachers and measure judgment, not usage.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Professional Development]]**: workshops give common language and policy, but not situated judgment.
- **[[Instructional Judgment]]**: deciding when AI supports learning and when another approach serves students better.
- **"I-Need-It" Moment**: relevance grows when a new capability meets a real, immediate need.
- **[[Instructional Coaches]] and teacher leaders**: trusted colleagues who know local context and can help interpret AI use.
- **[[AI Governance]]**: coaches should help shape tool evaluation and may raise concerns about tools.
- **[[Digital Promise]]** research-practice partnership with D2L in higher education.

## Key Takeaways

- **Workshop Limits**: A workshop builds tool understanding but not in-the-moment lesson-planning decisions.
- **Relevance Trigger**: Educators valued AI most when linked to a problem they were already solving.
- **Trusted Colleagues**: Judgment develops through practice and conversation with people who know the instructional context.
- **Context Dependence**: A strategy for ninth-grade history may fail in third grade.
- **Share Failures**: Open discussion of AI uses that failed teaches when AI does not belong.
- **Measure Judgment**: More AI use does not show teachers feel prepared to decide.
- **Support the Supporters**: Coaches need time, peers and authority, not just an "AI champion" title.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Judgment is built through practice in context** — Decision-making skill develops through repeated application to real problems, with feedback from people who understand the setting; information alone does not produce it.
→ explains: Workshop Limits, Relevance Trigger, Trusted Colleagues, Context Dependence, Share Failures

**T2. Usage is a poor proxy for preparedness** — Counting activity (AI use) does not show whether the underlying capability (sound decisions) exists, so measures must target the capability itself. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why does the article say one-off AI training will not prepare teachers?
**A**: Teachers face context-dependent decisions while planning lessons and assessments that a workshop cannot anticipate. Judgment develops over time through practice, conversation and reflection with trusted people.

**Q2**: What did the Digital Promise study find that shapes the argument?
**A**: Educators found AI support most useful when someone connected it to a problem they were already trying to solve, such as an instructional designer showing AI for weekly course overviews, which one participant called an "I-need-it" moment.

**Q3**: How should districts apply this when designing AI professional learning?
**A**: Involve coaches and teacher leaders in governance and tool evaluation, give them protected time to experiment, make help available during planning, and measure whether teachers can evaluate AI critically rather than how much they use it.
