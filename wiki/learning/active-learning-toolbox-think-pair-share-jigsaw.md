## Summary

An instructor argues that effective active learning does not require redesigning a course: simple strategies like Think-Pair-Share, Jigsaw, and micro-lectures shift focus from covering material to what students can do with it. She advises adding one strategy to one lesson and building from the student response.

## Core Concepts

- [[Active Learning]]: students do something with information rather than only receive it.
- [[Think-Pair-Share]]: individual thinking, then partner discussion, then group sharing.
- [[Jigsaw Method]]: students become experts on one portion and teach peers.
- [[Micro-Lectures]]: short content segments with engagement checkpoints.
- [[Learning by Teaching]]: responsibility for helping others understand deepens learning.
- [[Michele Poulos]]: author and instructor sharing practice.

## Key Takeaways

- **Start Small**: Add one active learning strategy to one lesson, then build.
- **Think-Pair-Share**: Processing time before speaking increases participation.
- **Jigsaw**: Teaching peers builds accountability, collaboration, and deeper learning.
- **Micro-Lectures**: Break content into segments with discussion or application checkpoints.
- **Mindset Shift**: Ask "What can students do with this?" not "How do I cover this?"
- **Broad Fit**: Works across disciplines, modalities, and class sizes.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Learners retain and understand more when they act on information** — Engagement and retention rise when students discuss, solve, teach, or reflect instead of passively receiving content.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about active learning?
**A**: The most effective active learning strategies are often the simplest, and small instructional changes create the greatest impact on engagement and success. It works because students do something with information rather than just receive it.

**Q2**: How do Think-Pair-Share and Jigsaw work, and why are they effective?
**A**: Think-Pair-Share has students think alone, discuss with a partner, then share with the group, giving processing time that boosts participation. In Jigsaw, students become experts on one portion and teach peers, which encourages accountability and collaboration.

**Q3**: How should an instructor begin adopting active learning?
**A**: Do not overhaul the course overnight; pick one lesson and add one strategy such as a discussion prompt or reflective exercise. Observe student response and build from there.
