## Summary

Early studies show AI tutors lift performance during use, but gains often fail to transfer to independent work unless the tool is purpose-built to preserve student reasoning. Sustained usage is a persistent problem, and the most promising results come from combining AI with human tutors. The field still lacks evidence that AI tutoring matches the impacts of traditional high-impact tutoring.

## Core Concepts

- **[[High-Impact Tutoring]]** — consistent, targeted tutor support; effective but costly (about $1,200 to $2,000+ per student) and hard to scale
- **[[AI Tutoring]]** — AI that instructs students directly or helps human tutors respond to student needs
- **[[Purpose-Built AI Tutors]]** — pedagogical guardrails, curriculum alignment, structured prompting; e.g. [[Medly]] and [[Goblins]]
- **[[Khanmigo]]** — Khan Academy's AI chatbot; rarely used by students in a Tennessee two-year study
- **[[Tutor CoPilot]]** — Stanford tool giving human tutors real-time response suggestions
- **[[Eedi]] and [[Google DeepMind]]** — study of human-supervised AI tutoring
- **[[Laurence Holt]]** — coined the "5% problem" of edtech usage
- **[[Stanford SCALE Initiative]]** — research on tutoring and generative AI in K-12
- **[[Randomized Controlled Trials in Education]]** — the evidence standard the article calls for

## Key Takeaways

- **Transfer gap**: Unconstrained GPT tutor users scored 17% worse than controls once AI was removed.
- **Guardrails**: Hint-based tutor avoided harm but did not improve independent performance.
- **Medly**: 929 students gained 0.33 SD on new, unpracticed science questions.
- **Goblins**: Gains scaled with use, 0.11 SD to 0.31 SD, but correlational only.
- **5% problem**: Nearly half of elementary students assigned an AI literacy platform never used it.
- **Khanmigo usage**: 96% tried it, yet used it in only 17% of mistake exercises.
- **Tutor CoPilot**: Four-point mastery gain, nine points for lower-rated tutors.
- **Human-supervised AI**: Tutors approved over three-quarters of AI messages with little editing.
- **Hybrid caveat**: i-Ready plus human tutors showed no difference on state assessments.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Performance with assistance is not learning** — Support that makes the task easier can raise in-the-moment performance without building independent ability; tools that preserve reasoning and error-handling are likelier to produce durable learning. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]
→ explains: Transfer gap, Guardrails, Medly

**T2. Availability is not usage** — A tool only helps to the extent students actually engage with it at the intended dosage; supplemental tools are often barely used.
→ explains: 5% problem, Khanmigo usage, Goblins

**T3. Dividing work between humans and AI can scale individual support** — Humans supply motivation, quality control, and judgment while AI scales delivery, preserving much of tutoring's impact at lower cost.
→ explains: Tutor CoPilot, Human-supervised AI, Hybrid caveat

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central finding about AI tutoring?
**A**: AI can improve performance during use, but gains often do not carry over to independent work, and the field still lacks compelling evidence that AI tutoring matches traditional high-impact tutoring. Purpose-built and human-AI hybrid designs look most promising.

**Q2**: What did the RCT of nearly 1,000 high-school students show about unconstrained versus guardrailed GPT tutors?
**A**: The unconstrained tutor group performed far better during practice but scored 17 percent worse than controls when the tool was removed. The guardrailed version (hints instead of answers) avoided the harm but only matched controls without AI.

**Q3**: How should designers and schools act on the usage and hybrid findings?
**A**: Don't assume access means productive use; build in engagement support and preserve student reasoning. Consider human-in-the-loop models such as Tutor CoPilot or human-supervised AI messages to scale tutoring.
