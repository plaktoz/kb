## Summary

Annoto reads two October 2026 studies together: a Maryland randomized trial where an AI course assistant lowered grades, and an Arizona study where the Goblins AI math tutor was linked to about three extra months of learning. Its thesis is that deployment design, not model quality, decides whether [[AI Tutor]]s help or hurt.

## Core Concepts

- **[[University of Maryland]] trial**: a provost-requested [[Randomized Controlled Trial]] in fall 2025; 15 of 30 professors added an AI course assistant (about 1,100 students). Students averaged 0.11 GPA points lower, engaged less and attended fewer office hours; first-generation students fared worse.
- **Usage pattern**: only about 15% used the assistant, mostly to request answers; its presence may have signaled AI use was welcome.
- **[[Goblins]] study**: about 6,000 Arizona middle schoolers; consistent practice (2+ problems a week) linked to a 0.11 standard deviation gain, roughly three months of learning. It was not randomized.
- **Integration vs. access**: Goblins was embedded in teacher-assigned practice; Maryland's assistant was an open door.
- **Tutoring vs. answering**: step-by-step scaffolding versus handing over solutions, tied to [[Productive Struggle]].
- **[[Teacher in the Loop]]**: teachers assigned, paced and saw results in Arizona.
- **Evaluation practice**: pilot with a control group, instrument engagement signals, and check who the tool serves worst.

## Key Takeaways

- **Maryland Result**: Assistant users averaged 0.11 GPA points lower than controls.
- **Arizona Result**: Consistent Goblins practice linked to about three extra months of learning.
- **Causation Caveat**: Arizona study was not randomized; motivated students may self-select.
- **Design Over Model**: Deployment design, not model quality, decides outcomes.
- **Scaffold, Don't Solve**: Tutors should guide reasoning rather than vend answers.
- **Embed It**: Tools inside assigned work beat ambient access.
- **Check Subgroups**: Averages hide harm to first-generation students.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Learning lives in effortful struggle** — Tools that remove the effortful middle of problem solving remove the learning with it; tools that scaffold the steps preserve it. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]
→ explains: Scaffold, Don't Solve, Maryland Result

**T2. Observational usage-outcome links may reflect who chooses to use the tool** — Motivated students may both practice more and score higher, so non-randomized gains don't prove the tool works. · *model:* [[Selection Bias]]
→ explains: Causation Caveat, Arizona Result

**T3. Averages hide who is hurt** — An aggregate effect conceals subgroup differences, so outcomes must be examined by group. 
→ explains: Check Subgroups, Maryland Result

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about AI tutors and grades?
**A**: AI tutors are neither a failure nor the future; they are a design problem. Deployment design, not model quality, decides whether they help or hurt.

**Q2**: What evidence separated the Maryland and Arizona outcomes?
**A**: Maryland's open-access assistant was used by only about 15% of students, mostly for answers, and grades fell 0.11 points. Goblins was integrated into teacher-assigned practice with step-by-step help and linked to about three extra months of learning.

**Q3**: How should an institution pilot an AI tutor?
**A**: Use a control group, choose tutors that scaffold and are grounded in course content, embed them in assigned work, keep teachers in the loop, and track engagement and subgroup effects.
