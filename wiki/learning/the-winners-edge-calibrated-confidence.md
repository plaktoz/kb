## Summary

Using Magnus Carlsen's chess career as a case study, this piece argues that over-estimating risk is as costly as under-estimating it — paralysis from imaginary dangers prevents peak performance. Carlsen's breakthrough came when he deliberately shifted toward optimism around age 16-17, accepting short-term losses until his playing strength caught up to his confidence. The key distinction is between phony confidence (ignorance-based) and expert confidence (skill-based), which provides accurate risk assessment rather than false comfort.

## Core Concepts

- [[Magnus Carlsen]]'s optimism shift as a performance case study
- [[Asymmetric Risk]]: over-estimating risk as costly as under-estimating — both are calibration failures
- [[Phony Confidence]] vs. [[Expert Confidence]]: ignorance hides risks; skill-based confidence assesses them clearly
- [[Risk Tolerance]] calibrated by stakes: boldness in low-stakes, caution in high-stakes

## Key Takeaways

- **Carlsen's insight**: Players who "see dangers that are not there" underperform despite deep knowledge
- **Deliberate optimism**: Accepting short-term losses to build longer-term confidence/performance alignment
- **Asymmetry principle**: "Over-estimating risk can be as costly as under-estimating it"
- **Phony vs. expert confidence**: When uncertain which you have, assume phony and add safety margin
- **Stakes-based rule**: Low-stakes → boldness; high-stakes → caution
