## Summary

[[Data Colada]] investigators analyzed the original data files from [[Dan Ariely]] and [[Klaus Wertenbroch]]'s 2002 *Psychological Science* study on procrastination and deadlines — one of the most cited papers in behavioral economics — and found four distinct statistical anomalies consistent with data fabrication or severe tampering. The original study's headline finding, that evenly spaced external deadlines dramatically improved proofreading performance, produced effect sizes implausibly larger than the gender gap in height. Ariely declined to provide alternative data and publicly acknowledged "serious anomalies," while Wertenbroch confirmed he never had access to the data and requested retraction.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Replication Crisis]]** — the broader failure of behavioral science findings to hold up under independent replication
- **[[Dan Ariely]]** — co-author under scrutiny; sole custodian of the original data files
- **[[Data Colada]]** — forensic research integrity blog (Uri Simonsohn, Joe Simmons, Leif Nelson) that conducted the analysis
- **[[Procrastination]]** — the original study topic: whether self-set vs. imposed deadlines affect task completion
- **[[Precommitment]]** — the behavioral mechanism the study aimed to test: locking in future behavior to overcome present-bias
- **[[Statistical Forensics]]** — methods for detecting fabricated or manipulated data (effect size benchmarking, duplicate detection, inter-item correlation, rounding patterns)
- **[[Cohen's d]]** — effect size metric; original study reported d = 2.5, far exceeding plausible human behavioral effects
- **[[COPE Guidelines]]** — Committee on Publication Ethics framework; invoked by *Psychological Science* editor in response

## Key Takeaways

- **Implausible effect size**: Evenly spaced deadlines produced *d* = 2.5 — larger than the gender difference in height (*d* ≈ 1.8).
- **Duplicate observations**: 18 of 20 Last Day participants had an exact "corrections twin" with IDs exactly 10 apart; no duplicates in other conditions.
- **Missing correlations**: Variables that should correlate strongly (subjective ratings, performance across tasks, time spent) showed near-zero correlations in original data but +.63 to +.95 in the replication.
- **No rounding in time estimates**: Only 11.7% of original participants gave round-number time estimates vs. 85% in the replication — consistent with chance, not human behavior.
- **Ariely's response**: Denied the files are the actual data but provided no alternative; acknowledged "serious anomalies."
- **Wertenbroch's response**: Confirmed he never had data access and requested retraction.
- **Original citation count**: 2,100+ Google Scholar citations; widely assigned in economics and psychology courses.
- **Part 2 forthcoming**: Study 1 from the same paper will be analyzed with similar conclusions expected.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Occam's Razor]]**: When four independent anomalies each individually strain credibility, the simplest explanation that accounts for all simultaneously — fabrication — is more parsimonious than four separate benign coincidences.
- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: The study's effect size (d = 2.5) was accepted partly because reviewers focused on the p-value rather than benchmarking the magnitude against known human behavioral effects — a failure to apply base rate thinking to effect plausibility.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What was the central finding of Ariely & Wertenbroch's 2002 Study 2, and why did Data Colada conclude the data were fraudulent?
**A**: The study claimed evenly spaced deadlines dramatically outperformed self-set or single-deadline conditions in proofreading performance. Data Colada identified four simultaneous anomalies — implausible effect sizes, duplicate observations, missing within-condition correlations, and absence of rounding in time estimates — that collectively cannot be explained benignly.

**Q2**: What specific statistical pattern in the Last Day condition most clearly suggests data fabrication?
**A**: 18 of 20 participants had exact "correction twins" — another participant with identical counts across all three tasks — with ID numbers precisely 10 apart (e.g., S1/S11, S7/S17). This pattern does not appear in the other two conditions and is inconsistent with genuine independent data.

**Q3**: What practical implication does this finding have for using deadline research in productivity or behavioral economics courses?
**A**: The evenly-spaced-deadlines effect — one of the most widely cited demonstrations of precommitment in applied behavioral science — may not be reliable, requiring instructors and practitioners to revisit curricula built around it and to weight the replication's null results more heavily.
