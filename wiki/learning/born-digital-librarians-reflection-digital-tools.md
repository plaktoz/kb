## Summary

A librarian argues that popular classroom "engagement" tools like Miro, Quizlet, and Kahoot are largely digitizations of existing analog activities rather than genuinely new capabilities, and that they reduce the tactility and embodied dynamism of learning while requiring students to hand over personal data to for-profit companies.

## Core Concepts

- **Digitization vs. innovation**: Miro functions as a virtual blackboard, Quizlet digitizes flashcards, and Kahoot replaces pen-and-paper trivia — the tools mirror old activities rather than expand what's pedagogically possible.
- **[[Disembodiment of Learning]]**: The argument that removing physical, tactile elements from classroom activities (handwriting flashcards, physically moving to a blackboard) subtly degrades the learning experience even as it adds convenience.
- **Access barrier**: Expecting all students to be fluent with laptops and digital tools is itself a barrier for those with less technological experience, illustrated by a mature student who found the tech itself got in the way of learning the material.
- **Data privacy**: Many of these tools are privately owned, for-profit platforms that require students to give up personal information such as email addresses or browsing data.

## Key Takeaways

- **Tactility matters**: The friction of physically distributing papers or getting up to add ideas to a real blackboard reintroduces a dynamism that can pull students out of a "digital funk."
- **Not all digitization is progress**: Popular tools largely replicate pre-existing classroom activities rather than enabling new pedagogy.
- **Equity concern**: Assuming universal laptop/tool fluency disadvantages students with less prior technology exposure.
- **Privacy trade-off**: Adoption of these tools often comes bundled with data collection by for-profit vendors.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this reflection?
**A**: Many popular digital classroom tools are digitizations of existing analog activities rather than genuine pedagogical innovations, and this shift toward digital-only formats reduces the tactile, embodied quality of learning.

**Q2**: What examples does the author use to make this case?
**A**: Miro as a virtual blackboard, Quizlet as digital flashcards (replacing the learning value of handwriting them), and Kahoot as digital pen-and-paper trivia.

**Q3**: What practical alternative does the author point to?
**A**: Reintroducing physical, tactile classroom activities — like distributing paper or having students physically add ideas to a blackboard — as a way to counter the disembodiment and "digital funk" that heavy tool reliance can create.
