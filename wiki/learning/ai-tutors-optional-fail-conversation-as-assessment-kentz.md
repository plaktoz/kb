## Summary
A two-year study of Khan Academy's Khanmigo found students rarely used an optional AI tutor, and rarely about math. The author argues AI-supported thinking must be required and assessed, with the chat transcript itself graded by a teacher. Role-play bots that students know will be judged raise effort and make thinking visible.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Khanmigo]]**: Khan Academy's AI tutor, studied in 18 Tennessee middle schools in "One Click Away" by [[Philip Oreopoulos]] and Nina Low.
- **[[Sal Khan]]**: Khan Academy CEO, who said the study showed "how much motivation matters"; Khanmigo is now built into practice problems.
- **[[Optional vs. Required AI Use]]**: Optional help lets students pick the chatbot that gives answers instead of the tutor that asks questions.
- **[[Conversation as Assessment]]**: Students submit full AI transcripts, graded against a co-built rubric on questioning, critical response and ownership.
- **[[Friction Bots]]** and **[[AI Friction Labs]]**: Role-play AI characters (a confused classmate, a skeptical ecologist) that force students to explain and defend ideas; the author's company, with beta classrooms reporting early feedback only.
- **[[Productive Struggle]]**: Difficulty that forces real understanding; some students found it "interrogating" or repetitive.
- **[[Teacher-in-the-Loop Evaluation]]**: AI drafts a first-pass evaluation and flags teachable moments; the teacher decides.

## Key Takeaways
- **Low Uptake**: 96% tried Khanmigo once; median student used it a third of days.
- **Modest Gains**: Outcomes matched Khan Academy practice without AI; conversations were rarely about math.
- **Optional Fails**: Given a choice, students pick the answer-giving chatbot over the questioning tutor.
- **Requirement Insufficient**: Mandated use fixes participation, not effort; passive interaction persists.
- **Pilot Results**: 18 of 21 students changed their AI approach; confident users rose from 2 to 11.
- **Retakes**: Five calculus students retook the role-play, averaging about 34 points higher.
- **Visible Thinking**: Transcripts show how students reasoned, got stuck and recovered.
- **Caveats**: Small pilot, no control group, mostly self-reported data, and no design is cheat-proof.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Effort rises when the work will be judged** — People invest more in an activity when they know what they must show and that someone whose judgment they respect will evaluate it. The same AI tool drew little effort as an optional tutor but real effort when the conversation was assessed.
→ explains: Optional Fails, Requirement Insufficient, Retakes

**T2. Defaults beat intentions** — When an easier path (an answer-giving chatbot) sits beside a harder one (a questioning tutor), most people take the easier one unless the design removes the choice.
→ explains: Low Uptake, Optional Fails

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central argument about getting students to think with AI?
**A**: Optional AI tutors go unused or are bypassed for answer-giving chatbots, and mere requirement is not enough. The conversation itself should be the assessment, judged by a teacher, so students know their thinking will be evaluated.

**Q2**: What did the Tennessee "One Click Away" study find?
**A**: 96% of students tried Khanmigo, but the median used it on only a third of available days, rarely discussed math, and gained only modestly, about what Khan Academy practice without AI gives.

**Q3**: How could a teacher apply this in a classroom?
**A**: Assign periodic, well-prepared AI role-plays (such as a confused classmate), have the AI draft an evaluation of the transcript against a rubric, and have the teacher review the flagged moments and give final feedback.
