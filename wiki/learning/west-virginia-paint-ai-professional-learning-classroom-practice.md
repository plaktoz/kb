## Summary

West Virginia is building a statewide AI professional learning system, PAINT, that moves teachers from foundational AI knowledge to classroom application rather than ending at a one-off workshop. It combines a train-the-trainer model, a three-level progression, hands-on resource creation during training, and a "screen value" lens on when AI actually improves learning. The model is still being implemented, so long-term outcomes are unknown.

## Core Concepts

- **[[PAINT (Pedagogical AI for New Teaching)]]**: West Virginia's hands-on AI professional learning initiative where teachers explore tools, collaborate, and build resources for their own classrooms.
- **[[Cliff Sullivan]]**: AI coordinator at the West Virginia Department of Education; shaped the approach from earlier work with the West Virginia STEAM Technical Assistance Center.
- **[[Train-the-Trainer Model]]**: Regional specialists support local leaders called AI Mountaineers, who deliver training in their counties and schools across 55 counties.
- **[[Pioneer, Trailblazer, Ridge Climber]]**: Three learning levels moving from foundational AI literacy, to applying AI to real challenges, to solving school- or system-wide problems collaboratively.
- **[[SREB AI Professional Learning Framework]]**: Four phases of awareness, exploration, application, and iteration; the Southern Regional Education Board's framework aligned with the state plan.
- **[[Screen Value]]**: Judging whether AI makes learning more meaningful, including uses in planning where students never touch a device.
- **[[Generative AI in Education]]**: Teachers use AI agents to challenge students, for example one arguing the British won the American Revolution.

## Key Takeaways

- **Needs First**: Design professional learning around teachers' real challenges, not a predetermined set of AI activities.
- **Usable Output**: Training should produce a resource teachers can use, not just something they remember.
- **Scale**: A train-the-trainer model builds local expertise across 55 counties instead of relying on one team.
- **Flexibility**: Teach transferable AI skills rather than one product; set shared expectations on literacy, privacy, and responsible use.
- **Progression**: Pioneer, Trailblazer, and Ridge Climber levels build skills over time instead of one session.
- **Practice in Training**: Teachers adapt an existing lesson with AI during the session and leave with a resource.
- **Screen Value**: Ask whether AI deepens thinking, even when students are not using a device.
- **Challenger AI**: An AI agent arguing a false claim forces students to defend conclusions with primary-source evidence.
- **Start Small**: Begin with approved tools and one recurring challenge, then build capacity collaboratively.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Capability transfers through practice on one's own work** — Knowing how a tool works does not equal knowing when and how to use it; skill is built by applying ideas to real tasks with time to practice, collaborate, and refine. · *model:* [[Experiential Learning]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument of West Virginia's approach to AI professional learning?
**A**: Training must move teachers from awareness to classroom application, since knowing how a tool works is not the same as knowing when and how to use it. Professional learning should produce something teachers can actually use.

**Q2**: How does West Virginia scale AI training across 55 counties, and what are its learning levels?
**A**: Regional specialists support local AI Mountaineers who deliver training in their counties, backed by asynchronous learning. The levels are Pioneer (foundational literacy), Trailblazer (applying AI to real challenges), and Ridge Climber (solving larger problems together).

**Q3**: How can a teacher use AI to challenge students rather than give them answers?
**A**: A teacher can build an AI agent that argues the British won the American Revolution, so students must use primary-source evidence to refute it. The agent keeps questioning, so students must examine documents, cite evidence, and defend their conclusions.
