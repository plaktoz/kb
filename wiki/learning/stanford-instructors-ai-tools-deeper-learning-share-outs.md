## Summary

Stanford's Center for Teaching and Learning runs "Teaching with AI" community share-outs where instructors present how they use AI in courses. Four examples show AI tools built to keep students thinking rather than hand over answers, from a Socratic chemistry tutor to a classical Japanese reading bot. Instructors stress close human curation and iterating on tools with student feedback.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Center for Teaching and Learning]] (CTL)**: Hosts the share-out series under [[AI Meets Education at Stanford (AIMES)]]; 23 instructors have presented over two academic years.
- **[[Amit Kaushal]]'s Question Tool**: AI groups, ranks, and summarizes real-time student questions like a moderator, keeping the lecturer-learner relationship intact.
- **[[Waheeda Khalfan]]'s Foundations Navigator**: An AI pipeline maps prerequisite knowledge across biology courses, feeding self-diagnostic assessments and preparation guides.
- **[[STEPS]] (Science Tutor for Effective Problem-Solving)**: [[Shima Salehi]]'s AI tutor asks guiding questions instead of giving answers, protecting [[Productive Struggle]].
- **[[BungoBot]]**: [[Ariel Stilerman]]'s [[Socratic Method]] tutor for classical Japanese, joined by Hiki (reverse dictionary) and Sata (conversation partner).
- **Human Curation**: Experts review and refine AI output to keep materials accurate.

## Key Takeaways

- **Community Learning**: 23 instructors across six schools presented over two academic years.
- **Question Triage**: Kaushal's tool surfaces common confusion and allows anonymous participation.
- **Faster Course Design**: Khalfan's prerequisite analysis went from months to weeks.
- **No Answers Given**: STEPS and BungoBot respond with questions, not solutions.
- **Shifting Attitudes**: Students first resented STEPS, then valued that it slowed them down.
- **Accelerated Access**: BungoBot sped up grammar so students could work on manuscripts sooner.
- **Co-Design**: Students supplied weekly feedback; by week seven the tool was "theirs."
- **Humans Stay Essential**: "We will never automate the process entirely."

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Effortful struggle drives learning, so tools should guide rather than answer** — Learning depends on the learner doing the cognitive work; removing the struggle removes the gain, even if learners initially prefer the shortcut. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What design approach unites the AI tools described in the Stanford share-outs?
**A**: They push students toward answers themselves rather than supplying them, as STEPS and BungoBot do with guiding questions. Instructors also keep humans closely involved in curating the content.

**Q2**: How did students react to STEPS, and why does it matter?
**A**: At first they were frustrated and asked why it did not give the answer. Over time they appreciated that it taught the problem-solving process, with one saying nothing else makes you slow down and understand what you are learning.

**Q3**: How can an instructor apply AI to course design without losing quality?
**A**: Use AI to speed up analysis, as Khalfan's team cut months of work to weeks, but review and refine the output personally. Khalfan stresses that close human curation is needed and the process will never be fully automated.
