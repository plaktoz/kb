## Summary
Filament Games' October 2026 roundup finds that nearly everyone in education has tried AI, yet most students and about half of educators report never receiving formal training. Classroom AI pilots are running ahead of a thin research base, prompting districts like Madison to take a staff-first approach while UNESCO consults on global governance and colleges rapidly expand AI degrees. The unifying argument is that purpose-built tools with clear learning goals are easier to measure and show more promise than general-purpose chatbots.

## Core Concepts
- **[[AI Training Gap]]**: [[Microsoft]]'s third annual AI in Education Special Report (3,000+ respondents) shows a perception gap — leaders believe guidance and training are in place while students and teachers report otherwise.
- **[[Purpose-Built Learning Tools]]**: A [[Stanford]] review of 800+ papers finds AI-for-learning research very limited, but carefully designed tools show more promise than [[General-Purpose Chatbots]].
- **Classroom AI Pilots**: [[NPR]]'s [[Lee V. Gaines]] reports schools experimenting ahead of evidence, with states and federal government leaving decisions to districts; a Dayton, Ohio pilot tested a [[Social-Emotional Learning]] chatbot.
- **[[Staff-First AI Rollout]]**: [[Madison Metropolitan School District]] keeps ChatGPT and Gemini blocked on student Chromebooks this year after ~two years of staff training, planning student rollout next school year — echoing restrictions by New York City and Los Angeles.
- **[[UNESCO]] AI Governance Consultation**: Ahead of [[Digital Learning Week]] 2026, UNESCO released a discussion paper on education as a common good plus six background papers (e.g., right age for AI, when a tool is safe to scale); comments due October 15, 2026.
- **[[Distributed AI Literacy]]**: [[Ashish Fernando]] (EDMO) describes colleges embedding AI across disciplines; the [[University of Florida]] offers 200+ AI courses across all 16 colleges.
- **[[Game-Based Learning]]**: Filament argues interactive simulations and learning games provide low-stakes, repeated active practice with measurable outcomes.

## Key Takeaways
- **Near-universal use**: About 9 in 10 respondents have already used AI for school.
- **Guidance perception gap**: 4 in 5 leaders call guidance clear; half of students/teachers disagree.
- **Training shortfall**: 77% of students and 53% of educators report no formal AI training.
- **Training cadence**: Respondents want AI training on a monthly or quarterly rhythm.
- **Evidence lag**: Stanford review of 800+ papers finds limited research on AI helping students.
- **Goal-specific tools**: Tools built for a specific learning goal are far easier to evaluate.
- **Staff-first path**: Madison trains staff first, delays student AI access until next year.
- **AI degree boom**: AI master's programs grew from 116 (2022) to 310 (2025).
- **Degree breadth**: 304 U.S. colleges and universities now offer AI or AI-related degrees.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Measurability requires predefined success** — An intervention can only be evaluated when what counts as success is defined before use begins; open-ended tools with no fixed goal resist evaluation.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding running through the October 2026 roundup?
**A**: AI use in education is nearly universal but training, guidance, and research lag behind, and purpose-built tools with clear learning goals show more promise and are easier to measure than general-purpose chatbots.

**Q2**: What data reveals the AI training gap in Microsoft's report?
**A**: While 7 in 10 leaders believe at least half their students and teachers have been trained, 77% of students and 53% of educators report no formal AI training at all.

**Q3**: How might a district apply these findings when introducing AI?
**A**: Follow Madison's staff-first model — train educators before granting student access — and favor tools built around specific learning goals so outcomes can be measured.
