## Summary
Research-backed study techniques — active recall, spaced repetition, time boxing (Pomodoro), and digital-distraction control — consistently outperform passive rereading and cramming for exam retention, with UNCF framing these as complements to (not replacements for) HBCU mentorship and academic-support structures.

## Core Concepts

- [[Active Recall]] — closing the book and retrieving information from memory (flashcards, practice questions, verbal summaries) rather than rereading; per Harvard's Derek Bok Center, this measurably improves exam performance because effortful retrieval strengthens memory more than passive review
- [[Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve]] — the basis for spaced repetition: people forget new information rapidly unless they revisit it at increasing intervals, which reinforces material just as it starts to fade
- [[Time Boxing / Pomodoro Technique]] — 25 minutes of focused study followed by a 5-minute break, repeated with a longer break every four cycles; addresses the fact that concentration typically fades after 30–40 minutes of continuous work
- [[Digital Distraction Fragmentation]] — per APA research cited, switching between studying and checking messages/social media fragments attention and increases both stress and cognitive load

## Key Takeaways

- **Familiarity bias**: rereading/highlighting feels productive but is measurably less effective than active recall, because recognizing information isn't the same as being able to retrieve it.
- **Spaced repetition mechanism**: reviewing material at increasing intervals (not once) counters the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve and strengthens long-term retention with less last-minute stress.
- **Environmental design matters**: silencing notifications, using website blockers, or physically removing the phone reduces the need to resist distraction moment-to-moment.
- **Framing**: success isn't about total hours studied — it's about using time intentionally, paired (per UNCF) with mentorship and academic-support resources available through HBCUs.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Evidence-based study techniques — active recall, spaced repetition, time boxing, and distraction control — produce better exam retention than marathon cramming or passive rereading.

**Q2**: Why does active recall outperform rereading, according to the research cited?
**A**: Retrieving information from memory (flashcards, practice questions) forces effortful reconstruction that strengthens the memory trace, while rereading only builds familiarity — which feels like learning but doesn't transfer to retrieval on a test.

**Q3**: What specific mechanism does spaced repetition exploit, and how should it be applied?
**A**: It counters the Ebbinghaus forgetting curve — the rapid, predictable decay of new information — by reviewing material at increasing intervals over days or weeks, reinforcing it right as it starts to fade rather than cramming it once.
