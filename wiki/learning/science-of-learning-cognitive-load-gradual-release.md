## Summary

[[Nathaniel Swain]]'s book *Harnessing the Science of Learning* translates cognitive-load theory, retrieval practice, spaced repetition, and knowledge-rich curriculum design into practical strategies for educators specifically, rather than learners. Its "[[I Do, We Do, You Do]]" gradual-release model — explicit demonstration, guided practice, then independent practice — maps directly onto cognitive science of skill acquisition, and the article argues professional development for teachers rarely follows this same evidence-based structure.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Cognitive Load Theory]]** — working memory is small; visual clutter or extraneous demands during new learning impede it, especially for young learners
- **[[I Do, We Do, You Do]]** — gradual release of responsibility: explicit demonstration, guided collective practice, then independent practice
- **[[Distraction-Free Instruction]]** — "no bells and whistles" principle for reducing extraneous cognitive load during early instruction
- **[[Technology Evaluation Questions]]** — does a tool reduce or add cognitive load, support or substitute explicit instruction, extend or duplicate a teacher's role

## Key Takeaways

- **Working memory is the bottleneck**: minimizing extraneous load matters most for young learners and students with learning differences.
- **Skipping "I do, we do" causes confusion**: independent practice without demonstration can build confidence in wrong approaches.
- **PD rarely follows its own advice**: professional development is often delivered as information transfer without gradual release.
- **Three PD design principles**: small increments, modeling before practice, connecting to existing knowledge.
- **Technology isn't inherently good or bad**: its value depends on whether it reduces cognitive load and supports explicit instruction.
