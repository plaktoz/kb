## Summary

Researchers at Rowan University and the University of Kentucky, backed by a $489,316 NSF grant, are testing whether commercially available generative AI can validly score teachers' "professional noticing" of student math thinking and give feedback faster than humans. AI scores will be compared with expert raters across 150 teachers. The project is early, with teacher data collection starting in spring 2027, and some educators doubt the feedback speed and value for teachers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Professional Noticing]]**: Attending to, interpreting, and deciding how to respond to students' thinking.
- **Generative AI scoring**: Tools such as [[Claude]] and [[ChatGPT]] score teacher responses using rubrics, decision trees, and refined prompts.
- **Expert-rater validation**: AI scores are compared with expert scores under validated protocols to test reliability across assessment types.
- **Feedback latency**: Current observation-and-debrief evaluation is slow; the goal is feedback within 24 hours.
- **Human element**: Critics worry about losing body language and feelings; supporters note noticing is qualitative and AI already codes qualitative data.
- **Standardized packages**: Scoring materials bundled for other educators to reuse.

## Key Takeaways

- **Study scale**: 100 in-service and 50 preservice elementary teachers; grant runs to July 2028.
- **Method**: Three noticing assessments, two video-based and one writing-based, scored by experts and AI.
- **Real-world choice**: Commercial AI tools used deliberately to mirror how educators would use them.
- **Feedback goal**: Reports within 24 hours noting strengths and growth areas; technology not yet there.
- **Skeptic view**: One teacher says typical feedback takes 48-72 hours and the approach may suit administrators better.
- **Classroom use**: AI could flag students whose correct answers had contradictory explanations.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What question is the research team trying to answer?
**A**: Whether commercially available generative AI can validly and reliably measure teachers' professional noticing of children's math thinking and give useful feedback faster than humans.

**Q2**: How will the team test whether AI scoring is accurate?
**A**: Expert raters and AI tools will score the same teacher responses on three assessments, and the two sets of scores will be compared. The team will also survey and interview teachers about the feedback.

**Q3**: What are the practical doubts or hopes about the tool?
**A**: A skeptical teacher says 24-hour feedback is implausible and the tool may suit administrators better. A coach hopes AI can quickly surface patterns, such as correct answers with contradictory explanations, so teachers can follow up.
