## Summary

The S.M.A.R.T. Learning Framework synthesizes findings from leading learning-science books — including Peak, Deep Work, Make It Stick, Spark, and Why We Sleep — into five meta-learning skills that can roughly halve the time required to acquire any new skill or body of knowledge. The acronym stands for State optimization, Mistake optimization, Active recall, Reduction, and Turning off. Together the five skills address the full learning cycle: preparation, practice design, retrieval, compression, and consolidation.

## Core Concepts

- [[SMART Learning Framework]] — five meta-learning skills: State optimization, Mistake optimization, Active recall, Reduction, Turning off
- [[State Optimization]] — using pre-session exercise (15–30 min moderate intensity) to flood the brain with dopamine, noradrenaline, and [[BDNF]] for peak focus
- [[Mistake Optimization]] — the 85/15 rule: succeed 85% of the time, fail 15%, to stay in the neurological sweet spot for growth without stress shutdown
- [[Active Recall]] — struggling to retrieve information from memory (vs. passive review) builds stronger neural pathways; effort equals retention
- [[Reduction (Learning)]] — compressing learning into one acronym, sentence, page, or visual; complexity fades, simplicity endures
- [[Sleep and Memory Consolidation]] — sleep acts as the brain's renovation phase; high performers average 1.5 hours more sleep than typical adults
- [[Anders Ericsson]] — deliberate practice researcher; his work in "Peak" underpins the intensity and sleep principles
- [[Cal Newport]] — author of "Deep Work"; attention residue elimination is a key state-optimization technique
- [[Anterior Cingulate Cortex]] — fires an internal alarm on mistakes, lighting up memory circuits and making subsequent focus more sticky

## Key Takeaways

- **State optimization**: 15–30 min of moderate exercise before studying maximizes neurochemical readiness.
- **Eliminate attention residue**: write down lingering thoughts before studying to clear mental clutter.
- **85/15 rule**: design drills so you fail roughly 15% of the time to stay in the growth zone.
- **Let mistakes sting**: resist looking up the answer immediately; the discomfort drives deeper encoding.
- **Periodic pause**: every 5–10 min, stop and recall aloud what you just learned.
- **End-of-session recap**: explain the session's learning as if teaching; note 3–5 keywords.
- **Start-of-session recall**: spend 2 min retrieving last session's keywords from memory before new material.
- **Reduce to one thing**: compress every subject into an acronym, sentence, page, or visual.
- **Prioritize sleep**: napping or full-night rest consolidates learning and refreshes the hippocampus for the next session.
- **Nap advantage**: daytime nappers showed 20% better information recall at 6 pm vs. non-nappers.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Desirable Difficulty]]**: The 85/15 rule and active recall both exploit the counterintuitive finding that introducing controlled struggle during practice strengthens memory encoding — easy practice feels productive but produces shallow learning.
- **[[Spaced Repetition]]**: The start-of-session recall habit is an informal implementation of spaced repetition — the exponentially decaying forgetting curve is flattened each time retrieval effort is applied at increasing intervals.
- **[[Compression as Understanding]]**: The Reduction skill is grounded in the principle that the ability to simplify is a proxy for depth of understanding; if you cannot compress it, you do not yet understand it.
