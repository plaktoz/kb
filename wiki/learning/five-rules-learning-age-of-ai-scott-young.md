## Summary

Scott H. Young argues learning in the AI era is genuinely both "the best and worst of times": AI can do homework for you, which is self-defeating since you can't learn without doing the work, but it also offers cheap, patient tutoring, on-demand explanations, and extra practice. He draws on Carl Hendrick's autopilot analogy — pilots faced with an emergency reached instinctively for more automation rather than manual skill — as a warning about over-reliance eroding underlying competence.

## Core Concepts

The **autopilot analogy** frames [[AI and Learning]] as a skill-atrophy risk parallel to [[Automation Bias]] in aviation: automated systems save more lives than they endanger on average, but heavy reliance can degrade the human skill meant to serve as a backup, a dynamic directly relevant to how students use AI tutoring tools.

## Key Takeaways

- **Self-defeating shortcut**: Having AI do your homework prevents the practice needed to actually learn.
- **Genuine upside**: AI offers cheap, patient tutoring, on-demand explanations, and unlimited practice problems.
- **Autopilot warning**: Pilots facing emergencies reached for more automation, not manual skill — a caution about skill atrophy.
- **Not anti-AI**: Young explicitly rejects the "machines are bad, humans good" framing; automation is net positive but must be used deliberately.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this piece?
**A**: AI is simultaneously the best and worst tool for learning — it can replace the practice that builds skill, but also lower the cost of tutoring and practice when used deliberately.

**Q2**: What real-world analogy does the author use to illustrate the risk of AI over-reliance?
**A**: The introduction of aviation autopilot: pilots facing emergencies instinctively reached for more automation rather than manual flying skill, suggesting habitual reliance can erode the very skill needed in a crisis.

**Q3**: What's the practical implication for how students should use AI tools?
**A**: Use AI for tutoring, explanations, and practice generation, but avoid using it to produce the first attempt at work — doing the work yourself first is what builds the underlying skill.
