## Summary

A mixed-methods study of 90 faculty in the Cottrell Scholars community, led by [[Timothy J. Atherton]], found that decisions about using AI depend less on institutional policy, access, or general AI beliefs than on each person's pedagogical orientation: their views on AI's role in thinking and learning. Faculty fell on a continuum rather than in pro- or anti-AI camps. The authors recommend discipline-specific conversations and low-stakes practice, noting that centrally distributed guidance may have limited influence.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Pedagogical Orientation]]**: The set of views a person holds about the role AI should play in thinking and learning; it predicted AI use in teaching and in research and other work.
- **[[Cottrell Scholars Collaborative]]**: The [[Research Corporation for Science Advancement]] community studied; the study grew out of its discussions about computation in science.
- **[[Mixed-Methods Research]]**: Numerical survey questions paired with open-ended responses, used to replace speculation with faculty voices.
- **[[Productive Struggle]] vs. [[Cognitive Delegation]]**: Lower-orientation faculty see struggle as essential to learning; higher-orientation faculty delegate selected tasks while still evaluating, verifying, and revising output.
- **[[Institutional AI Policy]]**: Initiatives and information sources showed little consistent relationship with orientation.
- **[[AI Validation Assignments]]**: Assignments where students validate, interpret, and revise AI-generated work.

## Key Takeaways

- **Adoption**: About 78% of respondents use AI in at least one context.
- **Driver**: Orientation toward thinking and learning mattered more than policy, access, or general AI beliefs.
- **Breadth**: Orientation also predicted AI use in research, not just teaching.
- **Continuum**: Heavy users voiced reservations; limited users saw practical value.
- **Policy Gap**: 64% reported institution-wide AI initiatives versus 8% department-level.
- **Recommendation**: Discipline-specific conversations and low-stakes trials of AI outputs.
- **Next Step**: Test orientation in larger faculty populations and against classroom decisions.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Behavior follows internal beliefs about the task more than external rules** — How people use a tool tracks their underlying view of what the activity is for, so policy and access alone have limited influence unless people can work through how the tool fits their goals and values.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What was the study's central finding about faculty AI adoption?
**A**: Faculty decisions about using AI depended less on institutional policies, access, or general AI beliefs and more on pedagogical orientation, meaning their views on AI's role in thinking and learning.

**Q2**: What evidence showed institutional action had limited influence?
**A**: 64% of respondents reported institution-wide AI initiatives and 8% department-level ones, yet initiatives and information sources showed little consistent relationship with AI pedagogical orientation.

**Q3**: What do the authors recommend for supporting faculty and students?
**A**: Discipline-specific conversations and low-stakes opportunities for faculty to try AI and decide when it supports learning versus short-circuits expertise development, plus assignments requiring students to validate, interpret, and revise AI-generated work.
