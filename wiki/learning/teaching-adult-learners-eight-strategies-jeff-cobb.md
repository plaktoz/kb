## Summary
Adult learners bring life experience, autonomy, and practical goals, but face time pressure, insecurity, and resistance to new methods. Jeff Cobb distills 20+ years of experience into eight strategies, from relevance and storytelling to chunking, in-the-moment feedback, and flexible structure. His top habit is for the trainer to keep learning too.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Adult Learning Theory]]**: The principles [[Malcolm Knowles]] popularized, built on learner autonomy, experience, and relevance.
- **[[Andragogy]]**: Teaching adults, which differs from teaching children because adults choose to learn and bring prior experience.
- **[[Cognitive Overload]]**: The risk of too much information at once, addressed by compartmentalized lessons.
- **[[Storytelling]]**: A memory device that taps adult learners' emotions to improve retention.
- **[[Formative Assessment]]**: Quizzes, reflection prompts, and peer feedback that surface misunderstandings early.
- **[[Microlearning]]**: Short lessons of 10 to 15 minutes that fit busy schedules.
- **[[Learning Styles Myth]]**: A 2008 review by [[Harold Pashler]] found no solid evidence for matching instruction to style.
- **[[Generative AI]] in learning**: Helps with practice more than thinking; a 2026 University of Maryland trial found low uptake and lower grades.
- **[[Thinkific]]**: The author's pick of online course platform.

## Key Takeaways
- **Relevance**: Ground lessons in real-world applications adults can use immediately.
- **Experience**: Match language to learners' level; avoid jargon with novices.
- **Stories**: Use stories as memory devices to improve retention.
- **Chunking**: Break content into short sections with recaps to prevent overload.
- **Timely Feedback**: Correct mistakes at the moment of need.
- **Visuals**: Use one clear focal point per slide or page.
- **Discussion**: Encourage questions, including written options for reluctant speakers.
- **Flexible Structure**: Allow discussion but keep control of the schedule.
- **Challenges**: Resistance, low motivation, insecurity, time limits, and ego clashes need specific responses.
- **AI Caveat**: About 15% of offered students used a course AI tutor; access meant lower grades.
- **Top Habit**: Trainers should keep learning themselves.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Adults learn what connects to their own experience and needs** — Adult learners filter material through real-world experience, so lessons stick when tied to immediate application and their prior knowledge. · *model:* [[Andragogy]]
→ explains: Relevance, Experience, Stories, Discussion

**T2. Working capacity is limited, so information must be chunked** — Learners follow material better when it is split into logically sequenced short sections with recaps. · *model:* [[Cognitive Load Theory]]
→ explains: Chunking, Visuals

**T3. Uncorrected errors take hold** — Mistakes or wrong assumptions should be corrected when they appear, before the incorrect idea sets and confuses learners.
→ explains: Timely Feedback, Discussion

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about teaching adults?
**A**: Adults differ from children in autonomy, experience, and need for immediate relevance, so effective teaching builds on those traits through eight strategies. The most important habit is for the trainer to keep learning.

**Q2**: How does the article say to handle cognitive overload and feedback?
**A**: Break content into short lessons (5 to 10 minute videos with a recap quiz) and correct mistakes at the "moment of need," such as through auto-graded quizzes that explain each answer.

**Q3**: How should course creators apply AI in adult learning?
**A**: Use AI for practice feedback and new scenarios rather than doing the learners' thinking, since learners who let AI think for them learn less. A University of Maryland trial found low tutor uptake and lower grades for students with access.
