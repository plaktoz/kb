## Summary
Inquiry-based science teaching conflates a subject's epistemology (how experts generate new knowledge) with its pedagogy (how novices should be taught fundamentals). Because novices lack the knowledge base for open-ended inquiry, explicit, structured instruction — followed by retrieval practice — is the more effective approach for building foundational science knowledge.

## Core Concepts
- The [[Epistemology vs Pedagogy]] distinction — how experts discover knowledge differs from how novices should learn it.
- [[Novice-Expert Differences]] — experts perceive underlying structure; novices fixate on surface features.
- [[Explicit Instruction]] as a four-phase model: curricular delineation, prerequisite checks, teacher-led modeling, and consolidation.
- [[Cognitive Load Theory]] — avoiding redundancy and split-attention effects via "blank canvas modeling."
- [[Retrieval Practice]] as necessary to secure knowledge against forgetting.

## Key Takeaways
- **Category Error**: Teaching science "like scientists work" (inquiry) wrongly assumes pedagogy should mirror epistemology.
- **Novices ≠ Junior Experts**: Novices lack the structural knowledge that lets experts learn productively through open-ended inquiry.
- **Four-Phase Model**: Map knowledge needs, check prerequisites, model explicitly, then consolidate and practice.
- **Practice Sequencing**: Start with similar/blocked examples before mixing with prior knowledge ("decompose and recompose").
- **Explicit Instruction Isn't Enough Alone**: Must be paired with spaced retrieval practice to prevent forgetting.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models
- **[[Map is Not the Territory]]**: Conflating how expert scientists work (the territory) with how science should be taught to novices (the map) is exactly the kind of category error this model warns against.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim?
**A**: Inquiry-based science teaching is based on a category error — it assumes pedagogy for novices should mirror the epistemology experts use to discover new knowledge.

**Q2**: What cognitive-science evidence supports this?
**A**: Studies show novices and experts differ qualitatively, not just quantitatively — experts perceive underlying structure while novices fixate on surface details, even with relevant knowledge present.

**Q3**: What should educators do instead of pure inquiry-based teaching?
**A**: Use structured explicit instruction (mapping knowledge, checking prerequisites, modeling, consolidating) combined with spaced retrieval practice.
