## Summary

A September 2026 [[Wonkhe]] x [[Studiosity]] "temperature check" of UK universities finds institutions moving decisively away from the early "prohibition and detection" model of [[Academic Integrity]] toward a supportive posture driven by pedagogy and active integration of AI into assessment. Yet student representatives say the system still lacks coherence and trust, especially around vague guidance and AI-use declarations. The authors propose five areas of practice — four technical/pedagogical and one cultural — that together form a reasonably robust, explicable approach.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Prohibition and Detection Model]]**: The initial institutional response to generative AI, now viewed in hindsight as the wrong approach and a source of student stress and anxiety.
- **[[AI in Assessment Frameworks]]**: All specify the permitted level of AI per assessment — via an open/closed binary, a "traffic light" three-point scale, or the [[Perkins AI Assessment Scale]] (five-point).
- **Differentiated approaches**: A single set of misconduct rules no longer works; programmes and disciplines need varied rules under a broadly consistent institutional framework.
- **The five areas of practice**:
  1. **[[AI Literacy]]** — baseline understanding plus discipline-specific competence to match tool, task, and outcome; framed as supporting employability.
  2. **Closed/synchronous assessments** — can validate learning but are hard to scale, raise inclusion challenges, and are vulnerable to wearable tech.
  3. **Open assessments** — "designing in" AI enables [[Authentic Assessment]], formative tasks, and AI as a "critical thinking partner," but vague guidance turns opportunity into anxiety.
  4. **[[Validation of Learning]]** — AI-use declaration forms and [[Viva Voce|vivas]]; the weakest area, with scalability gaps and a risk of slipping into "policing."
  5. **Culture of openness** — wraps the other four; students want to be partners in defining the problem, not consulted on policy already in draft.
- **[[Support and Validate Framework]]**: Studiosity's (the article's commercial partner) approach for students to self-validate learning at scale via formative feedback rather than punitive detection.
- **Mixed institutional signals**: The slow creep of AI into staff communications, marking, and feedback may contradict messages to students about appropriate use.

## Key Takeaways

- **Shift underway**: Universities moving from detecting misconduct to supportive, pedagogy-led integrity.
- **Method**: Policy review, OIA guidance, plus three round tables (two leaders, one students).
- **No single fix**: Each of five areas is incomplete alone; together they're robust.
- **Declarations backfire**: Unclear guidance makes AI-use declarations a source of student anxiety.
- **Trust deficit**: Students doubt institutions will believe declarations or fear exposing misunderstanding.
- **Closed exams aren't a panacea**: Hard to scale, inclusion issues, wearable-tech vulnerability.
- **Co-design demanded**: Students want involvement before solutions are drafted.
- **Students don't want to cheat**: They worry about AI's impact on careers and futures.
- **Disclosure**: Article produced in partnership with Studiosity, which sells a related framework.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Swiss Cheese Model]]**: The authors explicitly argue no single practice (literacy, closed exams, open assessment, validation) closes every gap, but layered together their weaknesses offset each other — classic [[Defense in Depth]] applied to academic integrity.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the Wonkhe x Studiosity "Keeping the Learning Real" report?
**A**: Universities are moving away from "prohibition and detection" toward a supportive, pedagogy-driven approach that integrates AI into assessment, but the integrity system still isn't coherent or trusted by students.

**Q2**: Why is the "validation of learning" area identified as the weakest, and what goes wrong there?
**A**: Declaration forms and vivas face scalability gaps and risk tipping into "policing"; where guidance is unclear, students fear their declarations won't be trusted or will expose their misunderstanding of the rules, which adds anxiety.

**Q3**: How should an institution apply these findings when revising its AI and academic integrity policy?
**A**: Combine all five areas rather than relying on any one, give clear per-assessment AI guidance within a consistent framework, and bring students in as partners at the problem-definition stage — while ensuring staff use of AI doesn't contradict the messages given to students.
