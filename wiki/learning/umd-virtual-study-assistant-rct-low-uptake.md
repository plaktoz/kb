## Summary

The [[University of Maryland]] ran a [[Randomized Controlled Trial]] of its own AI tutor, the Virtual Study Assistant (VSA), before rolling it out campuswide. The results were inconclusive, mainly because only about 15% of students in the treatment group actually used the tool. UMD is holding off on wider deployment, and the study highlights how hard it is to isolate the effects of [[Generative AI]] on learning. It also points to a need for much more instructor training and for universities to rethink whether to build their own ed-tech.

## Core Concepts

- **Virtual Study Assistant (VSA)**: a closed [[AI Tutor]] built on [[OpenAI]]'s [[ChatGPT]]. It connects to the [[Learning Management System]] and answers questions around the clock using course materials such as documents, slides and video transcripts.
- **Study design**: fall 2025, 2,379 undergraduates and 30 instructors across several disciplines, comparing LMS participation and grades of students with and without VSA access. Published as a working paper by Annenberg EdExchange.
- **Findings**: VSA access slightly lowered final grades and LMS participation. Only about 15% of students offered the tool used it.
- **Confounds**: students could also use ChatGPT and Gemini through the university. The study suggests that instructors promoting the tutor, and being able to read chat logs, may have put students off or shaped what they asked.
- **Usage patterns**: 73.8% of users asked for information, explanations or solutions; 11.3% used it for practice or test prep; 0.7% asked for feedback on their own work.
- **Tutor mode vs. default mode**: few instructors switched from the default mode, which gives the correct answer immediately, to tutor mode, which "guides students toward a correct response."
- **[[Jennifer King Rice]]**: UMD provost; commissioned the study and says the university needs to understand what effective deployment requires before "simply turning this on."
- **[[Jing Liu]]**: lead author and director of UMD's Center for Educational Data Science and Innovation; says instructors need "much more extensive" training: "You cannot force people to only use one tool."
- **[[Justin Reich]]**: MIT Teaching Systems Lab; says field RCTs are "a bundle of interventions" whose causal mechanisms can't be fully separated.
- **[[Stephen J. Aguilar]]**: USC; argues institutions are bad at building bespoke ed-tech and "can't compete against the market." He sees the opportunity in smaller, personalized models that do a few things well.

## Key Takeaways

- **Low uptake**: Only about 15% of students with access used the VSA.
- **Marginal harm**: VSA access slightly reduced final grades and LMS participation.
- **Answer-seeking dominates**: 73.8% of users sought answers; 0.7% sought feedback on their work.
- **Default matters**: Most instructors left the tool on answer-giving mode instead of tutor mode.
- **Surveillance effect**: Instructors could read chat logs, which may have deterred honest use.
- **Training gap**: Faculty got only "light training"; researchers want much more.
- **Deployment paused**: UMD is still not rolling the tool out campuswide.
- **Build vs. buy**: Aguilar says universities can't beat commercial AI tools at their own game.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Default Effect]]**: Most instructors never changed the default answer-giving mode, so students mostly asked for answers instead of guided tutoring.
- **[[Observer Effect]]**: Because instructors could read chat logs, students' awareness of being watched may have changed what they asked, or whether they used the tool at all.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why did UMD's randomized trial of its Virtual Study Assistant fail to produce clear findings?
**A**: Only about 15% of students offered the tool used it, and they could also use ChatGPT and Gemini. That made it hard to isolate the VSA's effect on learning.

**Q2**: How did students who did use the VSA mostly use it, and what design factor shaped that?
**A**: 73.8% asked for information, explanations or solutions, and only 0.7% sought feedback on their own work. Most instructors left the tool on its default mode, which gives answers immediately, instead of tutor mode.

**Q3**: What lessons does the study offer universities planning to deploy AI tutors?
**A**: Train instructors much more thoroughly, think carefully about default settings and the privacy of chat logs, and study tools before deploying them. Aguilar also suggests universities focus on small, personalized models instead of competing with commercial tools.
