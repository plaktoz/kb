# The Feynman Learning Technique

## Summary
The Feynman Technique is a four-step learning method developed by physicist Richard Feynman that uses the act of teaching to expose and fill gaps in understanding. True learning requires being able to explain a concept in simple language — not just recall terminology — because jargon masks the illusion of knowledge. By iteratively simplifying, identifying gaps, and transmitting ideas, you convert fragile information into durable, transferable knowledge.

## Key Takeaways
- **Teach to a child**: Write out what you know as if explaining to a 6th-grader — complexity hides ignorance.
- **Identify gaps**: Where your explanation falters reveals exactly what you don't understand.
- **Go back to source**: Fill each gap by returning to the material until basics are clear.
- **Simplify and organize**: Narrative coherence signals genuine understanding; confusion signals a gap.
- **Transmit (optional)**: Teaching to a real person surfaces questions you didn't know you had.
- **Jargon is a red flag**: If you need complex vocabulary, you lack flexibility — you cannot explain in multiple ways.
- **Knowing vs. naming**: Knowing a bird's name tells you nothing about the bird; understanding lets you reason from it.
- **Circle of competence**: Mapping gaps defines the honest boundary of what you actually know.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1: What is the core claim of the Feynman Technique?**
A: The best way to learn something is to attempt to teach it in simple terms — the act of explaining exposes the exact boundaries of your understanding and turns isolated information into usable knowledge.

**Q2: Why does jargon undermine real understanding, according to this article?**
A: Complex vocabulary allows you to mimic knowledge without possessing it. When you can only repeat fixed phrases, you have no flexibility; when you truly understand, you can rearrange simple words into multiple explanations, proving genuine grasp.

**Q3: How would you apply the Feynman Technique to learn a new technical concept?**
A: Write out everything you know about the concept in plain language as if for a 12-year-old, note every spot where the explanation breaks down, return to source material to fill those gaps, then reorganize into a coherent simple narrative — and optionally explain it to a colleague to surface further blind spots.
