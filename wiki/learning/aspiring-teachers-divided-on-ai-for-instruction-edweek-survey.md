## Summary
An EdWeek Research Center survey of 268 teacher educators finds aspiring teachers split on using AI for K-12 instruction: about a quarter of faculty say their students view it negatively, and about 2 in 5 say positively. Students worry that outsourcing planning to AI undermines their own thinking, while some educators argue hands-on experience is needed to spot AI-generated student work.

## Core Concepts
- **[[EdWeek Research Center]] survey**: 268 [[Teacher Education]] faculty, August-September 2026.
- **[[Preservice Teachers]]**: Aspiring teachers with mixed views on [[AI in Education]] for lesson plans and quizzes.
- **[[Critical Thinking]]**: Students fear [[Cognitive Offloading]] to AI removes reflection on what students should understand.
- **Non-AI alternatives**: [[Holly Niedermeyer]] offers a classmate-feedback option instead of AI.
- **[[AI Literacy]]**: [[Jennifer Kidd]] argues experimenting with AI helps teachers detect AI-generated student work.

## Key Takeaways
- **Split Views**: ~25% of faculty report negative student views; ~40% report positive.
- **Offloading Risk**: Outsourcing lesson design to AI skips the teacher's own critical thinking.
- **Mixed Use**: Students would use AI for lesson ideas but create most materials themselves.
- **Opt-Out Options**: Professors offer non-AI alternatives given ethical and environmental concerns.
- **Detection Skill**: Without using AI tools, teachers are less able to spot AI-written work.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Outsourcing a task outsources the thinking it forces** — Delegating a task to a tool removes the reasoning that doing it yourself would require, so the delegator's judgment goes unexercised. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Offloading Risk, Mixed Use

**T2. Judging an output requires first-hand experience of its source** — You can only evaluate or detect what a tool produces if you have used the tool yourself.
→ explains: Detection Skill

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: How do aspiring teachers view using AI for instruction, according to teacher educators?
**A**: They are divided: about a quarter of faculty say students hold negative views, while about 2 in 5 say positive views.

**Q2**: Why does Sophie Melikitu James hesitate to use AI for lesson planning?
**A**: She says outsourcing tasks to AI can take away from students because the teacher didn't apply their own critical thinking to what students should understand.

**Q3**: Why do some educators say future teachers should experiment with AI even if they won't use it for planning?
**A**: Jennifer Kidd argues that without using AI tools themselves, teachers are less able to judge how much of student work is AI-generated.
