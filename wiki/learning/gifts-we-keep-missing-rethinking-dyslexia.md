# The Gifts We Keep Missing: Rethinking Dyslexia

## Summary

Marking Dyslexia Awareness Month, the authors argue that a "deficit-only view" of [[Dyslexia]] causes real emotional harm by ignoring the cognitive strengths that often accompany it. Drawing on research and Dyslexic Advantage community survey data, they identify three notable strength areas — spatial reasoning, divergent creativity, and unusual idea combination — and call for a strengths-based approach in how dyslexia is diagnosed and supported. The piece frames dyslexia's developmental arc as a slow-blooming flower: early reading struggles can give way to flourishing strengths given proper support.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Dyslexia]]**: A neurobiologically based, highly heritable condition affecting approximately 17% of the population, traditionally framed around reading deficits.
- **[[Strengths-Based Education]]**: An approach that identifies and nurtures cognitive strengths alongside addressing deficits, contrasted with a "deficit-only view."
- **[[Twice-Exceptional Students]]**: Learners with "spiky profiles" — e.g., high verbal reasoning paired with weak reading fluency — who are prone to shame and withdrawal when only their weaknesses are addressed.
- **[[ADHD]]**: Co-occurs with reading disabilities in 25-40% of cases; the article cautions that treating ADHD alone does not resolve dyslexia's core challenges.
- **Robert Ballard**: Ocean explorer cited as an example of superior three-dimensional spatial/mental visualization ability associated with dyslexia.
- **Catherine Drennan**: MIT professor referenced for how dyslexic visualization skills can aid scientific problem-solving.

## Key Takeaways

- **Prevalence**: Dyslexia affects approximately 17% of the population and is neurobiologically based and highly heritable.
- **Strength area 1**: Superior three-dimensional spatial reasoning, illustrated by explorer Robert Ballard's visualization abilities.
- **Strength area 2**: Heightened divergent creativity, shown on alternative-uses-style creativity tasks.
- **Strength area 3**: Aptitude for forming unusual idea combinations plus strong recognition memory.
- **Emotional cost**: Deficit-only framing causes shame and withdrawal, especially in twice-exceptional students with spiky cognitive profiles.
- **Comorbidity caveat**: ADHD co-occurs with reading disabilities in 25-40% of cases; treating ADHD alone won't fix dyslexia's core challenges.
- **Developmental arc**: Early reading struggles can precede later-flourishing strengths, likened to a slow-blooming flower.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Survivorship Bias]] (inverted)**: Clinical and educational focus on dyslexia's visible reading failures obscures the strengths that don't show up until later or in different domains — the article's core corrective is to broaden the sampling of what counts as evidence of ability.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central argument about how dyslexia should be viewed?
**A**: Dyslexia should not be seen only through a deficit lens; it is often accompanied by genuine cognitive strengths (spatial reasoning, divergent creativity, unusual idea combination) that a strengths-based approach can identify and nurture.

**Q2**: What evidence does the article cite for dyslexia-linked strengths?
**A**: Community survey data from Dyslexic Advantage and research pointing to superior 3D spatial reasoning (e.g., Robert Ballard), stronger performance on divergent-creativity tasks like alternative-uses tests, and an aptitude for forming unusual idea combinations with strong recognition memory.

**Q3**: What is the practical implication for supporting dyslexic students, especially twice-exceptional ones?
**A**: Educators and clinicians should pair deficit remediation with strengths identification, since a deficit-only approach causes shame and withdrawal in students with spiky profiles, and should also screen for co-occurring ADHD without assuming its treatment alone will resolve reading challenges.
