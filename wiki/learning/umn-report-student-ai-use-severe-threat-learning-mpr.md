## Summary

MPR News reports on a University of Minnesota expert survey finding that careless use of general-purpose generative AI could "severely" and one-sidedly disrupt the learning processes that commit ideas to memory and support higher-order thinking, especially for elementary students. Lead author Panayiota Kendeou argues students should build skills independently first, and use AI only afterward to stress-test and extend their work. The report also notes a dearth of high-quality research on how AI affects learning.

## Core Concepts

- [[University of Minnesota]] report: learning scientists surveyed experts on how [[Generative AI]] affects learning.
- [[Panayiota Kendeou]]: educational psychology professor and lead author of the report.
- [[Higher-Order Thinking]]: the deep understanding needed to use ideas in novel ways, which careless AI use threatens.
- [[Skill Development]]: foundational skills such as writing and critical thinking must be learned before tool use.
- [[Primary Education]]: experts urged extra caution for elementary students because of the skills practiced at that age.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: handing the task to a tool before the student has learned to do it.
- [[Social-Emotional Development]]: collaboration, social-emotional development, and human connections are also at risk.
- [[Learning Science Evidence Base]]: little high-quality research exists on AI's effect on learning.

## Key Takeaways

- **Severity**: Experts agreed some AI contexts cause "severe and one-sided" disruption to learning.
- **Memory Risk**: Casual use threatens processes needed for committing new ideas to memory.
- **Primary School**: Experts urged extra caution with AI for elementary-age students.
- **Writing First**: Students should put thoughts in writing independently before using a tool that does it.
- **Skills Before Access**: Teachers aiming to build skills should not give students AI access.
- **Later Benefits**: Skilled students can use AI to stress-test ideas, expand arguments, and find resources.
- **Areas at Risk**: Cognitive thinking, learning skills, collaboration, social-emotional development, and human connection.
- **Evidence Gap**: High-quality research on AI's effect on learning is scarce.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Skills form through independent effort** — Students build skills and agency by doing the task themselves first, so a tool that does the task before the skill exists blocks its development. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]
→ explains: Writing First, Skills Before Access, Primary School, Memory Risk

**T2. Tools help those who already hold the skill** — The same tool that harms a learner still acquiring a skill can extend a learner who has mastered it, so sequencing matters more than access. · *model:* [[Scaffolding (Learning Theory)]]
→ explains: Later Benefits, Skills Before Access, Severity

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the report's central finding about student AI use?
**A**: Experts reached wide consensus that careless, general-purpose generative AI use in certain contexts could cause "severe and one-sided" disruption to critical learning processes, especially for elementary students.

**Q2**: What evidence base and mechanism underlie the warning?
**A**: Researchers surveyed experts and found little high-quality research on AI and learning. The concern is that casual use threatens the processes for committing ideas to memory and the higher-order thinking needed to use them.

**Q3**: How does Kendeou suggest teachers apply this?
**A**: Let students work independently first so their ideas and skills are preserved, and give access to AI only once they have developed those skills. Then they can use it to test themselves, expand arguments, and find resources.
