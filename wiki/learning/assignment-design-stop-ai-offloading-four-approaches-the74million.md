## Summary

Korchevskyi argues that banning AI is not the answer; assignments should be structured so students still do most of the thinking even when AI can answer instantly. He describes four approaches from his school network: placing students inside the story, adding oral explanation, having students build usable products, and setting micro-tasks during lessons. He cites a Pew survey finding that one in 10 American teenagers complete all or most of their schoolwork with AI chatbots.

## Core Concepts

- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: handing to AI the thinking an assignment was meant to build.
- [[Oral Assessment]]: asking students to explain their reasoning aloud to show they understand their own work.
- [[Project-Based Learning]]: students build a working product, which makes basic skills necessary.
- [[Pew Research Center]]: survey source on teens' AI use for schoolwork.
- [[Dmytro Korchevskyi]]: author; founder of the school network whose curriculum team developed the four approaches.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ban Not Answer**: Banning AI is not the solution; restructure assignments so students still do the work.
- **Survey Signal**: One in 10 US teens say they complete most schoolwork with AI.
- **Detection Limit**: Experienced teachers identified AI-written essays only 37.8% of the time.
- **Personal Stakes**: Framing lessons around the student's own choices resists AI shortcuts.
- **Oral Explanation**: Students who worked through material answer follow-up questions with ease.
- **Usable Prototypes**: Building working products makes basic skills necessary, and students extend them.
- **Micro-Tasks**: Short in-lesson questions are too fiddly to hand to AI.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Delegable work skips the reasoning it was meant to build** — A task that AI can complete alone lets students bypass the thinking the assignment targets. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Detection Limit, Micro-Tasks

**T2. Explanation exposes understanding** — Students who can explain their reasoning under questioning show whether their understanding is real.
→ explains: Oral Explanation

**T3. Purposeful work resists delegation** — Tasks tied to a student's own stakes or a working product keep the student doing the reasoning.
→ explains: Personal Stakes, Usable Prototypes

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Korchevskyi's central argument about AI in assignments?
**A**: Banning AI is not the answer. Assignments should be structured so students still do most of the thinking even when AI can answer instantly.

**Q2**: How does an oral presentation reveal whether a student understands their work?
**A**: Questions about why a particular argument or fact was used require the student to link facts and reasoning from memory. Students who simply read back generated text find these questions noticeably harder.

**Q3**: How could a teacher apply the micro-task approach in a lesson?
**A**: Ask students for the next step in a problem, a response to a classmate's argument, or a prediction of an experiment's outcome. Each requires a modest independent effort.
