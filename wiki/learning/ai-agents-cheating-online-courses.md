## Summary
AI agents can now complete entire online courses on a student's behalf — logging in, watching lectures, taking tests, writing papers, and chatting with classmates — threatening the integrity of asynchronous online education, where over half of U.S. college students now take at least one course (up from about a third in 2019). ## Core Concepts
- **[[Asynchronous Online Learning]]**: Self-paced courses with prerecorded lectures and digitally submitted work, where students may never meet professors or peers even by video — structurally harder to proctor than in-person classes.
- **[[AI Agent Impersonation]]**: The escalation beyond copy-pasted chatbot essays — an agent given a command like "log in and complete my quiz" can autonomously imitate the student across an entire course.
- **[[Proctoring Shift in Physical Classrooms]]**: In-person educators have countered AI cheating by moving to oral presentations and handwritten exams — options largely unavailable in online formats.

## Key Takeaways
- Online course enrollment share among U.S. college students rose from about a third (2019) to over half (last year), expanding the surface area for this problem.
- The credibility question (whether online degrees equal campus-earned ones) predates AI but is sharpened by agent-based cheating.
- Online education has long been championed as an equity tool for nontraditional students (e.g., working parents) — a tension with the integrity risk AI agents now pose.

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: How does AI-agent cheating in online courses differ from earlier chatbot-essay cheating?
**A**: Rather than a student copy-pasting a chatbot-generated essay, an AI agent can now autonomously complete an entire course on the student's behalf — watching lectures, taking tests, writing papers, and even chatting with classmates — with a simple command like "log in and complete my quiz."

**Q2**: Why are online courses harder to protect from AI cheating than in-person classes?
**A**: In-person educators have shifted to oral presentations and handwritten exams to counter AI misuse, but asynchronous online courses — where students may never meet professors or peers even on video — don't easily support those same countermeasures.

**Q3**: What broader equity tension does AI-agent cheating create for online education?
**A**: Online education has been championed for two decades as a way for nontraditional students (e.g., low-income working parents) to access degrees flexibly; agent-based cheating threatens to undermine the credibility of those very degrees.
