## Summary

AI tutoring can help students learn when feedback, guided practice, and teacher oversight build skills they can use independently. A 2025 PNAS study found that general-purpose GPT-4 users performed better during assisted practice but worse on a later exam without AI, while a teacher-guided version avoided that decline without a clear gain. The article urges schools to test tools on classroom problems, check accessibility and privacy, keep teachers involved, and evaluate learning with new examples before expanding access.

## Core Concepts

- [[AI Tutoring]]: AI that instructs students directly or suggests responses to human tutors.
- [[Scaffolding (Learning Theory)]]: support that should build skill for independent use.
- [[Purpose-Built AI Tutors]]: tools designed with teacher input and guardrails, in contrast to general-purpose chatbots.
- [[Tutor CoPilot]]: Stanford tool that suggests responses to human tutors; the trial showed a four-point gain in mastery.
- [[Fluency Illusion]]: assisted-practice performance mistaken for durable learning.
- [[Universal Design for Learning]]: CAST framework for multiple ways to access information and express learning.
- [[Randomized Controlled Trials in Education]]: the design needed for cause-and-effect claims about a tool.

## Key Takeaways

- **Skill Transfer**: Help matters only if students can later solve problems independently.
- **Chatbot Warning**: General GPT-4 users did worse on a later exam without AI.
- **Guided Design**: The teacher-designed version avoided the harm but showed no clear exam gain.
- **Tutor CoPilot**: AI suggestions for human tutors raised mastery four percentage points.
- **Classroom Tests**: Try tools on wrong steps, vague requests, and repeated confusion.
- **Human Route**: Students need a clear path to a teacher when confusion continues.
- **Accessible Responses**: Check that every student can read, hear, and navigate responses.
- **New Examples**: Follow-up checks should use new problems that require the same skill.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Help should fade into independent skill** — Assistance counts as learning only if the student can apply the skill without it. · *model:* [[Scaffolding (Learning Theory)]]
→ explains: Skill Transfer, Classroom Tests

**T2. Assisted performance is not independent performance** — Gains during AI-supported practice can disappear once the support is removed. · *model:* [[Fluency Illusion]]
→ explains: Chatbot Warning, Guided Design, New Examples

**T3. Adults must review the help students receive** — Human judgment remains necessary when AI feedback misses the student's actual difficulty.
→ explains: Human Route, Tutor CoPilot

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about AI tutoring?
**A**: AI tutoring can support learning when feedback, guided practice, and teacher oversight help students build skills they can use independently.

**Q2**: What did the 2025 PNAS study find about students who used the general-purpose GPT-4 interface?
**A**: Both AI groups performed better during assisted practice. On a later exam taken without AI, students who used the general-purpose interface performed worse than students who practiced without it.

**Q3**: How should a school test an AI tutor before adopting it?
**A**: Try familiar situations such as an incorrect first step, a partly correct explanation, a vague request for help, and repeated confusion. Then review accuracy, fit to the student's level, and consistency with classroom instruction.
