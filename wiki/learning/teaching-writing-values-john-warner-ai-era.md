## Summary

[[John Warner]] argues that debates over teaching writing in the age of generative AI are really disagreements about values, not policy or pedagogy, so people talk past each other until those values are stated. He lays out his own framework: writing is a way of thinking, it should be taught inside authentic rhetorical situations, and process and human exchange matter more than end products. By his account, schools that tackle the problem collaboratively have made real progress, though accountability structures still constrain high school teachers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Values Alignment in Education Debates]]** — policy (buy ChatGPT or not) and pedagogy (where to use AI) sit downstream of unstated values
- **[[Writing as Thinking]]** — grappling with an idea while writing changes and deepens ownership of it
- **[[Rhetorical Situation]]** — message, audience, purpose; all writing happens inside one
- **[[Writing Practice]]** — the skills, knowledge, attitudes and habits that transfer to new genres
- **[[Rubrics and Templates]]** — [[Maja Wilson]]'s critique of rubrics; five-paragraph templates deny structural choice-making
- **[[Generative AI in Writing Instruction]]** — [[Princeton University]] ChatGPT rollout, "[[Everyone Is Cheating Their Way Through College]]" framing
- **[[Process-Focused Assessment]]** — feedback should surface the writer's choices, not judge outcomes

## Key Takeaways

- **Values First**: Disagreements about AI in writing are values disputes; unstated assumptions block productive conversation.
- **Learning vs. Ranking**: Warner grades to reflect learning; others grade to rank students against a standard.
- **Authentic Audience**: Writing for a grader is not an authentic rhetorical situation and hinders development.
- **Rubric Critique**: We write to audiences, not rubrics; rubrics shift focus to abstract principles.
- **Struggle Matters**: AI use that removes the struggle of choice-making hinders development as writers.
- **No AI Assessment**: Writing is for other unique intelligences, so AI should not assess student writing.
- **Progress Report**: Best-performing schools use collaborative approaches; accountability structures constrain high school teachers.
- **Echo Chamber Limit**: Invitations come from people who already share his values, limiting broader progress.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Warner's core claim about debates over AI and teaching writing?
**A**: The debates are fundamentally about values rather than policy or pedagogy. Without understanding and sharing assumptions about those values, people cannot productively discuss how to put them into practice.

**Q2**: Why does Warner say rubrics and five-paragraph templates hinder student writers?
**A**: We write to audiences, not rubrics, so rubrics pull attention to abstract principles. Templates may earn better grades but deny students practice making choices about sequence and structure.

**Q3**: How does Warner's framework imply AI should and should not be used in writing courses?
**A**: AI should not be used in ways that keep students from the struggle of choice-making, nor to assess student writing. The exchange between student and instructor should stay between humans, focused on the writer's choices.
