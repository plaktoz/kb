## Summary
How students use AI matters more than whether they use it: AI can support executive function skills such as planning, organization and time management, or it can replace the struggle that builds them. The article argues for preserving desirable difficulties and offers a metacognition-based test for parents.

## Core Concepts
- [[Executive Function]]: planning, task initiation, organization, time management, and self-monitoring.
- [[Desirable Difficulty]]: struggle that improves encoding and long-term transfer.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: handing cognitive work to AI, with learning costs when excessive.
- [[Learned Industriousness Theory]]: overcoming present challenges primes people to take on future ones.
- [[Metacognition]]: students reflecting on their own learning and AI use.
- [[Robert Bjork]] and [[Elizabeth Bjork]]: researchers who framed desirable difficulties (named "Bork" in the source).
- [[Large Language Models]]: usable for practice questions and feedback to calibrate comprehension.

## Key Takeaways
- **Adoption**: 55-70% of high schoolers and 85-95% of college students use AI for homework.
- **Supporting vs Replacing**: Breaking down tasks and building schedules supports; asking AI to complete stages replaces.
- **Evidence of Cost**: MIT found reduced brain activation in students relying heavily on AI.
- **Grades vs Exams**: In a 26,000-student study, heavy AI use meant higher HW scores but markedly lower exam scores.
- **Task Initiation**: Offloading the opening cedes the framing work and yields less original thinking.
- **Organization**: Students must learn to judge what is principal versus subordinate themselves.
- **Comprehension Calibration**: AI-generated practice questions and feedback strengthen self-assessment.
- **Parent Test**: Ask how, why, what gained, and what given up when using AI.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Reduced struggle degrades learning and transfer** — Effortful processing changes how material and processes are encoded; removing the effort weakens retention and long-term transfer. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]
→ explains: Evidence of Cost, Grades vs Exams, Task Initiation

**T2. Offloading a cognitive task to a tool can stunt the skill it exercises** — Whether a tool supports or replaces a skill depends on whether the person still does the cognitive work. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Supporting vs Replacing, Organization, Comprehension Calibration

**T3. Reflecting on one's own learning is the first step to ownership of it** — Students who can explain how and why they use a tool can judge when it helps or hinders. · *model:* [[Metacognition]]
→ explains: Parent Test

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about AI and student skill development?
**A**: How students use AI matters more than whether they do; AI can support executive function skills or replace the struggle that builds them.

**Q2**: What evidence is cited that heavy AI reliance harms learning?
**A**: MIT found decreased brain activation in heavy AI users, and a 26,000-student study linked heavy AI use to faster, higher-scoring homework but markedly lower exam scores.

**Q3**: How can a parent or tutor assess whether a student's AI use is healthy?
**A**: Ask the student to explain how and why they use AI, what they get from it, and what they give up. This builds metacognition and ownership of learning.
