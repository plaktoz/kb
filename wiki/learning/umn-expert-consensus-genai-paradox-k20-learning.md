## Summary

A Delphi-style panel of learning experts rated 65 learning processes and reached consensus on 42 of them, finding a "GenAI Paradox": general-purpose GenAI is risky for initial acquisition and higher-order thinking but can strengthen the middle practice and consolidation step. Experts also flagged social and self-regulation risks, greater vulnerability in elementary grades, and a thin evidence base. They recommend using GenAI well and thinking well with it, rather than asking whether it is good or bad.

## Core Concepts

- [[Generative AI]]: focus is general-purpose large language models such as ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini.
- [[GenAI Paradox]]: acquisition is vulnerable, consolidation is enhanced, and higher-order thinking is vulnerable again.
- [[Cognitive Offloading]]: forgoing the effort needed to acquire information or think deeply.
- [[Metacognitive Laziness]]: loss of self-regulated learning and metacognition skills through overreliance.
- [[Acquire-Practice-Apply Cycle]]: GenAI helps most in the practice stage.
- [[Higher-Order Thinking]]: critical thinking, epistemic cognition, and evaluation, where risk is one-sided.
- [[Self-Regulated Learning]]: only help-seeking reached enhancement consensus.
- [[Social-Emotional Development]]: collaboration and belonging are vulnerable with no clear enhancement path.
- [[Delphi Method]]: preregistered consensus process; thresholds were mean 3.0 or higher with 75% agreement.
- [[Learning Science Evidence Base]]: importance is rated high but evidence strength is low.
- [[Panayiota Kendeou]], [[Jeffrey A. Greene]], [[Nia Nixon]]: lead coordinating authors.

## Key Takeaways

- **Scope**: Experts identified 65 learning processes; 42 reached consensus.
- **Paradox**: Learn first without GenAI, consolidate with it, then think independently.
- **Enhanced**: Feedback, self-testing, tutoring, scaffolding, and deliberate practice benefit most.
- **Memory Risk**: Encoding, retrieval, and information acquisition are vulnerable to disruption.
- **Thinking Risk**: Ten of 18 higher-order thinking items showed vulnerability only.
- **Social Risk**: Collaboration, belonging, and social connection face one-sided risk.
- **Writing**: The only domain learning process with consensus, and at high risk.
- **Elementary Grades**: Students lack knowledge to judge outputs, so are most vulnerable.
- **Evidence Gap**: Importance ratings were high, evidence ratings mostly low.
- **Teachers First**: GenAI may be best placed in teachers' hands before students'.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Durable learning requires effortful processing** — Skills and memories form through the effort of acquiring and practicing them, so tools that remove that effort undermine the learning they appear to speed up. Short-term performance gains can short-cut long-term learning. · *model:* [[Desirable Difficulty]]
→ explains: Paradox, Memory Risk, Thinking Risk

**T2. Offloading thinking causes skill decay** — When a tool does the thinking, the learner stops practicing the skills needed to think and to use the tool wisely, so those skills fail to develop or decay. · *model:* [[Cognitive Offloading]]
→ explains: Thinking Risk, Memory Risk, Elementary Grades

**T3. Conclusions can be no better than the evidence behind them** — Meta-analyses and summaries built on small, short, or poorly designed studies are unreliable, however confident the headline. · *model:* [[Garbage In, Garbage Out]]
→ explains: Evidence Gap

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the "GenAI Paradox" in the experts' consensus?
**A**: Casual use of GenAI threatens initial acquisition of information and later higher-order thinking, yet it can enhance the middle step of consolidating and strengthening what was learned. Learners should first learn without it, then use it to consolidate, then prioritize their own thinking.

**Q2**: How did the experts define consensus, and what did they find about the evidence?
**A**: Consensus required a mean rating of at least 3.0 with at least 75% of respondents rating 3.0 or above; 42 of 65 processes met it. Importance ratings were generally high while evidence-strength ratings were mostly low.

**Q3**: How should schools and educators act on the findings?
**A**: Teach students to use GenAI well and think well with it, prioritizing the learning process over polished products and avoiding use during initial acquisition. Policy should be calibrated to processes rather than choosing between unrestricted adoption and blanket prohibition.
