## Summary

[[ResearchED]] is a grassroots, volunteer-driven Saturday conference that began in London in 2013 and is now spreading its evidence-based teaching ideas to the U.S., most recently in St. Louis. Its cheap, jargon-free, practitioner-led format moves [[Science of Learning]] findings to classroom teachers, who then often become presenters themselves. Organizers say the U.S. lags England and Australia by 10 to 15 years in adopting these ideas.

## Core Concepts

- **[[ResearchED]]**: founded in 2013 by [[Tom Bennett]] and Hélène Galdin-O'Shea at Dulwich College after UK teachers asked online why they were never taught how learning works. A franchise model lets local organizers stage events under its banner.
- **[[Science of Learning]]**: the study of how memory, attention and practice work, applied to teaching. [[Retrieval Practice]] is one example, illustrated by [[Patrice Bain]]'s random mini-quizzes.
- **[[Tom Sherrington]]**: keynote speaker who argued educators should do "the things we know really, really well" and get every child participating. His book *Rosenshine's Principles in Action* grew out of an early ResearchED talk on [[Barak Rosenshine]].
- **Format**: one day, on a Saturday, in a school; tickets $60-$80 versus $595-$795 for ISTE+ASCD; unpaid speakers; sessions approved by a local committee.
- **[[Cindy Nebel]]** and **[[MJ Mercanti-Anthony]]**: organizer and Bronx principal who put the U.S. gap at 10-15 years behind England and Australia.
- **Structural barriers in the U.S.**: state-level control ("50 countries that need change") and sacrosanct teacher autonomy, versus England's national curriculum and a teacher-training framework.
- **AI stance**: only one session mentioned [[Artificial Intelligence]], on evaluating tools that embed the science of learning.

## Key Takeaways

- **Grassroots Origin**: Born from UK teachers' online debate about why teacher prep ignored learning science.
- **Small Scale**: The St. Louis event drew just 160 attendees and about 40 sessions.
- **Volunteer Filter**: Unpaid speakers and Saturday timing mean passionate, committed participants without sales pitches.
- **Monday-Ready**: Attendees leave with practical techniques rather than abstract theory.
- **Audience-to-Presenter Cycle**: Teachers try ideas, then return to present results, sometimes becoming authors.
- **US Lag**: Organizers estimate the U.S. trails England and Australia by 10 to 15 years.
- **Autonomy Barrier**: State control and teacher autonomy slow adoption, yet resistance fades when teachers see engaged classrooms.
- **Application Over Novelty**: Sessions stay grounded in evidence and focus on use rather than new ideas.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Costly voluntary participation filters for commitment and quality** — When contributors bear their own costs and receive no payment, those who take part are the committed ones, and promotional motives are screened out. · *model:* [[Skin in the Game]]
→ explains: Volunteer Filter, Monday-Ready

**T2. Practice spreads through peer-to-peer cycles that compound** — Practitioners who adopt an idea and return to teach it recruit others, so small gatherings can grow into broader change. · *model:* [[Flywheel Effect]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is ResearchED and what problem does it address?
**A**: It is a grassroots one-day teacher conference, begun in London in 2013, that brings research on how learning works to classroom teachers who were never taught it in training. It uses a jargon-free, evidence-grounded format.

**Q2**: What features of the format keep it focused and high quality?
**A**: Tickets are cheap ($60-$80), speakers are unpaid and pay their own way, events are on Saturdays, and every session is approved by a local committee. Organizer Cindy Nebel says this means every talk is passionate and attendees get usable ideas rather than sales pitches.

**Q3**: Why is adoption slower in the U.S., and what does the article suggest helps?
**A**: Education is controlled state by state and teacher autonomy is prized, unlike in England's national system. Seeing engaged classrooms quickly dissolves resistance, and organizers hope attendees will bring colleagues next time.
