## Summary

People forget up to 70% of new information within 24 hours, and common study habits like rereading, highlighting, and cramming actively work against retention. Ten evidence-based techniques — anchored in [[Spacing Effect]], [[Testing Effect]], and [[Cognitive Load Theory]] — reliably improve how fast and how durably people learn, without requiring more total study time.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Spacing Effect]]** — distributing study across multiple sessions beats massed cramming, documented by [[Hermann Ebbinghaus]] in 1885 and confirmed by a 2013 meta-review
- **[[Testing Effect]]** — actively retrieving information from memory (self-quizzing) strengthens retention far more than passive rereading, per a landmark 2006 [[Roediger and Karpicke]] study
- **[[Cognitive Load Theory]]** — working memory holds only about four items at once; chunking related concepts into meaningful units reduces overload
- **[[Feynman Technique]]** — explaining a concept simply, in one's own words, exposes the exact gaps where understanding breaks down
- **[[Deliberate Practice]]** — focused, uncomfortable practice at the edge of current ability with immediate feedback (Anders Ericsson), where 20 focused minutes can outperform two casual hours

## Key Takeaways

- **Forgetting is fast**: up to 70% of new material is lost within 24 hours without reinforcement
- **Interleaving beats blocking**: mixing topics within a session builds stronger retention than drilling one topic exhaustively
- **Sleep is part of learning**: memory consolidation happens largely during sleep, not as a reward after studying
- **Multitasking has a real cost**: the brain doesn't truly multitask — it switches, and each switch carries a cognitive tax
- **Passive review is the costliest mistake**: rereading and highlighting feel productive but produce recognition, not recall

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Forgetting Curve]]**: Ebbinghaus's original finding that memory decays predictably over time unless reinforced — the entire rationale for spaced repetition schedules like 7-3-2-1 or 1-3-5-7 reviewed in the article.
