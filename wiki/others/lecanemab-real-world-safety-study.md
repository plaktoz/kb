## Summary

A Duke Health study of 230+ patients found real-world safety and treatment persistence for [[lecanemab]], an anti-amyloid Alzheimer's therapy, closely matched clinical-trial results — about 80% of patients stayed on the drug for at least a year, with most side effects resolving without hospitalization.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Lecanemab]]**: FDA-approved anti-amyloid therapy that removes amyloid protein linked to Alzheimer's disease from the brain.
- **[[ARIA (Amyloid Related Imaging Abnormalities)]]**: The most closely watched side effect — brain swelling or bleeding — which developed in 24.3% of patients in this study.
- **[[APOE ε4]] genotype**: Patients homozygous for this high-risk Alzheimer's gene variant were about four times more likely to develop ARIA.
- **[[Cerebral Amyloid Angiopathy (CAA)]]**: Silent amyloid buildup in brain blood vessels, present in over 50% of Alzheimer's patients, thought to underlie ARIA.

## Key Takeaways

- 79% of patients who started lecanemab continued treatment for up to 396 days; the 21% who discontinued did so mostly due to adverse events.
- ARIA events could occur up to 30 weeks after starting therapy, with two peaks around 10 and 25 weeks, underscoring the need for sustained monitoring rather than just early-treatment vigilance.
- 31% of patients had serious adverse events, most commonly infusion reactions, falls, or strokes — though some weren't necessarily attributable to the drug itself.
- Seven common clinical, blood, and imaging tests could not yet reliably predict who would develop ARIA; one combined MRI/blood-biomarker method showed the most promise.
- Duke piloted an AI-supported imaging tool to detect subtle ARIA changes earlier and plans to expand its use.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A real-world study of more than 230 patients found that lecanemab's safety profile, treatment persistence, and disease-progression effects in everyday clinical practice closely match what was seen in controlled clinical trials.

**Q2**: What specific genetic factor most increases ARIA risk, and by how much?
**A**: Patients who are homozygous for the APOE ε4 gene variant were about four times more likely to develop ARIA than other patients, with no difference in rates by sex.

**Q3**: What practical implication does the ARIA timing data have for patient monitoring?
**A**: Since ARIA events can occur up to 30 weeks after starting therapy with peaks around 10 and 25 weeks, clinicians need sustained monitoring throughout treatment rather than just intensive monitoring in the first few weeks.
