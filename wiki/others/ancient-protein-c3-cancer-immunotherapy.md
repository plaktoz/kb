## Summary

Nagoya University researchers found that complement protein [[C3]] — an evolutionarily ancient molecule present even in sponges and jellyfish — improves cancer immunotherapy only when produced locally inside a tumor, not when circulating in the bloodstream. Recreating this local effect with a mimicking drug restored immunotherapy efficacy in previously resistant tumors and extended survival in mice.

## Core Concepts

- **[[C3 (complement protein)]]**: Normally made in the liver and released into blood to help fight infections; here shown to have a distinct, more important local function when produced by tumor-associated fibroblasts.
- **[[Immunosuppressive myeloid cells]]**: Cells that weaken the body's ability to attack tumors; locally produced C3 breaks down into a fragment (iC3b) that blocks these cells from infiltrating the tumor.
- **[[Immune checkpoint blockade]]**: The class of immunotherapy (anti-PD-1 antibody) tested in this study, whose efficacy depended on local, not circulating, C3 levels.

## Key Takeaways

- **Location matters more than quantity**: Reducing circulating C3 by 90% did not reduce immunotherapy efficacy, but blocking local tumor C3 production did — despite only a 9% drop in circulating C3.
- **Mimicking the effect works**: A drug designed to imitate C3's blocking function restored immunotherapy efficacy in tumors that had previously resisted treatment.
- **Human tissue validation**: In lung cancer patients, high local C3 in tumor tissue predicted better outcomes (about half responded to treatment vs. none among low-C3 patients); blood C3 levels showed no such link.
- **Clinical implication**: Local C3 levels could become a biomarker for predicting which patients will respond to immunotherapy, and low-C3 tumors could be treated with C3-mimicking drugs to overcome resistance.

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Complement protein C3 improves cancer immunotherapy effectiveness only when produced locally within a tumor, not when circulating in the blood — a finding with implications for predicting and improving immunotherapy response.

**Q2**: What specific experimental evidence supports the "local, not circulating" mechanism?
**A**: Reducing circulating C3 by 90% in mice did not reduce immunotherapy efficacy, but blocking tumor-local C3 production did reduce efficacy, even though circulating C3 only fell by 9% in that condition.
