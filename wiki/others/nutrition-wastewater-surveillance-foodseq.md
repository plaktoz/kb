## Summary

Researchers at UNC Chapel Hill and Duke have repurposed COVID-era wastewater surveillance technology into FoodSeq-FLOW, a DNA-sequencing method that identifies population-level dietary habits — down to specific plant and animal species eaten — from municipal wastewater samples. The approach avoids the cost, time, and recall bias of traditional dietary questionnaires and captures all demographics connected to the sewage system, at a cost of less than a cent per person.

## Core Concepts

- **[[FoodSeq-FLOW]]**: DNA-sequencing technology that identifies dietary DNA markers in wastewater, developed from a prior COVID-19 wastewater surveillance system.
- **[[Wastewater Epidemiology]]**: Using sewage sampling as a population-level, non-self-report data source — pioneered for COVID tracking and now extended to nutrition.
- **[[Recall Bias]]**: A key limitation of traditional dietary questionnaires that FoodSeq avoids, since it doesn't depend on people accurately remembering or honestly reporting what they ate.
- **[[Food Desert]]**: Areas lacking access to healthy food, particularly fresh produce — a policy target the researchers hope more granular dietary data can help address.
- **[[Septic System Coverage Gap]]**: A structural limitation — people on septic tanks (common in rural areas) aren't captured by wastewater-treatment-plant sampling, systematically underrepresenting already-underrepresented rural populations.

## Key Takeaways

- **The method sampled wastewater from 21 treatment plants covering more than 2 million people** in North Carolina at less than a cent per person.
- **FoodSeq can identify DNA markers down to the specific species eaten** (the study documented 60 fish species, including locally caught varieties not seen elsewhere in the state).
- **It captures all demographics connected to the sewage system**, including young children too young to complete dietary questionnaires — a group traditional methods struggle to measure.
- **Key limitation**: data can be confounded by environmental/industrial runoff, DNA degradation, seasonal tourism mixing visitor and resident waste, and exclusion of septic-system-dependent rural populations.
- **Next step proposed**: a multi-year, statewide rollout to capture seasonal dietary patterns, since current data are single snapshots rather than year-round trends.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is FoodSeq-FLOW and where does the underlying technology come from?
**A**: A DNA-sequencing method that identifies dietary DNA markers in wastewater samples, adapted from a wastewater surveillance system originally built to track COVID-19.

**Q2**: What key advantage does wastewater-based dietary tracking have over traditional questionnaires?
**A**: It avoids recall bias and self-report inaccuracy, is far cheaper (less than a cent per person), and captures all demographics connected to the sewage system, including young children who can't reliably complete questionnaires.

**Q3**: What population is systematically excluded from this method, and why does that matter?
**A**: People on septic tank systems — common in rural areas — aren't connected to municipal wastewater infrastructure and therefore aren't captured, meaning already-underrepresented rural populations remain underrepresented in this new data source too.
