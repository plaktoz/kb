## Summary
Stanford researchers discovered a new immune cell type in planarian flatworms — named "[[Ruptoblasts]]" — that destroys nearby pathogens by exploding within seconds to minutes and vanishing almost completely, a process they've termed "[[Ruptosis]]," offering a potentially novel model for future targeted infection and cancer treatments.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Ruptoblasts]]**: Glandular immune cells discovered in flatworms that intensify their normal secretion systems to explosively release toxic contents when triggered by the hormone [[Activin]].
- **[[Ruptosis]]**: The explosive cell-death process itself, distinguished from other known explosive cell deaths by its extreme speed — seconds to minutes versus the hours typical of comparable mammalian or bacterial processes.
- **[[Activin]]**: A hormone already known to affect flatworm regeneration and reproduction, found here to trigger the ruptoblast explosion response when levels rise during tissue rejection.

## Key Takeaways
- **Discovered via "Frankenstein" worm fusion experiments**: fusing flatworm fragments from different individuals triggered tissue rejection resembling human transplant rejection, but through an entirely different cellular mechanism.
- **One ruptoblast can kill up to 70 nearby cells** within about five minutes of activation.
- **Broad-spectrum kill activity**: tested against E. coli bacteria, human kidney cells, and mouse blood cells, ruptoblasts destroyed all three target types while containing damage to the immediate vicinity.
- **Evolutionarily restricted**: found only in basal bilaterians like flatworms, suggesting vertebrates lost this defense mechanism — likely because they lack the abundant stem cells flatworms use to repair the resulting tissue damage.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core discovery in this study?
**A**: A previously unknown immune cell type in flatworms, called ruptoblasts, that destroys nearby pathogens and cells by exploding within seconds to minutes, a process the researchers named ruptosis.

**Q2**: What triggers ruptosis, and how was it discovered?
**A**: Rising levels of the hormone activin trigger the explosive response; researchers found it while fusing flatworm fragments from different individuals and observing an intense inflammatory rejection response in the "Frankenstein" worms.

**Q3**: Why might this discovery matter for future medicine?
**A**: Ruptosis's speed and tightly localized damage — unlike slower-leaking mammalian cell death — could inspire new targeted treatment approaches against bacterial infections or tumors, since researchers know so little about immune mechanisms in understudied organisms.
