## Summary

A review of over 350 papers by [[Dudley Lamming]] (University of Wisconsin-Madison) argues that most sedentary adults consume more protein than they need, and that protein restriction — not just calorie restriction — can extend lifespan by raising [[FGF21]], a hormone linked to longer lifespan in mice, improved blood sugar, and reduced inflammation. This directly complicates the [[Protein Intake Guidelines]] debate, which had been trending toward recommending *more* protein for muscle preservation and healthy aging.

## Core Concepts

- [[FGF21]] (fibroblast growth factor 21) — hormone that rises when protein intake is low; increases energy expenditure, improves blood sugar control, reduces inflammation, and correlates with longer lifespan in mice
- [[Protein Restriction]] — extends lifespan in flies and rodents even without reduced calorie intake; recent human trials show reduced weight, fat mass, and improved fasting blood sugar
- [[Methionine, Isoleucine, Valine]] — amino acids whose excess intake may trigger growth-promoting pathways linked to obesity, inflammation, and age-related disease
- [[Personalized Nutrition by Activity Level]] — Lamming's conclusion that protein needs should be set by physical activity level, not just age, since athletes tolerate high protein without the same metabolic downsides

## Key Takeaways

- **Sedentary adults likely over-consume protein** relative to what supports healthy aging, even as US guidelines recently nearly doubled recommended intake (to 1.2–1.6 g/kg/day)
- **FGF21 is the proposed mechanistic link** between lower protein intake and slower aging, mirroring effects seen with calorie restriction
- **Athletes appear protected** from high-protein downsides, likely because exercise and muscle-building offset the metabolic costs
- **This directly contradicts the case for universally higher protein intake** documented in [[Protein Intake Guidelines]] — the right answer may be activity-dependent rather than one-size-fits-all

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Context-Dependent Optimization]]**: the apparent contradiction between "eat more protein" and "eat less protein" research dissolves once activity level is treated as the moderating variable — a reminder that many nutrition debates are actually the same finding fought over different population contexts, not competing truths.
