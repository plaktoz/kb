## Summary
Attention is the fundamental resource being colonized by modern algorithmic platforms — YouTube, Spotify, LinkedIn, and Reddit serve engagement-maximizing content rather than letting users choose their own information diet. The author argues this is equivalent to handing over the keys to your own mind, and contrasts it with an earlier, more deliberate internet built around personal bookmarks, niche blogs, wikis, and RSS feeds. The intentional internet still exists beneath the corporate layer, and returning to it is a habit that builds with consistent practice.

## Core Concepts

- [[Tetris Effect]] — prolonged focus on something reshapes perception; whatever occupies your attention long enough begins to shape your thinking
- [[Attention Economy]] — algorithms on major platforms optimize for engagement, not user-chosen information diets
- [[Algorithmic Feed]] — the mechanism by which platforms like YouTube, Spotify, LinkedIn, and Reddit serve AI-generated filler and corporate-aligned messaging over user-directed content
- [[Intentional Internet]] — the older web built around personal bookmarks, niche blogs, wikis, and [[RSS Feeds]]; requires deliberate choice about where to go and why
- [[Cal Newport]] — [[Deep Work]] and digital minimalism frameworks share conceptual grounding with the article's argument
- [[Information Diet]] — the curated set of sources and topics a person deliberately chooses to consume

## Key Takeaways

- **Tetris Effect**: What you attend to reshapes how you perceive and think over time.
- **Algorithmic capture**: Platforms no longer let users choose; they serve engagement-maximizing content instead.
- **Screen control = mind control**: Allowing others to control your screen is like giving them the key to your brain.
- **The old web was intentional**: Bookmarks, blogs, wikis, RSS feeds required choosing where to go and why.
- **Harmful content existed, but wasn't served**: Pre-algorithm web had bad content, but it wasn't algorithmically pushed alongside mundane searches.
- **Intentional internet still exists**: It persists beneath the corporate layer for those willing to find it.
- **Tolerance for slowness is the price**: Returning requires accepting a slower, finite content experience.
- **Habit-forming**: Like any habit, intentional browsing builds with consistent attention.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Tetris Effect]]**: The psychological mechanism underlying the entire argument — attention is not passive reception but an active shaper of cognition, which is exactly why ceding control of it to an algorithm is so costly.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When engagement becomes the platform's optimization target, content quality degrades into proxy metrics (clicks, watch-time, reactions) — the very dynamic that makes algorithmic feeds hostile to genuine learning.
- **[[Autonomy and Intrinsic Motivation]]**: The intentional internet maps onto self-determination theory — choosing your own sources preserves the autonomy that algorithmic feeds systematically strip away, which explains why deliberate browsing feels more cognitively satisfying even when it's slower.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of "Attention is all you have"?
**A**: Algorithmic platforms have seized control of users' attention by serving engagement-maximizing content instead of letting users choose their own information diet — equivalent to giving platforms the key to your brain.

**Q2**: What psychological mechanism explains why ceding attention to algorithms is so consequential?
**A**: The Tetris Effect — prolonged focus on anything reshapes perception, so whatever platform algorithms push long enough begins to shape how you think, not just what you consume.

**Q3**: How does the author suggest reclaiming intentional attention, and what is the cost?
**A**: Return to the older, deliberate internet — personal bookmarks, niche blogs, wikis, RSS feeds — by tolerating a slower and more finite content experience; the habit builds with consistent practice.
