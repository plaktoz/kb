## Summary

The 5-minute rule is a CBT technique for procrastination: commit to an avoided task for just five minutes with genuine permission to stop afterward, which works through behavioral activation by making the emotional barrier to starting small enough to overcome.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Behavioral Activation]]**: A core CBT process where action changes emotion faster than waiting for motivation — the theoretical engine behind the five-minute rule's effectiveness.
- **[[Procrastination Cycle]]**: Avoiding a task brings short-term relief but makes the task loom larger and more anxiety-provoking over time, reinforcing further avoidance — a loop that must be interrupted, not scheduled around.
- **[[Genuine Permission to Stop]]**: The rule's second, essential clause — being truly free to quit after five minutes is what makes the initial five-minute commitment credible to the avoidant part of the mind.
- **[[Procrastination vs. ADHD]]**: Chronic procrastination bundled with lifelong distractibility, disorganization, and unfinished projects may indicate ADHD rather than a simple avoidance habit, requiring different treatment.

## Key Takeaways

- **Two clauses matter equally**: commit to five minutes, and be genuinely free to stop when the timer ends — no guilt, no fine print.
- **Procrastination is about emotion, not scheduling**: people who procrastinate usually know exactly how long a task takes and when it's due; what they're avoiding is a feeling.
- **Three mechanisms drive the rule's effectiveness**: the feared task proves more tolerable than dread predicted, momentum builds once started, and each repetition weakens the avoidance habit itself.
- **If a task feels too big even for five minutes**, break it into smaller steps — e.g., "write the report" becomes "open the document and write one sentence."

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: The five-minute rule reduces procrastination by making an avoided task's starting commitment small enough to bypass emotional resistance, using the CBT principle of behavioral activation.

**Q2**: What is the mechanism by which the five-minute rule breaks the procrastination cycle?
**A**: A five-minute commitment is too small to trigger the avoidance response; once started, the feared task usually proves more tolerable than expected, momentum builds, and each successful start weakens the habitual link between the task and escape.

**Q3**: How should someone apply this rule if even five minutes feels too daunting for a task?
**A**: Break the task into a smaller entry point — for example, turning "write the report" into "open the document and write one sentence" — since the goal is entry, not a specific amount of progress.
