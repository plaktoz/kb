## Summary

A sourced roundup of four-day work week statistics that separates controlled trial results from surveys of what workers want, and corrects the two most-cited numbers: Iceland's "four-day week" was actually a 35–36 hour week, and Microsoft Japan's 40% productivity jump was one month of sales per employee that Microsoft later moved out of its results. The strongest evidence (141 companies vs. 12 controls, Nature Human Behaviour 2025) shows wellbeing gains that track real hour cuts, while pilots measured revenue and self-ratings rather than output per hour. A four-day week that keeps 40 hours keeps the workload — and Gallup data shows compressed four-day workers report more burnout, not less.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Four-Day Workweek]] vs. compressed week**: Many "four-day" schemes (Tokyo, Belgium, Bentley-Gallup survey framing) fit full-time hours into four longer days; the trial benefits follow genuine hour reductions.
- **[[Iceland Shorter Working Week Trials]]**: 2015–2019 trials (Autonomy and Alda, 2021) moved 2,500+ workers from 40 to 35–36 hours; follow-on union contracts covered ~86% of the workforce, with cuts as small as 13 minutes a day or 35 minutes a week.
- **[[Microsoft Japan]] Work-Life Choice Challenge**: Sales per employee rose 39.9% in August 2019; on Nov. 8, 2019 Microsoft Japan moved the figure out of its results, saying it came from a range of factors. Retained results: days worked −25.4%, 30-minute meetings +46%, 92.1% positive rating.
- **Nature Human Behaviour study (Fan, [[Juliet Schor]], Kelly and Gu, 2025)**: 2,896 employees at 141 organizations in six countries; gains tracked hour cuts and ran through work ability, fewer sleep problems and less fatigue.
- **[[4 Day Week Global]] and [[Autonomy]] pilots**: UK 2022 pilot (61 companies, ~2,900 workers) with researchers from Boston College, Cambridge and UCD; US/Ireland 2022 pilot (33 companies, 903 employees).
- **Measurement gap**: Pilots tracked revenue, absenteeism, resignations and self-ratings — not output per hour; year-over-year revenue comparisons had no control group.
- **Work redesign**: Cutting meeting number and length, email etiquette, and automated templates are what make the model stick (Portugal pilot, Gomes and Fontinha, 2024).
- **Policy landscape**: Thirty-Two Hour Workweek Act reintroduced Sept. 8, 2026 as H.R. 10323 by Rep. [[Mark Takano]]; state pilot bills in New Jersey (S4242) and Pennsylvania (HB 884); Spain rejected a 37.5-hour week (Sept. 2025); Kickstarter union codified its 32-hour week after a 42-day strike.
- **[[Burnout]] and work quality**: Gallup concludes work-experience quality has 2.5x–3x the wellbeing effect of days or hours worked.

## Key Takeaways

- **Iceland reframed**: The famous trial was a 35–36 hour week, not four days.
- **Microsoft Japan caveat**: 40% figure was one month of sales; later withdrawn from results.
- **Strongest evidence**: 141 trial companies improved; 12 control companies didn't.
- **Dose-response**: Bigger personal hour cuts produced bigger wellbeing improvements.
- **UK retention**: 92% continued after trial; 89% still running it a year later.
- **UK reality**: Average hours fell 38 to 34; days 4.86 to 4.52.
- **UK wellbeing**: 71% less burnout; resignations fell 57% (not statistically significant).
- **US/Ireland pilot**: 96.94% of employees wanted to continue.
- **Revenue, not productivity**: UK revenue +1.4% over trial; no output-per-hour metric.
- **Germany 2024**: 3.95 fewer hours with no significant revenue or profit change.
- **Not universal**: Germany 20% dropped it; Portugal 4 of 21 reverted to five days.
- **Low adoption**: 9% of US organizations (SHRM 2023); 0.3% of Indeed postings.
- **Worker demand**: 81% of US workers support it; 10% would take a pay cut.
- **Valuation gap**: Owl Labs workers value it at 8% of salary; Portugal pilot workers at 28%.
- **Compressed backfire**: 34% of Gallup four-day workers burned out vs. 29% five-day.
- **Redesign predicts survival**: 2+ organizational changes → 8% reversion vs. 38% otherwise.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: The famous 40% Microsoft Japan number and pilot revenue figures show how a single headline proxy gets mistaken for productivity — the pilots never measured output per hour at all.
- **[[Parkinson's Law]]**: Work expands to fill available time; the Portugal and UK results show the model only holds when meetings and email are deliberately shrunk, not when the same work is compressed into fewer days.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central correction about four-day work week evidence?
**A**: The most-cited numbers are misleading — Iceland's trial was a 35–36 hour week and Microsoft Japan's 40% was one month of sales — and the real benefits follow genuine hour cuts, not compressed four-day schedules.

**Q2**: What did the largest controlled study find, and through what mechanism?
**A**: Across 2,896 employees at 141 organizations, burnout fell and job satisfaction and health improved (not seen in 12 control companies), with larger gains for bigger hour cuts, driven by better work ability, fewer sleep problems and less fatigue.

**Q3**: If a company wants a four-day week to last, what does the evidence suggest it should do?
**A**: Genuinely cut hours and redesign work before starting — especially reducing meeting number and length — since Portugal firms making two or more organizational changes reverted only 8% of the time versus 38% for those making one or none.
