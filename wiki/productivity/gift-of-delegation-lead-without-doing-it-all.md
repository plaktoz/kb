## Summary

[[Ellen Lail]] argues that overcommitted leaders treat [[Delegation]] as all-or-nothing, either throwing tasks over the fence or micromanaging. Thoughtful, calibrated delegation returns time for strategic priorities and accelerates the development of the people who receive the work. She offers a seven-step checklist for deciding when and how to delegate each task.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Delegation]] as a spectrum**: leaders default to two extremes, 100% hand-off or [[Micromanagement]]; the best delegators customize the level to the person and situation.
- **Best and cheapest resource test**: ask whether you are the best and cheapest resource for a task; if not, delegate it.
- **[[Risk Mitigation]] with boundaries**: assess the risks of success or failure, then set clear boundaries and decide where you want to be involved in decisions.
- **[[Sponsorship]] and status communication**: identify where the team member needs your help, and set the expectation that they report status and completion.
- **[[Strategic Thinking]] time**: overextended leaders still do their old job alongside their current one, leaving no room to think strategically.
- **[[Talent Development]]**: delegated work lets employees learn, grow, show what they can do, and build their brand.

## Key Takeaways

- **Time Scarcity**: Overcommitted leaders asked what they want more of all answer "more time."
- **False Binary**: Many leaders see delegation as giving up total control, so they avoid it.
- **Two Gifts**: Thoughtful delegation returns calendar time and accelerates people's development.
- **Seven-Step Checklist**: Test resource fit, collaborate, identify support, assess risk, set boundaries, define involvement, require status updates.
- **Customize the Level**: Match delegation to the person and situation rather than choosing an extreme.
- **Employee Upside**: Delegated work builds confidence, trust in the leader, and loyalty.
- **Leader Upside**: Freed time goes to strategic priorities, better results, and family and friends.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Delegate what you are not the best and cheapest resource for** — A leader's time should go only to tasks where they are the most cost-effective person to do the work; everything else belongs with someone else.
→ explains: Seven-Step Checklist, Leader Upside

**T2. Calibrate control to person, task, and risk** — Delegation is a spectrum; the right level of hand-off depends on the resources needed, the collaboration required, and the risk, so both full abdication and micromanagement are errors.
→ explains: False Binary, Customize the Level, Seven-Step Checklist

**T3. Delegation pays twice** — The same act of handing off work frees the leader's capacity and builds the recipient's capability, so withholding it costs both sides.
→ explains: Two Gifts, Employee Upside, Leader Upside

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Ellen Lail's central claim about delegation?
**A**: Thoughtful delegation gives leaders back time and accelerates their people's development, but many leaders avoid it by seeing it as all-or-nothing. The best delegators avoid both extremes of total hand-off and micromanaging.

**Q2**: What first question should a leader ask when a new task arrives, and what follows?
**A**: Ask "Am I the best and cheapest resource to complete this task?" and delegate if not. Then collaborate with the team member on what they need, identify where they need your help, assess risk, set boundaries, define your involvement in decisions, and require status updates.

**Q3**: How should an overcommitted leader apply this if they struggle to let go?
**A**: Find a comfortable medium between the extremes, customizing how much they hand over to the person and situation. The freed time can go to strategic priorities and to family and friends.
