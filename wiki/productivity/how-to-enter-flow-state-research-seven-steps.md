## Summary
Flow is deep, absorbed focus on a task that stretches you just enough, and it cannot be switched on but can be set up through its preconditions. Research most consistently points to challenge-skill balance, clear goals and immediate feedback, and interruptions, short sleep and stress are the usual blockers. The article (published by Welltory, which makes one of the apps it mentions) offers seven steps and notes when to see a doctor.

## Core Concepts
- [[Flow (Psychology)]]: a state of deep involvement described by [[Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi]] in 1975, with up to nine characteristics.
- [[Challenge-Skill Balance]]: the best-supported precondition for flow.
- [[Proximal Goals]] and [[Immediate Feedback]]: the other preconditions people can fix fastest.
- [[Context-Switching Cost]]: interrupted work gets done faster but feels more stressful and effortful.
- [[Sleep Deprivation]]: short-term sleep loss hits simple attention hardest.
- [[Arousal and Flow]]: flow peaks at moderate arousal, following an inverted U under stress.
- [[Hyperfocus]] and [[ADHD]]: proposed to possibly be the same phenomenon as flow, a hypothesis only.

## Key Takeaways
- **Three Stable Conditions**: Challenge-skill balance, clear goals and sense of control.
- **Evidence Base**: A scoping review mapped 252 flow studies from 2000 to 2016.
- **Interruption Cost**: Interrupted email workers finished faster but reported more stress and effort.
- **Sleep Effect**: A 70-study meta-analysis found sleep loss hurt simple attention most.
- **Arousal Curve**: Flow peaks at moderate activation; it is not deep relaxation.
- **Focus Blocks**: A protected 60 to 90 minute block gives flow room to develop.
- **Seven Steps**: Hard-but-doable task, hour goal, fast feedback, no interruptions, warm-up, small start.
- **Flow Risks**: Absorption can attach to scrolling, gaming or unplanned buying.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Flow depends on matched challenge and skill** — Absorption arises when a task stretches but does not overwhelm existing skill; too easy yields boredom and too hard yields anxiety. · *model:* [[Flow (Psychology)]]
→ explains: Three Stable Conditions, Seven Steps, Sleep Effect

**T2. Task switching taxes attention** — Each interruption forces reloading the prior task, so work may finish faster but at higher stress and effort, leaving no room for the warm-up flow needs. · *model:* [[Context-Switching Cost]]
→ explains: Interruption Cost, Focus Blocks

**T3. Performance and flow peak at moderate arousal** — Both too little and too much activation are linked to less flow, producing an inverted U. 
→ explains: Arousal Curve

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim about entering flow?
**A**: You cannot switch flow on, but you can set up its preconditions: a challenge matched to your skill, a clear near-term goal and immediate feedback.

**Q2**: What evidence shows interruptions harm focus?
**A**: In a lab study of 48 people answering emails, interrupted participants finished faster but reported more stress, frustration, time pressure and effort.

**Q3**: How would you apply this to a workday?
**A**: Book a daily 60 to 90 minute focus block with notifications off and a written one-hour goal, and end each session by writing the next step.
