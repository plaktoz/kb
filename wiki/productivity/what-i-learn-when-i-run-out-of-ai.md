## Summary

Jack Cheng finds that hitting weekly AI usage limits — forcing a fallback to less powerful tools — unexpectedly improves the quality of his work by letting ideas mature before he acts on them. He argues that unlimited AI access encourages "snacking" (choosing easy, low-impact tasks over harder, meaningful ones) and erodes deliberation in a way that mirrors earlier critiques of instant computing. His prescription is to build intentional pauses into daily AI workflows, treating the constraint as a feature rather than a frustration.

## Core Concepts

- [[AI Usage Limits]] — weekly caps on powerful models (e.g. [[Fable]]) that force fallback to less intensive tools; Cheng reframes these as productive friction
- [[Snacking]] — [[Will Larson]]'s term for gravitating toward easy, low-stakes tasks instead of difficult, high-impact work; enabled by frictionless AI access
- [[Deliberation vs. Immediacy]] — tension between instant AI answers and the reflective pause that clarifies what question is worth asking; echoed throughout computing history
- [[Time-Sharing Costs]] — [[Susan Graham]]'s observation that unlimited computing access breeds sloppiness; [[Alan Kay]]'s critique of institutional time-sharing as eroding deep focus
- [[Oracle at Delphi]] — Cheng's central metaphor: Greek pilgrims walked 11 km uphill to seek answers; the journey itself filtered and clarified their questions before they arrived
- [[Sherry Turkle]] — identified in 1984 that computers' promise of instant correct results makes it psychologically hard to step away and think independently

## Key Takeaways

- **Forced waiting** lets feature ideas and questions mature before AI amplifies them into action.
- **"Snacking"** is the hidden cost of frictionless AI: easy wins crowd out meaningful work.
- **Computing history warns** that immediacy has always carried a deliberation tax (time-sharing, punch-card queues).
- **Oracle metaphor**: the 11 km uphill walk to Delphi clarified questions; pauses before AI queries can do the same.
- **Practical interventions**: let ideas sit overnight; write down *why* a question matters before submitting it to AI.
- **Selectivity filter**: ideas that survive a deliberate pause are worth pursuing; those that fade were driven by instant gratification.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Activation Energy]]**: Adding friction to an action — like a usage limit — raises the activation energy required to trigger it, naturally filtering low-value requests from high-value ones.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When AI availability makes speed the implicit metric, work optimizes for output velocity rather than output quality — exactly the "snacking" failure mode Cheng documents.

**A**: "Snacking" (Will Larson's term) is the tendency to choose easy, low-impact tasks over harder, meaningful ones; frictionless AI access lowers the cost of low-effort tasks so much that they crowd out deeper work.
