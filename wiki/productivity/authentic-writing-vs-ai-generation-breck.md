## Summary

Colin Breck argues that AI-generated writing — design docs, PR summaries, tickets, even personal messages — is pervasive but unreadable: detailed yet contextless, exhaustive yet meaningless. The core failure is that the author already holds all the context when prompting, so the output works for them but leaves recipients with no scaffolding to navigate it. Survey data reinforces this: 78% of readers stop when they suspect AI authorship, and 98% prefer the author's own imperfect voice.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI-Generated Writing]]** — machine-produced text that is technically complete but stripped of the author's judgment, perspective, and voice; optimized for the prompt-giver, not the reader
- **[[Author's Voice]]** — the distinct perspective and judgment a writer brings; what Bjarne Stroustrup described as making readers feel they can "hear the author's voice ringing in their head"
- **[[Context Transfer Problem]]** — when prompting AI you hold all the context; the generated output lacks the scaffolding a reader needs, forcing exhaustive reading of every line
- **[[Iain McGilchrist]]** and **[[Simon Sarris]]** — cited to argue that summarization erodes complex mental model-building; narrative resists compression
- **[[Cognitive Outsourcing]]** — delegating cognitive work (here, writing) to AI at the cost of the skills and mental models that the process itself builds
- **[[Abstract as AI-Suitable Task]]** — the one writing task Breck found genuinely suited for AI: the abstract, which is terse, mechanical, and abstracted from substance

## Key Takeaways

- **Reader abandonment**: 78% of readers stop reading when they suspect AI authorship.
- **Voice preference**: 98% prefer the author's own imperfect voice over polished AI prose.
- **Context asymmetry**: AI output works for the prompter; recipients have none of the scaffolding.
- **Legitimate AI use**: Fact-checking against source code, citation formatting, spelling/grammar — never substantive paragraphs.
- **Cognitive risk**: Outsourcing writing trains you to ask only questions machines answer well, hiding what you're not asking.
- **Future value**: Authentic, intentional writing will become more valuable as AI floods the world with hollow text.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When thoroughness (word count, coverage) becomes the proxy for quality, AI-generated text optimizes for the proxy while eliminating the actual signal — the author's judgment — that made thoroughness meaningful.
- **[[Opportunity Cost of Cognitive Outsourcing]]**: Every writing task delegated to AI is a missed iteration of mental model-building; the cost is invisible until the skill has atrophied, exactly the dynamic Breck draws from McGilchrist and Sarris.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Breck's central argument about AI-generated writing?
**A**: AI-generated writing is unreadable not because it is inaccurate but because it lacks the author's perspective, judgment, and voice — the scaffolding recipients need to navigate dense text — making it exhaustive for the prompter but meaningless for the reader.

**Q2**: What survey data does Breck cite, and what does it reveal?
**A**: 78% of readers stop reading when they suspect AI authorship, and 98% prefer the author's own imperfect voice, showing that readers actively detect and reject machine-produced prose.

**Q3**: Where did Breck find AI genuinely useful in his own writing?
**A**: In writing an academic paper, he used AI to verify factual accuracy against source code and logs, handle citations, catch spelling and grammar errors, and draft the abstract — the most mechanical, abstracted section — but never to write a single substantive paragraph.
