## Summary
ActivTrak's fifth annual State of the Workplace report, analyzing 443 million work hours across 1,111 companies, finds that [[AI Adoption]] reached 80% and is adding to workloads rather than reducing them, while [[Focus Efficiency]] fell to a three-year low even as burnout risk dropped — revealing a growing gap between how much capacity AI frees up and how well organizations use it.

## Core Concepts
- **[[AI Adoption]]**: 80% of employees now use AI tools (up from 53% two years ago), yet time spent across every measured work category — email, chat, business management — increased rather than decreased after adoption, showing AI functions as an added productivity layer, not a substitute for existing work.
- **[[Focus Efficiency]]**: The share of work time spent in focused, uninterrupted work; fell to 60%, a three-year low, with average focus sessions down to 13 minutes 7 seconds.
- **Tool sprawl**: Average organizations went from using 2 AI tools in 2023 to 7 in 2025, with 83% now using 6 or more — creating governance challenges even as individual tools deliver value.
- **The productivity sweet spot**: Employees spending 7-10% of work hours in AI tools show the highest productivity (95%), yet only 3% of employees fall in that usage range.

## Key Takeaways
- **AI adds work, doesn't subtract it**: every measured activity category increased after AI adoption in a 10,584-user comparison — email up 104%, chat up 145%.
- **Focus is eroding even as output rises**: productive hours grew 5% while focus efficiency hit a three-year low, suggesting AI may be absorbing attention in shorter, more fragmented bursts rather than sustained blocks.
- **Burnout down, disengagement up**: burnout risk fell 22% to 5% of employees, but disengagement risk rose 21% to nearly 1 in 4 — workers whose freed-up capacity isn't being productively redirected.
- **Weekend work has become structural**: Saturday and Sunday productive hours rose 46% and 58% respectively over three years, with weekend start times shifting more than an hour earlier.
- **No single work-location model wins**: remote-only workers log the most productive time; office-only workers show the highest focus efficiency; hybrid workers get the least of both.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: As organizations optimize for AI adoption and productive-hours metrics, the data shows those metrics rising even as the underlying goal — genuinely better, more sustainable work — arguably isn't, since focus quality and disengagement are both moving the wrong direction.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the report's central finding about AI's effect on workload?
**A**: Rather than lightening workloads, AI adoption is associated with increases across every measured work activity category, functioning as an additional productivity layer rather than a substitute for existing work.

**Q2**: What specific metric shows focus quality declining even as output rises?
**A**: Focus efficiency — the share of work time spent in focused, uninterrupted work — fell to a three-year low of 60%, with average focus sessions dropping to just over 13 minutes.

**Q3**: What's the practical implication of the "productivity sweet spot" finding for organizations rolling out AI tools?
**A**: Since employees using AI for 7-10% of their work hours are most productive but only 3% of employees fall in that range, organizations should focus less on maximizing AI adoption broadly and more on helping the majority of underutilizing employees reach that optimal usage band.
