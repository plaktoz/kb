## Summary
A remote employee's company introduced a daily 10am-12pm "focus hours" block but kept all standing meetings, so meetings piled into the afternoon and work spilled past 5pm. Commenters shared similar failed no-meeting-day policies and argued that rearranging meeting slots without cutting meetings fixes nothing. The article frames this as a policy meant to help workers that hurt them because leaders ignored downstream effects.

## Core Concepts
- [[Focus Hours]]: a protected block with no meetings or messages, here 10am-12pm daily.
- [[Deep Work]]: uninterrupted time needed for demanding tasks, undermined by constant pings.
- [[Remote Work]] flexibility: remote staff thrive on autonomy, and rigid restrictions backfire.
- [[No-Meeting Days]]: Tuesday, Wednesday, Thursday or Friday variants that commenters said lapsed or only displaced meetings.
- [[Meeting Compression]]: meetings squeezed into the remaining hours, producing back-to-back afternoons.
- [[Async Work]]: the cultural default one commenter says focus policies depend on.
- [[Unintended Consequences]]: leaders far from the front line miss how policies trickle down.

## Key Takeaways
- **Displacement**: Standing meetings were kept, so Tuesday went from 2-3 calls to 5 back-to-back.
- **Policy leakage**: Focus hours became the window when managers sent "not urgent" yet urgent Slack messages.
- **Partial scope**: A department-only no-meeting rule failed because other departments still scheduled meetings.
- **Rebound**: A no-meeting day collapsed in two weeks as complainers began scheduling meetings.
- **Better approach**: Individuals blocking flexible short focus slots worked better than universal company-wide hours.
- **Phased cuts**: One commenter cut meetings gradually by halving durations and consolidating them.
- **Root cause**: Commenters blamed unexamined meeting load, not the time slots themselves.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Rearranging demand doesn't reduce it** — Shifting when meetings happen without removing any leaves total load unchanged and merely concentrates it elsewhere. · *model:* [[Unintended Consequences]]
→ explains: Displacement, Root cause, Phased cuts

**T2. Rules lapse when incentives and scope don't match behavior** — A restriction applied to only part of the system, or opposed by the habits of those it binds, gets bypassed. · *model:* [[Unintended Consequences]]
→ explains: Partial scope, Rebound, Policy leakage

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why did the "focus hours" policy make remote work worse for the poster?
**A**: HR kept all standing meetings, so they compressed into the afternoon, leaving back-to-back calls until about 5:15pm and real work after hours.

**Q2**: What evidence from commenters shows that no-meeting rules often fail?
**A**: One company's no-meeting day was a "complete bust within two weeks" as the same people who wanted focus time began scheduling meetings, and a department-only rule was ignored by other departments.

**Q3**: How could an organization introduce focus time without the same backlash?
**A**: Cut meetings in phases (shorter, consolidated, replaced) alongside the protected time, or let individuals block flexible focus slots instead of imposing universal hours.
