## Summary

An MIT Sloan Executive Education course by Robert Pozen and Renee Fry takes an output-oriented view of productivity: decide what to achieve, set priorities, and know when to use AI. The article gives three takeaways: use AI as a co-worker, work backward from tentative conclusions in research, and delegate by stakes and repetition while keeping human judgment for the final stretch.

## Core Concepts

- [[Robert Pozen]] and [[Renee Fry]]: instructors of the MIT Sloan course Personal Productivity in an AI-Enabled Workplace.
- [[Output-Oriented Productivity]]: focus on what you want to achieve rather than hours of input.
- [[AI as Chief of Staff]]: AI as a force multiplier handling routine work so leaders have more thought and judgment time.
- [[Starting at the End]]: form tentative conclusions after targeted research, then revise as evidence emerges.
- [[AI Delegation Decision Rule]]: choose Keep it, Systematize with review, Quick assist, or Automate based on stakes and repetition.
- [[Last 30% Principle]]: AI gets work to about 70%; human judgment completes the rest.
- [[Human-in-the-Loop]]: verify AI outputs and protect confidential data.

## Key Takeaways

- **Output Focus**: Productivity is about achieving goals, not increasing hours worked.
- **Tool Pairing**: Claude for long documents, ChatGPT for brainstorming, Copilot inside Microsoft apps.
- **Data Overload**: The problem is no longer too little data but too much.
- **Start at the End**: Reassess tentative conclusions every two weeks on a three-month project.
- **Gap Check**: Ask AI "What was left out? What is missing?"
- **Delegation Rule**: High stakes and rare means keep it; low stakes and repeated means systematize.
- **Last 30%**: Humans remain responsible for checking results and protecting data.
- **Durable Skills**: Setting priorities, judging output, and persuading people remain human strengths.

## 🧠 Core Principles

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the course's central view of productivity in an AI workplace?
**A**: Productivity is output-oriented: decide what you want to achieve, set priorities, and know when to use AI. AI reduces routine work to leave more time for thought and judgment.

**Q2**: How does the AI Delegation Decision Rule work?
**A**: It sorts tasks by stakes and repetition into four approaches: keep it, systematize it with review, use AI for a quick assist, or automate it. High-stakes, rarely repeated tasks stay with you.

**Q3**: How should you apply AI to research?
**A**: Identify the questions first, form tentative conclusions after a few targeted rounds, and revise them as evidence emerges, for example every two weeks on a three-month project. Ask the AI what was left out or missing.
