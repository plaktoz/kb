## Summary

Stanford research by [[Arvind Karunakaran]], [[Roshni Raveendhran]], and [[Tami Kim]] studying AI rollouts at a law firm, ad agency, and IT services firm found that framing AI purely as a productivity tool makes employees disengage, while framing it as a way to enrich jobs — paired with real time, training, and resources — drives exploration and heavier use. A two-year comparison of two matched law-firm divisions using the same "LawBot" tool found the job-enrichment-framed division used it 58% more and experimented with it 70% more than the productivity-framed division.

## Core Concepts

- [[Job Enrichment Framing]] — presenting AI as a way to free employees for more meaningful, higher-level work rather than just speeding up existing tasks.
- [[Productivity Tool Framing]] — presenting AI purely as a speed/efficiency gain, which the research links to employee disengagement and feeling like "just another cog in the machine."
- [[Task Bundling]] — jobs made of strongly bundled tasks (e.g. sales) resist AI unbundling, while weakly bundled jobs (e.g. paralegal work, medical transcription) are more modular and easier — but also more vulnerable — to automate.
- [[Scaffolding (AI Adoption)]] — formal training, knowledge-sharing sessions, vendor Q&As, and dedicated exploration time that make a manager's enrichment framing credible rather than "cheap talk."

## Key Takeaways

- **184 employees interviewed** across three companies; those required to use AI reported their work felt stripped of meaning, sapping motivation to learn new skills.
- **Two-year law firm field study**: two divisions with identical practice area, size, and pay/promotion structure got the same AI tool but different manager framing.
- **58% more usage, 70% more experimentation** in the division where the manager asked paralegals what boring tasks AI could offload, versus the division told AI would speed up document drafting.
- **Metrics must change**: paralegals doing deeper AI-assisted research took on fewer cases but produced higher-value work — case-volume metrics no longer fit the new task profile.
- **Token-count metrics backfire**: tracking AI usage volume as a proxy for value incentivizes busywork (e.g. "generating cat memes to hit a quota") rather than real business outcomes.
- **Strongly vs. weakly bundled jobs**: sales roles resist AI unbundling because tasks are interdependent; paralegal/transcription-style jobs are modular, making AI adoption easier but automation risk higher.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: Tracking AI token consumption as a proxy for value causes employees to optimize for the metric itself (generating output for its own sake) rather than real business objectives — exactly the dynamic Karunakaran describes.
- **[[Intrinsic vs. Extrinsic Motivation]]**: Framing AI as job enrichment taps intrinsic motivation (meaning, autonomy, growth), while pure productivity framing relies on extrinsic pressure — which the law-firm comparison shows produces far less exploration and engagement.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this research?
**A**: How managers frame and support an AI rollout — enrichment versus pure productivity — determines whether employees embrace or resist the tool, more than the technology itself.

**Q2**: What specific evidence from the law-firm study supports this claim?
**A**: Two otherwise-identical divisions given the same AI tool diverged sharply based on manager framing: the division framed around job enrichment used the tool 58% more and experimented with it 70% more than the productivity-framed division.

**Q3**: What practical implication follows for managers rolling out AI tools?
**A**: Managers should frame AI around freeing employees for more meaningful work, back that framing with real training/time/resources, and update performance metrics to reflect the new, often lower-volume but higher-value, work AI enables.
