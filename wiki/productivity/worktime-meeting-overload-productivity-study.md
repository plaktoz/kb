## Summary

WorkTime's workforce data, cited in an MSN article on meeting overload, found that U.S. employees spend at least 21.5% of their workweek in meetings, with up to 60% of overall time consumed by "work about work" rather than core tasks. The piece uses these figures to argue that constant meeting interruptions disrupt deep focus, increase cognitive overload, and drive billions in lost productivity globally.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Work About Work]]**: Coordination overhead — status updates, email, meetings — that consumes time without producing core task output; estimated at up to 60% of overall employee time.
- **[[Meeting Overload]]**: The cumulative effect of excessive scheduled meetings (21.5%+ of the workweek) crowding out focused work time.
- **[[Cognitive Overload]]**: The mental strain from frequent context-switching between meetings and tasks, cited as a direct productivity cost of meeting-heavy schedules.
- **[[WorkTime]]**: Employee monitoring/productivity analytics vendor whose workforce data underpinned the MSN article's claims.

## Key Takeaways

- **21.5%+ of the workweek**: Minimum share of U.S. employee time spent in meetings, per WorkTime data.
- **Up to 60% on "work about work"**: Majority-adjacent share of overall time going to coordination overhead rather than core tasks.
- **Deep focus disruption**: Constant meeting interruptions are framed as the mechanism behind lost productivity, not meetings in isolation.
- **Billions in losses**: The article frames meeting-driven cognitive overload as a globally significant productivity cost.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Context-Switching Cost]]**: Each meeting interruption forces a cognitive reset when returning to deep work, so the real cost of meeting overload compounds beyond the raw hours logged in meetings themselves.
