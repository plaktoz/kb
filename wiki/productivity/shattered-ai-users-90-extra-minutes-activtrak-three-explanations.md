## Summary
Shattered's analysis of the [[ActivTrak Productivity Lab]] study (9,140 workers, 467 enterprises) finds AI users' workdays run 8.42 to 9.08 hours versus 7.58 for non-users, but stresses this is an association only. It lays out three competing explanations (new AI-created work, selection of already-busy people, and heavier workloads for tool-savvy staff), and closes with predictions and a checklist of what teams should measure. See also [[activtrak-ai-users-longer-workdays-more-app-switching]] and [[ai-users-longer-workdays-association-not-causation-tech-insider]] for other coverage of the same report.

## Core Concepts
- [[ActivTrak Productivity Lab]]: behavioral data from April 1 to September 25, 2026; does not name the AI products used.
- [[Heavy AI Users]]: more than 45 minutes across at least five sessions per day.
- [[Correlation vs Causation]]: the report shows association; three readings fit the data.
- [[Selection Effect]]: busy, overloaded people may adopt AI first, making AI look like the cause of long hours.
- [[Application Switching]] and [[Focus Sessions]]: 1.1 versus 0.95 switches per hour; 37.8 versus 48.2 minutes of focus.
- [[After-Hours Work]]: heavy users log 18 additional minutes, which the article reads as boundary erosion.
- [[Meeting Paradox]]: heavy users spend 3% of work time in meetings versus 5%, yet still work longer.
- [[AI Adoption Metrics]]: logins, seats and prompts measure usage, not results.
- [[Gartner]] layoff-rehire forecast and [[AI Capex]] as market context for the ROI question.

## Key Takeaways
- **Workday Range**: AI users work 8.42 to 9.08 hours versus 7.58 for non-users.
- **Sample Size**: 9,140 knowledge workers across 467 enterprises, about 20 per company.
- **Association Only**: ActivTrak states the findings show no causal relationship.
- **Three Explanations**: AI-created review work, busy people adopting AI first, heaviest workloads to tool experts.
- **Fragmented Focus**: Heavy users switch apps 15.2% more; focus sessions are 22% shorter than light users'.
- **After-Hours Creep**: Eighteen daily minutes equals about 90 minutes weekly of extra evening work.
- **Meeting Puzzle**: Fewer meeting minutes did not shorten heavy users' days.
- **Usage Targets Backfire**: Rewarding AI usage directly can manufacture longer days and shorter focus blocks.
- **Unconfirmed**: No product names and no output measures, so net benefit is unknown.
- **Measure Now**: Baseline hours before rollout, then pair activity metrics with trusted outcome metrics.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Association does not prove causation** — A link between two measures can reflect the cause running the other way or a third factor. Only before-and-after tracking of the same people can separate cause from selection. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Association Only, Three Explanations, Workday Range

**T2. Selection can masquerade as effect** — If the busiest people adopt a tool first, the tool looks like the cause of the outcome that actually drove its adoption. · *model:* [[Selection Bias]]
→ explains: Three Explanations, Unconfirmed

**T3. Rewarding a usage metric produces more usage, not more value** — When adoption itself is the target, people use the tool even where it adds friction, creating the symptoms the data shows. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Usage Targets Backfire, Measure Now

**T4. Every context switch costs focus** — Hopping between an AI tool, document, browser and messaging apps breaks unbroken work stretches, which time-on-task counters do not capture. · *model:* [[Context-Switching Cost]]
→ explains: Fragmented Focus, Meeting Puzzle

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the ActivTrak data show about AI users' workdays, and what does it not prove?
**A**: AI users' days span 8.42 to 9.08 hours versus 7.58 for non-users, a gap of up to about 90 minutes. ActivTrak says this is an association, not proof that AI causes longer days.

**Q2**: Why might busy people be overrepresented among AI users?
**A**: Ambitious or overloaded employees may adopt AI precisely because they already work long hours and want relief, so the tools look like a cause when they are a response. Only a before-and-after study tracking the same employees could settle it.

**Q3**: What should teams do before judging whether AI helps their own staff?
**A**: Take a pre-rollout baseline, then track total hours, after-hours work, app switching and focus-session length alongside outcome measures like tickets closed or error rates. If hours rise and outcomes stay flat, the tools are adding load.
