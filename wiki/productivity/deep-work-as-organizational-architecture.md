## Summary
Rework.com argues that deep work fails as an individual productivity tactic because organizations reward the opposite behavior — responsiveness, visibility, synchronous availability — and that the only durable solution is treating focused work as an architectural problem: designing async-first defaults, decision authority frameworks, and meeting cultures at the company level rather than relying on individual habit change.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Deep Work as Organizational Architecture]]**: Companies that achieve focused work as a competitive advantage design it structurally — async-first communication defaults, clear decision ownership, written-first norms — rather than expecting individual habit changes to survive misaligned organizational incentives.
- **[[Organizational Incentive Misalignment]]**: Responsiveness is proxied for engagement; visibility is proxied for productivity; meeting attendance signals commitment. Individual deep work tactics run headlong into these norms and can disadvantage people trying hardest to do good work.
- **[[Interruption Blast Radius]]**: Each interruption affects more than the interrupted person — a senior engineer's context switch creates downstream waits for QA, which delays product manager review, which pushes demos. The 23-minute recovery time (Gloria Mark, UC Irvine) compounds across teams.
- **[[Async-First Defaults]]**: Decisions written before discussed; status updates in async formats; documentation sufficient for action without a kickoff call. When the whole team shifts to async-first, the compounding runs in the opposite direction.
- **[[Organizational Deep Work Audit]]**: 5 diagnostic questions — where does project context live? how are decisions made? what does a senior IC's calendar look like? what happens if someone is unresponsive for 4 hours? when a team misses a deadline, what's the diagnosis?
- **[[Meeting as Last Resort]]**: Meetings are what you do when async has failed or stakes require real-time interaction. Most meetings solve coordination problems that better documentation would have prevented.

## Key Takeaways
- **McKinsey estimate**: knowledge workers spend 28% of the week managing email alone (excluding meetings).
- **23-minute rule**: average recovery time after an interruption (Gloria Mark, UC Irvine).
- **Organizational moat**: not a tool stack, but an operating model where your best people can do their best work on a random Tuesday without fighting for it.
- **3 COO actions (30 days)**: (1) async-first status norm, (2) cut recurring meetings by 30%, (3) create decision authority doc for top-20 decisions.
- **Team compounding**: async-first works only when the whole team adopts it — one person protecting focus blocks creates friction in an interrupt-driven culture.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Systems vs. Goals]]**: Individual deep work goals (block your calendar) fail because the system (organizational incentives) overpowers individual goals. Lasting change requires changing the system's incentive structure, not willpower.
- **[[Second-Order Effects]]**: The second-order cost of a meeting isn't just its duration — it's the context switch recovery time (23 min), downstream dependencies delayed, and signals sent about what the culture actually values.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why do individual deep work tactics (blocking calendar, turning off notifications) fail in most organizations?
**A**: Organizational incentives reward the opposite behavior — responsiveness signals engagement, visibility signals productivity, meeting attendance signals commitment. Individual habit changes run against these incentives and can actively disadvantage people trying to do focused work.

**Q2**: What is the "interruption blast radius" concept and why does it matter at team level?
**A**: Each interruption affects more than the individual — a 23-minute context switch recovery (per Gloria Mark, UC Irvine) for a senior engineer creates downstream delays for QA, PM, and delivery timelines that don't show up in any individual's productivity metric but aggregate as missed deadlines and slower shipping speed.

**Q3**: What are the three COO-level structural changes to enable deep work in 30 days?
**A**: (1) Announce an async-first status norm — project updates and non-urgent decisions in writing by default; (2) audit and cut recurring meetings by 30% by converting what can be converted to async; (3) create a decision authority doc for the 20 most common leadership decisions, naming owner, stakeholders, and escalation scope.
