## Summary

Therapists and coaches share nine unconventional, brain-based productivity tricks — from making the first step "ridiculously easy" to using music and movement as psychological triggers — designed to work with the brain's resistance to starting tasks rather than fighting it through willpower.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Ridiculously Easy First Step]]**: Therapist Risa Williams's technique of shrinking a task's starting point until it's too small for the brain to resist (e.g., "just open the document and type the title").
- **[[Interstitial Action]]**: Sam Dylan Finch's term for physical/verbal cues (countdowns, movement) that signal to the brain that one activity is ending and another beginning — related to Mel Robbins's [[Five-Second Rule]].
- **[[Power Planning]]**: Sam Laura Brown's method of scheduling all tasks directly into a calendar (including rest and exercise) instead of working from an open-ended to-do list, resembling [[Time Blocking]] but broader in scope.
- **[[Task Intensity Rating]]**: Rating parts of a task by emotional "intensity" rather than importance, then tackling low-intensity pieces first to build momentum before facing harder parts.

## Key Takeaways

- **Tiny first steps** ("open the document and type the title") reliably lead to doing more than planned, since starting is the hardest part.
- **Music-based triggers** — a "Hype Playlist" for motivation or a "task playlist" as a built-in timer — leverage dopamine and nostalgia to break distraction loops.
- **Movement before thinking** disrupts the "I'm stuck" loop; a walk in a circle or body shake can substitute for overthinking a task's start.
- **Micro-celebrations** (clapping, a small dance) after completing hard tasks reinforce the behavior via the brain's reward system.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Loss Aversion via Momentum]]**: Several of these tactics (tiny first step, task playlist, five-second countdown) work by making the *marginal* cost of starting near zero, exploiting the fact that people systematically overestimate a task's difficulty before beginning it — once started, the sunk momentum keeps them going past the artificially low commitment threshold.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A set of nine unconventional productivity techniques work by directly countering the brain's resistance to starting tasks — using tiny commitments, music, movement, and reward — rather than relying on willpower or traditional time management.

**Q2**: What is the "Ridiculously Easy First Step" technique and why does it work?
**A**: Shrinking a task's starting action to something almost trivially small (e.g., "just open the document") removes the psychological barrier to beginning — and because starting is the hardest part, people typically continue well past that tiny first step.

**Q3**: How could someone apply the "task playlist" idea to a chore they've been avoiding?
**A**: Pick one or two upbeat songs and commit to working on the task only for their duration — using the music as a built-in timer that makes the task feel more like a fun sprint than an open-ended obligation.
