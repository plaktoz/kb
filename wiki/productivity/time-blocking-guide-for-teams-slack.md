# What Is Time Blocking? A Guide for Teams

## Summary

[[Time Blocking]] divides the day into scheduled periods reserved for specific tasks, so you decide in advance where your attention goes instead of letting incoming requests set the agenda. It works because it protects [[Deep Work]], constrains tasks against [[Parkinson's Law]], reduces [[Decision Fatigue]], and turns vague intentions into concrete plans. Slack argues individual time blocking fails without shared team norms such as response windows, visible focus blocks, and consolidated meetings.

## Core Concepts

- [[Time Blocking]]: a time management method that assigns each calendar block to a specific task or type of work; unlike a [[To-Do List]], it fixes *when* work happens.
- **Historical lineage**: [[Benjamin Franklin]] organized his day into defined periods for work, meals, reflection, and rest; [[Cal Newport]] later tied the method to [[Deep Work]].
- **Five-step method**: list and prioritize tasks weekly, find your peak productivity window, assign work to blocks, add buffer time, then review and revise daily.
- **Related scheduling variations**:
  - [[Task Batching]]: grouping quick, similar shallow tasks (Slack, email, approvals) into two or three daily windows.
  - [[Day Theming]]: assigning each weekday a theme (planning, client work, creative work); [[Jack Dorsey]] used it while leading Twitter and Square.
- **Why it works**:
  - Protects [[Deep Work]] by giving one demanding problem full attention.
  - Counters [[Parkinson's Law]] by placing a time constraint around each task.
  - Reduces [[Decision Fatigue]] by moving "what next?" decisions into the planning phase.
  - Converts intentions into concrete plans, per research by [[Todd Rogers]], [[Katherine Milkman]], [[Leslie John]], and [[Michael Norton]] on [[Implementation Intentions]].
- [[Biological Prime Time]]: [[Chris Bailey]]'s term for your peak period of energy and concentration, which should host the most demanding work.
- **Team-level enablers**: shared [[Asynchronous Communication]] norms, including response windows, focus block visibility, and meeting consolidation to avoid a [[Swiss Cheese Schedule]].
- [[Slack]] features supporting time blocking: Do Not Disturb, status updates, scheduled messages, audio/video clips, and [[Google Calendar]] / [[Outlook Calendar]] integrations that sync availability to status.

## Key Takeaways

- **Plan over react**: A to-do list leaves timing open; time blocks commit work to slots.
- **Newport's claim**: A 40-hour time-blocked week matches 60+ unstructured hours of output.
- **Microsoft research**: Protected calendar focus time linked to higher engagement and well-being.
- **Buffer time**: Leave 10–15 minutes between major blocks for overruns and requests.
- **Daily review**: Spend 5–10 minutes rescheduling unfinished work and noting why tasks overran.
- **Concrete beats vague**: "Draft proposal 10 a.m.–noon Thursday" beats "Finish the proposal."
- **Match energy**: Put careful-thought work in your Biological Prime Time.
- **Batch communication**: Answer messages two or three times daily and tell colleagues when.
- **Treat focus like meetings**: Mark blocks busy, enable Do Not Disturb, and define what counts as urgent.
- **Team norms required**: A focus block won't survive if colleagues expect instant replies.
- **Swiss cheese schedule**: Scattered short calls leave gaps but few long stretches for concentration.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Parkinson's Law]]**: Because work expands to fill available time, a fixed block forces you to scope realistically what fits in the window rather than letting the task sprawl.
- **[[Implementation Intentions]]**: Specifying when and where an action will happen increases follow-through, which is exactly what time blocking supplies by attaching each task to a specific day and time.
- **[[Decision Fatigue]]**: Every in-the-moment choice about what to do next draws on limited mental energy, so making those choices once during planning preserves energy for execution.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument of Slack's guide to time blocking?
**A**: Time blocking lets you protect your attention by deciding in advance what each calendar block is for, but it only holds up at work when teams adopt shared async norms that respect those blocks.

**Q2**: What evidence does the article cite for time blocking's effectiveness?
**A**: Cal Newport estimates a 40-hour time-blocked week produces the same output as a 60+ hour unstructured week. Microsoft researchers also linked protected calendar focus time to greater work engagement and improved well-being.

**Q3**: How would you make time blocking work across a team rather than just for yourself?
**A**: Agree on response windows for messages, make focus blocks visible through shared calendars or Slack statuses, and consolidate meetings into defined periods to avoid a "Swiss cheese schedule." Also agree on what qualifies as urgent so real emergencies can still get through.
