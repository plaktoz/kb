## Summary

Ten years after publishing [[Deep Work]], [[Cal Newport]] argues in a New York Times essay that the problems he identified have worsened: society isn't just short on time for deep work, it's losing the ability to think deeply at all, as workplace messaging tools, addictive social media, and AI "quick-fix shortcuts" erode sustained cognition. He calls for "a revolution in defense of thinking."

## Core Concepts

- **[[Deep Work]]**: Newport's decade-old framework distinguishing focused, cognitively demanding work from shallow, distracted work — revisited here as still-relevant but insufficient for today's threats.
- **Cognitive shallows**: Newport's term for the state of diminished capacity for sustained thought, driven by compounding distraction sources (Slack, Zoom, social media, AI).
- **AI as a double-edged tool**: AI tools offer shortcuts around intellectually engaging work, which Newport frames as a new threat layered on top of older distraction sources rather than a neutral productivity gain.

## Key Takeaways

- **Newport's core shift in framing**: In 2016 the problem was "not enough time" for deep work; in 2026 the problem is "not enough capacity" to think deeply even when time exists.
- **Concrete proposed actions**: stop consuming social media, keep phones charging away from your body at home, restrict phones/laptops from meetings, and integrate AI only where it makes people smarter (not just busier).
- **Policy angle**: Newport cites Australia's under-16 social media ban as a model for legislative action, not just individual discipline.
- **Call to collective action**: Frames the fix as a "revolution," not a personal productivity hack — implying systemic/cultural change is needed, not just individual willpower.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Tragedy of the Commons]]**: Individual attention is a shared cognitive resource being depleted by platforms optimized for engagement rather than user well-being — Newport's call for collective/policy action (not just personal discipline) mirrors classic commons-protection logic.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A decade after Deep Work, Cal Newport argues society has moved from having too little time for focused thinking to losing the capacity for deep thinking altogether, driven by workplace tools, social media, and now AI.

**Q2**: What specific historical comparison does Newport use to frame the problem's evolution?
**A**: He contrasts his 2016 concern (not enough space in schedules for deep work) with his 2026 concern (rapidly losing the ability to think deeply regardless of available time).

**Q3**: What concrete actions does Newport recommend to counter this trend?
**A**: Stop consuming social media, keep phones charging at home rather than on your person, keep phones/laptops out of meetings, push for policy like banning social media for kids, and integrate AI tools only where they genuinely increase intelligence rather than just busyness.
