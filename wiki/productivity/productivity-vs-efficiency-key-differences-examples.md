## Summary
[[Productivity]] compares valuable output with the input used. [[Efficiency]] compares the resources actually used with a credible standard or alternative. A team can raise output while wasting more time, or cut cost while producing the wrong result, so managers should not treat the terms as synonyms. The guide adds [[Effectiveness]] as a third, separate question and argues for improving value and the system constraint before squeezing cost.

## Core Concepts
- **Three separate questions**:
  - [[Productivity]]: how much acceptable output came from the resources used?
  - [[Efficiency]]: how much time, cost, or effort was used compared with what should have been required?
  - [[Effectiveness]]: did the work achieve the intended result?
- **Productivity formula**: accepted output ÷ input. Counting only *accepted* output keeps the measure from rewarding rework.
- **Labor-efficiency formula**: standard hours for actual output ÷ actual hours × 100. Efficiency can also be expressed as a [[Variance Analysis|time or cost variance]] against standard.
- **Four combinations**: productive and efficient; productive but inefficient (overtime, rework, premium cost); efficient but ineffective (tickets that reopen, content no buyer reads, features no one adopts); and neither productive nor efficient.
- **Buffers and guardrails**: Removing every buffer, e.g. scheduling support at theoretical maximum occupancy, makes queue time spike when volume rises. Pair efficiency targets with quality, service, safety, workload, and turnover guardrails.
- **[[Theory of Constraints|Protect the constraint]]**: Improving a non-bottleneck makes one step look efficient while end-to-end throughput stays the same. The busiest step is not always the constraining step.
- **[[Work in Progress Limits]]**: Limiting WIP and removing blocked or low-value work are levers for productivity.
- **Paired scorecard**: Put each resource measure beside the outcome it can distort, e.g. cases per labor hour with reopen rate.
- **Monitoring limits**: Activity-tracking tools such as [[KeepActive]] can diagnose work-time and app-use patterns, but they cannot measure the value or quality of output.

## Key Takeaways
- **Worked productivity example**: 420 accurate invoices ÷ 210 hours = 2 per labor hour.
- **Rework trap**: Counting all 450 invoices, including 30 corrected, would reward rework.
- **Worked efficiency example**: 252 standard hours ÷ 210 actual = 120% labor efficiency.
- **Efficiency ≠ value**: 120% efficiency doesn't validate the standard or prove business value.
- **State denominators**: "Efficiency improved 12%" is uninterpretable without the formula used.
- **No universal score**: Non-repeatable roles should track a small set of outcomes.
- **Credible baseline**: Use comparable work mix and normal staffing, not quiet months.
- **Standardize selectively**: Templates and automation fit low-variation, high-error-cost work.
- **Tool sprawl**: Copying status between systems is an efficiency problem.
- **Quality at source**: Clear requirements and early feedback beat asking people to hurry.
- **Review segments**: A stable average can hide one overloaded queue.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Theory of Constraints]]**: System throughput is set by its bottleneck, so local efficiency gains elsewhere just build inventory the constraint can't finish. The guide states this directly as "improve the constraint, not the busiest team."
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: Once output counts or efficiency percentages become targets, teams optimize the number and not the value. That is why the guide counts only accepted output and pairs every metric with the outcome it can distort.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why should managers not treat productivity and efficiency as synonyms?
**A**: Productivity measures accepted output per unit of input, while efficiency measures resource use against a credible standard. A team can raise output while wasting more time, or lower cost while producing the wrong result.

**Q2**: How is labor efficiency calculated, and what does a 120% result actually show?
**A**: Labor efficiency is standard hours for actual output ÷ actual hours × 100, e.g. 252 ÷ 210 × 100 = 120%. It shows the team used fewer hours than the standard, but it does not prove the standard is valid or that the work created business value.

**Q3**: If one team in a multi-step process is highly utilized, what should a manager do first?
**A**: Map the flow from demand to accepted outcome, because the busiest step is not always the constraint. Find where work waits longest or piles up, protect that constraint and reduce work entering it, and only then optimize the surrounding steps.
