## Summary
[[Allie Dunin]] argues that she spent years solving the wrong problem. She tried to manage her time better when what she needed was to understand her energy, because "time is the same every day" while energy rises and falls. Matching tasks to her current energy, protecting it, treating rest as productive, and planning for her ordinary days rather than her best ones made her days easier and her plans more realistic.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Energy Management]] over [[Time Management]]**: Planners and colour-coded calendars didn't help on days when she knew what to do but lacked the mental energy to start.
- **Natural energy rhythms**: Energy fluctuates across the day, the week, and the year. Lower-energy days are information, not failures. See [[tapping-into-your-teams-circadian-rhythms]] and [[remote-work-energy-variability-and-productivity]].
- **[[Task-Energy Matching]]**: Replace "What should I do next?" with "What do I have the energy for?" Focused states suit writing and study. Tired states suit email, tidying, walks, and repetitive tasks.
- **Every commitment has a cost**: Each meeting, social event, or extra project draws on attention, time, and emotional capacity, even enjoyable ones. Protecting energy means choosing carefully, not doing less.
- **[[Rest]] as productive**: Walks, reading, earlier bedtimes, and quiet evenings aren't distractions from a productive life. They're part of what makes one possible.
- **Plan for realistic days**: Weekly plans should assume busy days, low-energy afternoons, and reduced capacity. Routines that "expect you to be human" are easier to stick to.
- **Maintenance vs. progress days**: Some days are for keeping life running rather than pushing forward.
- Related: [[redefining-efficiency-ai-age-storoni]], [[time-management-diagnostic-approach]], [[smart-time-management-priorities-over-minutes]]

## Key Takeaways
- **Wrong problem**: Better scheduling didn't fix the days when energy was missing.
- **Core insight**: Time is constant every day, but energy isn't.
- **Unequal hours**: Not every hour is meant for [[Deep Work]]; some suit admin.
- **New question**: Ask "What do I have the energy for?" instead of "What's next?"
- **Every yes costs**: Each commitment shapes what energy remains for everything else.
- **Rest fuels work**: More exhaustion meant less capacity for meaningful work.
- **Realistic planning**: Plans built around low-energy days are more likely to be kept.
- **Overwhelm reframe**: Ask "What's the kindest and most useful way to spend today's energy?"
- **Tools stay**: She still uses calendars and to-do lists, but not as the whole answer.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Planning Fallacy]]**: Her old weekly plans assumed she would wake up motivated every morning, a systematically optimistic estimate of capacity. Planning around busy days and low-energy afternoons corrects that bias, which is why her plans became easier to keep.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: "Every yes shapes what I'll have left for everything else": energy is a finite budget, so even an enjoyable commitment carries the cost of the work or rest it displaces.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Allie Dunin's central argument about time and energy?
**A**: Time management alone was the wrong problem to solve, because time is the same every day while energy is not. Learning to work with your energy, rather than demanding more of it, makes the hours you already have feel different.

**Q2**: How does she decide what to work on at a given moment?
**A**: She asks "What do I have the energy for?" instead of "What should I do next?" She does writing and study when focused, and email, tidying, walks, or repetitive tasks when tired.

**Q3**: How could you apply this approach to weekly planning?
**A**: Build the week assuming there will be busy days, low-energy afternoons, and reduced capacity, rather than planning for your best days. When overwhelmed, ask what the kindest and most useful use of today's energy is, and accept that some days are maintenance days rather than progress days.
