## Summary

Part 1 of a three-part series on [[Staff Work]] argues that great officers make decisions easier to reach — by starting from the actual problem rather than a pre-decided solution, identifying who actually holds the levers to act, surfacing genuine disagreements instead of forcing false consensus, and communicating with the point first and history second ([[Bottom Line Up Front]]).

## Core Concepts

- **[[Problem-First Framing]]** — writing the problem statement before the solution, since papers written backwards from a predetermined answer produce vague problem statements that don't actually distinguish between options
- **[[Bottom Line Up Front]]** — stating the answer, issue, or recommendation before the supporting history, rather than narrating events in the order they were discovered
- **[[Surfacing Disagreement]]** — explaining where a genuine disagreement sits and why it matters to the decision (differing assumptions, differing risk tolerance, differing cost allocation) rather than smoothing it away for a tidy consensus paper
- **[[Levers of Control]]** — identifying who owns the policy, budget, or process before recommending action, since vague coordination language often substitutes for actually naming who needs to decide something

## Key Takeaways

- **Start with the problem, not the solution**: if every possible answer seems to fit the stated problem, the problem is still too vague
- **Know the second and third level of detail** without unloading it all upfront — senior readers often skip straight to the specific number or assumption that worries them
- **Always recommend something**: ending a briefing with "so what should we do?" after days of research shifts the analytical work back onto the reader
- **Say what you need**: approval, a direction, intervention, or just a heads-up — ambiguity here means nothing happens after a meeting that seemed to agree on something
- **Use continuous tense for work already underway** ("I am checking with Finance") rather than future tense, which implies the work hasn't started yet

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Inversion]]**: thinking "one question ahead" — reading a recommendation as both the approver and the person who must carry it out — is a practical form of inversion: instead of only building the case forward, the writer works backward from likely objections and consequences before submitting the work.
