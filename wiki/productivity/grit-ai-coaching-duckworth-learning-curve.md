## Summary

[[Angela Duckworth]] argues [[Grit]] requires being "stubborn at the top" (a fixed long-term goal) but "agile at the bottom" (flexible tactics, including AI use), since staying on a [[Learning Curve]] matters more than raw talent. In a cover-letter-rewriting experiment, participants who used AI wrote better in the moment and also improved their own long-term writing skill more than those who practiced alone or not at all — because watching AI's output functioned like observing a great coach, per the [[Wooden Learning Model]] of explanation, demonstration, imitation, repetition.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Grit]]** — passion and perseverance for long-term goals; Duckworth's four-item scale measures perseverance and consistency of interest
- **[[Learning Curve]]** — Anders Ericsson's concept that sustained improvement, not talent, separates world-class performers
- **[[Effort vs. Learning Environment Tradeoff]]** — AI use reduces effort (fewer keystrokes) but can improve the learning environment through demonstration
- **[[Wooden Learning Model]]** — explanation, demonstration, imitation, repetition; applied to learning from AI outputs
- **[[Situational Agency]]** — the idea that designing your environment (coach, team, culture) matters as much as personal grit, illustrated via Michael Phelps and coach Bob Bowman

## Key Takeaways

- **Cover-letter experiment surprise**: AI-assisted participants' own writing skill improved more than unaided practice, both immediately and a week later.
- **Effort recalibration works both ways**: reduced effort from AI use is offset by higher-quality demonstration-based learning.
- **3 out of 4 Gen Z adults used AI in the last month** as of October, mainly for productivity, per Walton Family Foundation/Gallup data.
- **Delegate low-learning-curve tasks**: freeing time for higher-value work is the key discipline, not avoiding AI.
- **AI ambivalence can be rational**: concerns about accountability gaps and hallucinations are legitimate, not just resistance to change.
