## Summary

A University of Iowa study tracking 219 hybrid workers over one business week found that employees were more productive on work-from-home days than on office days, primarily because home environments produced 33% less energy variability throughout the day. The mechanism is not more total energy at home, but fewer and lower-quality interruptions — fewer co-worker drop-ins, noise, and commute stress — giving workers more agency over their own focus cycles. The study recommends [[Hybrid Work]] as a practical employer-employee compromise to lift productivity.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Energy Variability]]** — fluctuations in a worker's hourly energy level; high variability (typical in offices) correlates with worse goal progress
- **[[Hybrid Work]]** — the blended arrangement studied here; researchers suggest it captures home-day productivity gains without full remote's isolation risks
- **[[Deep Work]]** — the focused, uninterrupted state that consistent energy enables; home workers can more easily enter and sustain it
- **[[Emily Campion]]** — study co-author, associate professor of management and entrepreneurship at the Tippie College of Business, University of Iowa
- **[[Workplace Interruptions]]** — co-worker drop-ins, cubicle noise, difficult commutes; drain more energy than typical at-home distractions

## Key Takeaways

- **Home = more consistent energy**: WFH days had 33% less energy variability than office days.
- **Variability is the productivity killer**: each 1% reduction in energy variability yielded 26% higher goal progress.
- **Quality of interruptions matters**: office interruptions drain more energy than home distractions (laundry, pets).
- **Agency over attention**: WFH lets workers ignore email or silence Slack to protect focused work.
- **Frequency compounds the gain**: the more often workers were at home, the higher their productivity climbed.
- **Hybrid as compromise**: study recommends hybrid to balance employee preference for WFH with employer preference for in-office.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Attention Residue]]**: each co-worker interruption in the office leaves cognitive "residue" that slows re-engagement — exactly what Campion describes when noting the energy cost of transitioning away from and back into deep tasks.
- **[[Autonomy and Intrinsic Motivation]]**: when workers can control what disrupts them (turning off Slack, ignoring emails), they regulate their own energy more effectively — consistent energy is a direct by-product of perceived autonomy over one's environment.

**Q1**: What is the central finding of this University of Iowa study on remote work productivity?
**A**: Hybrid workers are more productive on home days than office days because WFH produces 33% less energy variability, enabling greater goal progress and faster work pace.

**Q2**: What specific mechanism links energy variability to productivity, and what statistic quantifies it?
**A**: High energy variability — caused by office interruptions like co-worker drop-ins and cubicle noise — disrupts task transitions and reduces focus; each 1 percentage point reduction in energy variability corresponds to 26% higher goal progress.

**Q3**: How should employers apply this research, and why is hybrid work specifically recommended?
**A**: Employers should consider hybrid arrangements to boost productivity: WFH days deliver the energy-consistency benefit workers need for deep work, while some office days preserve collaboration and presence without forfeiting the entire productivity gain.
