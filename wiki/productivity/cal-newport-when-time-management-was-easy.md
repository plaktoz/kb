## Summary

Cal Newport revisits Alan Lakein's 1973 ABC task-priority system and argues that its simplicity — a single finite list you could realistically complete — is impossible to replicate today, not because the system was flawed, but because modern work generates far more input, with far more urgency, than any list-based system was designed to absorb.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Alan Lakein]] / ABC Method**: A 1973 time-management system labeling tasks A (important/urgent), B (important/not urgent), C (small/easy), worked in strict priority order.
- **[[Task List Finitude]]**: Lakein's system assumed a list that could hold everything you needed to do and stay roughly stable through the day — an assumption that modern email/chat volume makes untenable.
- **[[Slow Productivity]]**: Newport's own proposed remedy — reforming office work itself so that something as simple as Lakein's system becomes sufficient again, rather than adding ever more complex time-management tooling.

## Key Takeaways

- Lakein's book sold 3+ million copies and was cited by Bill Clinton as an early-career influence.
- The ABC method: complete all A tasks, then B, then C; priorities can be re-labeled as deadlines approach.
- What's "shocking" in hindsight is the assumption of a stable, finite list — a single piece of paper could hold everything.
- Newport argues the mismatch between old systems and modern workloads is an indictment of how work has changed, not of the old systems.
- His stated goal is reducing the *need* for complex time-management systems by reforming office work itself, rather than building ever-better coping mechanisms.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Older time-management systems like Lakein's ABC method look almost quaint today not because they were bad advice, but because modern work generates far more volume and urgency than any finite task list can absorb.

**Q2**: What specific assumption did Lakein's system make that no longer holds?
**A**: That a person's full workload could fit on a single list and remain relatively stable throughout the day — an assumption broken by constant new inputs like email, chat, and meeting requests.

**Q3**: What is Newport's proposed fix, and how does it differ from typical productivity advice?
**A**: Rather than adopting a more complex time-management system, Newport argues for reforming office work itself (his "Slow Productivity" thesis) so that simple systems become adequate again — treating the volume of work as the problem, not the tools used to manage it.
