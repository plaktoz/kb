## Summary

US employees who use AI at work mostly report gains in speed (63%) and creativity (56%), with smaller shares reporting higher-quality work or more time on interesting tasks. These benefits are unevenly distributed: regular AI use is far higher among workers already more likely to hold quality jobs, such as college graduates and managers, and even among AI users, graduates, younger and higher-paid workers report more benefits. The findings come from year two of the [[American Job Quality Study]], which asks whether AI's gains will spread across the workforce or stay concentrated among already-advantaged workers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[American Job Quality Study]] (AJQS)**: A partnership between [[Jobs for the Future]], [[The Families & Workers Fund]] and [[Gallup]]; the full year-two report is due late October 2026.
- **Survey design**: Conducted Jan. 26 to March 24, 2026. AI-effect findings draw on 7,845 employees who used AI at least a few times; job-quality comparisons use the full 15,482-employee sample.
- **Self-reported benefits**: Findings reflect employees' own assessments, not employer evaluations of AI's effects.
- **[[AI Adoption]] gaps by role and industry**: About half of workers in professional and business services, financial activities and information/media use AI regularly. In healthcare support, manufacturing production and retail sales, 70% to over 80% never use it.
- **[[Job Quality]]**: Measured with multidimensional criteria; regular AI users are much more likely to hold a quality job.
- **[[Responsibility Creep]]**: 31% of AI users say AI led their employer to give them more responsibilities, rising to 38% among 18-to-24-year-olds.
- **[[Worker Agency and Voice]]**: One of five AJQS job-quality dimensions; 52% of employees want more influence over how new technology is adopted.
- **Confounding caveat**: The groups that tend to hold quality jobs (educated workers, managers, certain industries) also use AI more, so causation remains unclear.

## Key Takeaways

- **Speed leads**: 63% of AI users say it helps them work faster.
- **Creativity second**: 56% say AI helps them find more creative solutions.
- **Quality lags**: 46% report higher-quality work; 47% more time on interesting work.
- **Not yet the norm**: 28% use AI weekly or more; 54% have never used it.
- **Education gap**: 40% of college grads use AI regularly vs 17% of non-grads.
- **Manager gap**: 37% of managers are regular users vs 25% of individual contributors.
- **Job-quality link**: 52% of weekly users hold quality jobs vs 32% of non-users.
- **Frequency matters**: Regular users report faster work 79% vs 38% for irregular users.
- **Pay gap**: 70% of $10k+/month earners report speed gains vs 58% under $4k.
- **Age split**: Under-55 users report speed gains at 64% vs 54% for those 55+.
- **More work**: 37% of users who work faster are handed more responsibilities.
- **Voice deficit**: 52% want more say over technology adoption.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Matthew Effect]]**: AI's reported benefits accrue most to educated, managerial and higher-paid workers who already hold quality jobs, so an early advantage compounds rather than levels out.
- **[[Correlation vs Causation]]**: Weekly AI users are more likely to hold quality jobs, but those same subgroups have quality jobs anyway, so AI use cannot yet be credited with causing better jobs.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of Gallup's AJQS report on AI at work?
**A**: AI users mainly report speed and creativity gains, but both AI use and its benefits are concentrated among workers already more likely to hold quality jobs, such as college graduates, managers and higher earners.

**Q2**: How large is the AI usage gap between college graduates and non-graduates, and between managers and individual contributors?
**A**: 40% of college graduates use AI daily or weekly versus 17% of non-graduates. 37% of managers are regular users versus 25% of individual contributors.

**Q3**: What levers does Gallup suggest will decide whether AI's benefits spread more broadly?
**A**: Decisions about job design, worker influence and implementation will shape the outcome. This matters because 52% of employees say they have less influence over new technology adoption than they would like.
