## Summary

Part 2 of the [[Staff Work]] series turns from thinking-through-the-issue to producing the actual package — papers, emails, and slides — arguing that inconvenient facts belong in the main document (not buried in an annex), approval requests should be written literally and itemized, and slide decks should be organized around an argument (What's the problem? What's causing it? What does success look like? How do we solve it?) rather than around topic headers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Put the Inconvenient Stuff Upfront]]** — any fact, uncertainty, or trade-off that could change a decision belongs in the main paper, not an annex, since burying it there only makes the recommendation look tidier without actually resolving the risk
- **[[Literal Approval Paragraphs]]** — itemizing exactly what's being approved (a, b, c) since approval language often gets copied verbatim into meeting minutes and decision records
- **[[Title-as-Argument Slides]]** — writing slide titles that state a conclusion ("Current approval process adds six weeks without changing the risk") instead of a category label ("Assessment"), so reading only the titles reveals the argument
- **[[The Ten-Slide Constraint]]** — forcing an elevator-pitch deck to ten slides or fewer to surface the key argument, while still holding much deeper supporting detail in reserve for questions

## Key Takeaways

- **A cover note should orient, not duplicate**: it explains why a paper is coming up now and where the hard part is, without becoming a mini copy of the whole submission
- **Subject lines should do work**: showing the approving authority, deadline, and topic prevents generic subjects like "For approval pls" from becoming useless in a crowded inbox
- **Read only the titles test**: if a deck's titles read like a table of contents rather than an argument, it's organized by topic instead of by point
- **Orient the room before showing a complex chart**: explain what the rows, columns, and key pattern mean before diving into the conclusion
- **Read documents backwards before sending**: reading sentence-by-sentence in reverse interrupts the brain's tendency to autocorrect what it expects to see, surfacing missing words and repeated phrases
