## Summary

Cal Newport distinguishes "light" procrastination (everyday email/TV avoidance) from "deep" procrastination, arguing the latter isn't caused by disliking the work itself but by an overloaded schedule that breeds resentment — and that the fix is doing much less, not adopting stricter productivity systems.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Light vs. Deep Procrastination]]**: Light procrastination is ordinary distraction-avoidance, fixable with simple habits; deep procrastination is needing last-minute pressure just to start at all, producing all-nighters and extension requests.
- **[[Schedule Overload]]**: The proposed root cause of deep procrastination — too much work and too little time forces repeated unpleasant late-night work sessions.
- **[[Resentment Cycle]]**: Once work becomes associated with suffering, motivation collapses even though the same material, done at a comfortable pace, would satisfy the basic need for competence.
- **[[Competence (Psychological Need)]]**: Mastering material over time is intrinsically satisfying — the enemy isn't the subject matter but the pace forced by an overloaded calendar.

## Key Takeaways

- The common explanation ("the work itself is unpleasant") doesn't hold up: given unlimited time and no competing courses, most students would find the same work enjoyable.
- Stricter schedules and more intense productivity rules don't cure deep procrastination, because the problem is resentment, not disorganization.
- The fix is counterintuitive: making the schedule lighter (which should make procrastination easier) instead removes the fatigue and resentment that caused procrastination in the first place.
- Students who shift to lighter schedules regain genuine interest in their work, and interested people don't struggle to start.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Deep procrastination is caused by an overloaded schedule that builds resentment toward work, not by disliking the subject matter or lacking discipline — so the fix is reducing total workload, not tightening productivity systems.

**Q2**: What evidence does Newport use to argue against the "the work itself is unpleasant" explanation?
**A**: He asks readers to imagine the same assignment with no other courses competing for time and no deadline pressure — under those conditions, most people would find mastering the material genuinely satisfying, showing the problem is pace, not content.

**Q3**: How would someone apply this to a real scheduling decision?
**A**: Instead of adding a stricter to-do system to fight procrastination, they'd cut total commitments until the remaining workload can be done without nightly resentment — trading short-term "more capacity for last-minute heroics" for a schedule that no longer needs heroics at all.
