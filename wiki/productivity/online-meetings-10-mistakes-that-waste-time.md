## Summary
Online meetings are cheap to schedule but expensive to attend, so an effective meeting starts by asking whether a live conversation is needed at all and, if so, defining a clear outcome, attendee roles, and agenda in advance. The article lists ten common mistakes — from meeting by default and vague invites to inaccessible participation, ungoverned recordings, and ending without owners and deadlines — and pairs them with a before/during/after checklist. It extends the advice to organisation-level meeting policies, shared ownership across leaders, HR, IT, and legal, and process-level measures of meeting improvement.

## Core Concepts
- **Meet vs. write**: Use a live meeting for discussion, problem-solving, or decisions; use [[Asynchronous Communication]] (written updates, shared status pages) when people only need the same information.
- **Outcome-based invites**: "Choose the product launch date" beats "Team catch-up"; invites should state reason, outcome, preparation, and roles.
- **Agenda per item**: Topic and question, lead, time allotted, purpose (information/discussion/decision), and preparation.
- **Role-based invite lists**: Mark attendees as required, optional, or for awareness; those who only need the outcome get notes afterwards.
- **Intentional duration**: Size the slot to the agenda rather than calendar defaults; leave buffers; check fairness across [[Time Zones]].
- **Inclusive participation**: Captions, accessible pre-shared materials, describing visuals aloud, chat/poll/round-robin input — silence is not agreement.
- **Recording governance**: Tell attendees when recording or transcription is on, limit access, apply retention rules; a human owner must verify [[AI Meeting Notes]] before sharing.
- **Close with decisions**: State what was decided, what remains open, one named owner per action, deadlines, and where notes live.
- **[[Meeting Policy]]**: Usually coordinated by HR with IT, legal/compliance, and senior leaders who model the habits.

## Key Takeaways
- **Workload pressure**: 80% of 31,000 workers lack time or energy for their work.
- **Cross-time-zone meetings**: 30% of Microsoft-analysed meetings span time zones, up 8 points since 2021.
- **Person-hour cost**: A 30-minute call with eight people costs four person-hours.
- **Status-call example**: Twelve-person weekly hour uses 12 person-hours, then needs a second meeting.
- **Ask first**: Does the group need discussion, or just the same information?
- **Pre-reads over slides**: Share background beforehand; use live time for reactions and decisions.
- **Decision-maker present**: Missing approvers turn decisions into repeat meetings.
- **Named owners**: "Someone will update" stalls; "Jordan by Thursday" gets done.
- **Prune recurring meetings**: Replace status calls with written updates when discussion isn't needed.
- **Measure process, not people**: Track clear invites, on-time finishes, owners, completed actions.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Live meeting time is multiplied across every attendee** — Each minute of a synchronous meeting is paid by every person present and displaces their other work, so a meeting is justified only when it needs real-time interaction rather than information transfer. · *model:* [[Opportunity Cost]]
→ explains: Person-hour cost, Status-call example, Ask first, Pre-reads over slides, Prune recurring meetings

**T2. Shared responsibility without a named owner goes undone** — When an action is assigned to the group ("someone will"), no individual feels bound to it, so it stalls and the issue returns; a single owner and deadline restore accountability. · *model:* [[Diffusion of Responsibility]]
→ explains: Named owners, Decision-maker present

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core argument about running effective online meetings?
**A**: A meeting needs a clear reason to happen and a format that lets people contribute; teams should first check whether a live discussion is needed, then define outcome, attendees, and agenda, and close with confirmed decisions and owners.

**Q2**: How does the article illustrate the cost of an unnecessary meeting?
**A**: It multiplies duration by attendees — a 30-minute call with eight people is four person-hours, and a twelve-person weekly hour is 12 person-hours, before counting preparation, follow-up, or a second meeting when the decision-maker was absent.

**Q3**: How would you apply this to a recurring weekly status call?
**A**: Move routine updates to a shared written status page, then hold a short focused meeting only with the people needed to discuss or decide issues, ending with a named owner, deadline, and shared notes.
