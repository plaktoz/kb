## Summary

Time blocking divides the workday into task-specific blocks to counter fragmented, chaotic schedules, and comes in three variants: timeboxing, task batching, and day theming. The article gives a five-step setup process, lists situations where the method struggles, and argues managers should add shared labeling, meeting limits, coaching, and time-tracking metrics.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Time Blocking]]** — dividing the day into blocks, each assigned a specific task or set of tasks
- **[[Timeboxing]]** — a stricter variant: fixed limit on task duration, declared done when time is up
- **[[Task Batching]]** — grouping similar tasks into one block to reduce [[Context Switching]]
- **[[Day Theming]]** — dedicating whole days to a task type, energy level, or completion time
- **[[Deep Work]]** — focus time the method aims to protect from meetings and notifications
- **[[Microsoft Work Trend Index]]** — source of the 48% "chaotic and fragmented" work statistic
- **[[Hubstaff]]** — time-tracking tool used to get accurate task estimates and productivity metrics
- **[[Time Tracking]]** — gives task-level data for capacity planning and coaching

## Key Takeaways

- **Fragmentation**: 48% of employees say work feels chaotic and fragmented.
- **Focus time**: The average employee gets just 2-3 hours of deep focus daily.
- **Context switching**: Switching tasks can cost employees up to 40% of the productive day.
- **Timeboxing specificity**: Define the output ("500-word introduction"), not just the duration.
- **Estimate buffer**: Give yourself 1.5x to 2x the time you expect to need.
- **Setup order**: Block recurring tasks first, then priorities, breaks, and buffers.
- **Failure modes**: Reactive roles, unpredictable workloads, and teammates ignoring blocks undermine it.
- **Manager role**: Shared labels distinguish protected focus time from flexible blocks.
- **Meeting load**: About 25 meetings monthly; 60% are ad-hoc and disrupt plans.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Task switching destroys productive capacity** — Every task switch is a context switch, and the accumulated switching overhead consumes a large share of the working day; grouping similar work reduces it. · *model:* [[Context-Switching Cost]]
→ explains: Context switching, Setup order

**T2. Plans need slack to survive unplanned demand** — Last-minute requests and delays inevitably arise, so back-to-back scheduling fails; buffers and generous estimates preserve the plan.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the article about time blocking?
**A**: Dividing the day into task-specific blocks turns fragmented, chaotic days into structured schedules that protect deep work and raise productivity. It can be done via timeboxing, task batching, or day theming depending on role and working style.

**Q2**: Why does task batching go "a step further" than basic time blocking?
**A**: Switching tasks means switching contexts, which can cost up to 40% of the productive day. Batching groups similar tasks into one block so fewer switches occur.

**Q3**: How should you build a time-blocked week, and where does it break down?
**A**: Identify peak times, estimate generously (1.5x-2x), choose a tool, block recurring tasks first, then fill priorities with breaks and buffers. It struggles in reactive roles, with unpredictable workloads, or when teammates schedule over blocked time.
