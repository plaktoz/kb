## Summary
Switching away from an unfinished task leaves "attention residue" that degrades performance on the next task, so focus depends on how you structure blocks and transitions. The article's research-backed core is a short written "ready-to-resume" plan before switching, paired with restorative breaks and personal experimentation with block length, while flagging the 52/17 rule as a rule of thumb rather than a proven optimum.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Attention Residue]]**: Part of attention stays on an unfinished prior task, per [[Sophie Leroy]]'s research, and hurts performance on the next one.
- **[[Ready-to-Resume Plan]]**: A brief written note of where you stopped and what you will do on return; shown by Leroy and Glomb to reduce residue.
- **[[Deep Work]]**: [[Cal Newport]]'s distinction between cognitively demanding work and shallow logistical work.
- **[[Attention Restoration Theory]]**: [[Stephen Kaplan]]'s idea that "soft fascination", especially in nature, lets directed attention recover.
- **[[Focus Block]]**: A protected window of single-task work; the 52-minute block is a practitioner heuristic, not a trial result.
- **[[Temporal Batching]]**: Designating interruptible hours and protecting the rest.
- **[[Smartphone Presence Effect]]**: Ward and colleagues found a phone within reach reduces available cognitive capacity.

## Key Takeaways
- **Residue Cost**: Leaving a task unfinished makes the next task worse.
- **Time Pressure**: Residue is strongest when you expect time pressure on returning (Leroy and Glomb).
- **Ready-to-Resume**: A written stopping-point plan reduced residue and improved performance.
- **52/17 Caveat**: Comes from time-tracking of high performers, not controlled trials.
- **Deep vs. Shallow**: Uninterrupted email is not deep work; match blocks to hard tasks.
- **Soft Fascination**: Nature walks beat urban walks on a directed-attention task (Berman).
- **Break Quality**: Social media scrolling likely keeps taxing attention.
- **Personalize**: Track focus, output, errors; adjust block length in 10-minute steps.
- **Batching**: End blocks 5 minutes before known interruptions; batch unpredictable ones.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Unfinished tasks keep consuming attention** — Switching away from an incomplete task leaves part of cognitive capacity attached to it, degrading the next task, and closing the loop explicitly (a written resume plan) releases it. · *model:* [[Attention Residue]]

**T2. Directed attention is a depletable resource that recovers through low-demand engagement** — Recovery comes from activities that don't demand directed attention, not from further decision-heavy stimulation. · *model:* [[Attention Restoration Theory]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is attention residue and why does it matter?
**A**: It is the part of your attention that stays on an unfinished prior task after you switch. It makes you perform worse on the next task, especially when you expect time pressure on returning to the first.

**Q2**: What evidence supports the "ready-to-resume" plan, and what does it contain?
**A**: Leroy and Glomb found that writing a brief plan before switching reduced residue and improved performance on the interrupting task. The plan notes where you stopped and what you will do when you return.

**Q3**: How should you handle unavoidable task switches?
**A**: Where interruptions are predictable, end the focus block 5 minutes early and use that time for a transition ritual. Where they are unpredictable, use temporal batching, with set interruptible hours and fully protected hours.
