## Summary

An [[National Bureau of Economic Research|NBER]] working paper led by Harvard's [[David Deming]], drawing on data from about 9,000 workers, finds meetings consume 4.7 hours a week (12% of the workweek, ~14% of payroll) yet are linked to higher wages, revenue, and on-the-job learning. Deming argues meetings function as investments in [[Organizational Capital]] — a coordination technology for increasingly specialized knowledge work and a channel for developing [[Human Capital]]. For HR leaders, the takeaway is to treat meeting policy as an organizational-design question about which meetings create value, not to cut meetings as an end in itself.

## Core Concepts

- **[[David Deming]]**: Harvard Kennedy School professor and lead author, who calls meetings the "broccoli" of work — "widely disliked, but probably good for us anyway."
- **[[Knowledge Specialization]]**: As firms rely on employees with specialized knowledge, workers need settings to exchange information and coordinate — which meetings provide.
- **Meetings as Coordination Technology**: Expensive because they consume several workers' time at once, but a natural venue for coordinating specialized production.
- **[[Organizational Capital]]**: The researchers conclude the evidence is most consistent with meetings acting as investments in this capital.
- **[[Human Capital]]**: Meetings are also a channel through which firms facilitate learning and develop employees.
- **[[Remote and Hybrid Work]]**: Dispersed teams make cross-expertise meetings more important, distinct from recurring status meetings that merely relay information.
- **Organizational Design vs. Productivity Framing**: Meeting policy should ask whether meetings solve problems and share knowledge, or compensate for unclear processes.

## Key Takeaways

- **Time cost**: Average 4.7 hours/week in meetings, about 12% of the workweek.
- **Payroll cost**: Meeting time equals roughly 14% of an organization's payroll costs.
- **Common activities**: Planning/strategy, problem solving, project updates, information sharing.
- **Firm-level link**: Meeting-intensive firms show higher wages, revenue, and revenue per worker.
- **Worker-level link**: More meeting time tracks faster wage growth and more on-the-job learning.
- **Counterintuitive signal**: Firms with the highest opportunity cost of worker time meet more, not less.
- **Don't target reduction**: Meeting reduction should not be an objective in itself.
- **AI angle**: As AI absorbs routine tasks, human interaction's value may rise.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Revealed Preference]]**: Firms whose worker time is most expensive choose to hold more meetings, so their behavior reveals that they value meetings above their cost — despite widespread complaints about them.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: A meeting's true cost is the combined alternative output of every attendee, which is why the question shifts from "how many meetings" to "which meetings return more than that forgone work."

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of Deming's NBER research on meetings?
**A**: Despite being costly and unpopular, meetings are associated with higher wages, revenue, revenue per worker, and on-the-job learning, consistent with meetings functioning as investments in organizational capital.

**Q2**: What data quantifies the cost of meetings in the study?
**A**: Across about 9,000 workers, employees spend an average of 4.7 hours a week in meetings — about 12% of a standard workweek — representing roughly 14% of payroll costs.

**Q3**: How should HR leaders apply this research?
**A**: Rather than simply adding or cutting meetings, they should assess which meetings solve problems, coordinate specialized work, or develop employees versus those that only compensate for unclear processes or relay status updates.
