## Summary

Cal Newport argues that sustaining deep work over hours requires periodic "deep breaks" — restful pauses that let the mind recharge without creating attention residue or new obligations. Unlike ordinary breaks (email, social media), deep breaks avoid targets that generate obligations, trigger distraction habits, or introduce new stress, and should stay under 10-15 minutes.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Attention Residue]]**: The lingering cognitive pull from a prior task or stimulus that impairs focus when returning to demanding work — most ordinary breaks create it.
- **[[Deep Break]]**: A restorative pause designed to avoid attention residue by steering clear of obligations, ingrained distraction rituals, unrelated professional tasks, and complicated topics.
- **[[Deep Work]]**: Newport's broader concept of sustained, high-intensity focus, which this piece treats as inherently punctuated by necessary recovery periods rather than one unbroken block.

## Key Takeaways

- Few people sustain peak cognitive intensity for more than about an hour without relief.
- Deep breaks should avoid email, social media, and known distraction-ritual sites.
- Avoid unrelated professional tasks or stressful topics during a deep break.
- Keep deep breaks to 10-15 minutes, with exceptions like meals.
- Good examples: short walks, daydreaming about success, summarizing progress, unrelated reading.
- Deep breaks are implicit in scheduled deep work blocks, not separately scheduled.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Attention Residue]]**: The core mechanism explaining why not all breaks are equal — a break that pulls attention toward an open obligation (like checking email) leaves a cognitive trace that slows the return to focused work, while a break with no lingering obligation does not.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the article?
**A**: Sustained deep work requires periodic "deep breaks" that restore focus without generating attention residue, and skill at taking these breaks well is what separates people who can sustain long deep work sessions from those who burn out.

**Q2**: What specific mechanism makes some breaks harmful to subsequent focus?
**A**: Breaks that touch a target generating an unfulfilled obligation (like glancing at email) or trigger an ingrained distraction ritual leave "attention residue" that impairs the ability to quickly ramp back up concentration.

**Q3**: How would someone apply this to a four-hour deep work block?
**A**: They wouldn't schedule breaks separately, but would take spontaneous 10-15 minute deep breaks as needed within the block — such as a short walk or daydreaming about the work's payoff — while avoiding email, social media, or unrelated stressful tasks.
