## Summary

Katie Parrott examines why certain AI workflows become indispensable while others get abandoned, using her own failed experiments as case studies. She identifies four core failure modes — wrong problem fit, aspirational self-building, maintenance cost exceeding value, and lack of immediate reward — and proposes a structured "Agent Ops" audit to keep only workflows that earn their place. Her central insight is that immediate rewards predict workflow persistence better than delayed ones.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI Workflow Persistence]]** — the conditions under which an AI-augmented process survives regular use vs. gets quietly dropped
- **[[Symbolic Self-Completion]]** — a psychological concept where people adopt the symbols of an identity they aspire to rather than doing the actual work; drives aspirational tool-building
- **[[Immediate vs. Delayed Rewards]]** — research by [[Woolley and Fishbach]] showing immediate rewards predict habit persistence better than delayed rewards
- **[[Agent Ops Audit]]** — a structured review framework (Keep / Redesign / Revisit later / Retire) to evaluate each AI workflow
- **[[Context and Memory Architecture]]** — lessons learned from maintaining a personal AI assistant about how context windows and persistent memory affect tool value

## Key Takeaways

- **Wrong Problem**: Building tools for a workday you don't actually have is the #1 failure mode.
- **Aspirational Building**: Tools built for your ideal self often go unused — symbolic self-completion at work.
- **Maintenance Ceiling**: A workflow's value must exceed the cost of keeping it alive.
- **Immediate Reward Rule**: Workflows that pay off quickly get used; delayed-payoff tools get abandoned.
- **Agent Ops Audit**: Assign each workflow one of four outcomes — Keep, Redesign, Revisit later, Retire.
- **Manual-First Rule**: Run any automation manually three times before scheduling it.
- **5-Minute Review Cap**: Output must require under five minutes of review to be sustainable.
- **Retire Trigger**: Four unused runs should force a keep-or-retire decision.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Symbolic Self-Completion]]**: People reach for the tools and symbols of a productive identity rather than the output itself — Parrott's aspirational AI builds failed because they served an imagined self, not her actual workflows.
- **[[Immediate Reinforcement]]**: Woolley and Fishbach's finding that immediate rewards drive persistence more than delayed ones explains why the compound writing plugin survived while other tools didn't — it closed the feedback loop instantly.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument about why AI workflows fail?
**A**: Most AI workflows fail for one of four reasons: they solve the wrong problem, they are built for an aspirational rather than actual self, their maintenance cost exceeds their value, or they lack immediate rewards to drive persistent use.

**Q2**: What does research by Woolley and Fishbach show about reward timing and habit persistence?
**A**: Their research shows that immediate rewards predicted persistence better than delayed rewards — meaning workflows that pay off right away are far more likely to become lasting habits.

**Q3**: How can the Agent Ops Audit framework be applied in practice?
**A**: Review each AI workflow and assign it one of four outcomes — Keep, Redesign, Revisit later, or Retire — then apply the provisional rules: run automations manually three times before scheduling, cap review time at five minutes, and retire any tool after four unused runs.
