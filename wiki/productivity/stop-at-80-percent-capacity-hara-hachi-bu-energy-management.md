## Summary
Thanh Pham argues for stopping at 80% capacity, borrowing the Japanese eating principle Hara Hachi Bu, so that held-back margin covers rest, creativity and emergencies. The approach combines saying no more often, capping deep focus at two hours, naming three priorities before checking the inbox, and adding daily buffer blocks. Energy management, not just time management, is what makes performance sustainable.

## Core Concepts
- [[Hara Hachi Bu]]: Japanese dietary principle of eating until 80% full, applied to work capacity.
- [[Energy Management]] vs. [[Time Management]]: the T Framework covers Time, Energy and Intention.
- [[Deep Work]]: two hours of deep focus a day as the "gold standard".
- [[Saying No]]: sorting requests so low-value asks are declined.
- [[Burnout]]: the result of pushing toward 110% capacity.
- [[Buffer Blocks]]: 30-minute end-of-day blocks for admin, email and loose ends.
- [[Daily Prioritization]]: three urgent and important tasks get 80% of energy.

## Key Takeaways
- **Two-Hour Target**: Two hours of deep focus is the realistic standard; four to eight is overcommitting.
- **Say No More**: Decline low-value requests; saying yes to everything makes nothing important.
- **Margin Has a Job**: Held-back capacity covers rest, creativity and unexpected emergencies.
- **Energy Over Time**: Time management fills hours; energy management preserves reserve.
- **Three Before Inbox**: Name three priorities before email or Slack, or others set them.
- **Leave Something Undone**: Leave earlier or defer a non-critical task for a week.
- **Buffer Block**: Reserve the last 30 minutes for admin so work stops bleeding into evenings.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Reserve capacity absorbs shocks and sustains performance** — Running at full load leaves nothing for rest, creativity or the unexpected, whereas held-back margin lets you handle urgency without depletion. · *model:* [[Margin of Safety]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim?
**A**: You should stop at 80% capacity, borrowing Hara Hachi Bu, because the held-back margin covers rest, creativity and emergencies and prevents burnout. Chasing maximum output eventually burns you out.

**Q2**: How does the article distinguish time management from energy management?
**A**: Time management fills the hours on the calendar, while energy management keeps a reserve so important work and unexpected urgencies both get done with energy left. The 80% rule is energy management.

**Q3**: How can you start applying the 80% rule tomorrow?
**A**: Leave a little earlier or defer one non-critical task for a week, name your three priorities before checking email or Slack, and add a 30-minute buffer block at day's end.
