## Summary

The author found an AI-built schedule looked perfect yet left her more scattered, and links this to ActivTrak data showing AI users work longer days. She argues that speed gains get absorbed by more tasks, and that humans must still decide what their time is for, what is enough, and when their energy is best. A schedule should be treated as a draft, not a contract.

## Core Concepts

- [[Efficiency Paradox]]: faster task completion often leads to taking on more rather than gaining free time.
- [[ActivTrak Productivity Lab]] research: 9,140 knowledge workers; AI users had workdays up to 90 minutes longer; heavy users switched apps about 15% more.
- [[Energy Management]]: matching demanding work to the hours when mental clarity is highest.
- [[Boundaries]]: deciding when the workday ends and what to commit to, even when time technically exists.
- [[Patterns vs. Priorities]]: AI can learn habits but not whether they reflect values.
- [[AI-Assisted Planning]]: use AI after reflecting on priorities, energy, and what to say no to.

## Key Takeaways

- **Longer Days**: ActivTrak found AI users' workdays up to 90 minutes longer; causation is unproven.
- **Efficiency Paradox**: Saved time gets filled with more meetings and more email replies.
- **Two Human Decisions**: Decide what time is for, and know what is enough.
- **Habits Are Not Values**: Night-time emailing does not mean you want evenings devoted to work.
- **Energy Mismatch**: AI scheduled deep work at 3 p.m., the author's low-energy hour.
- **Schedule as Draft**: A plan should support life, leaving room for rest and recovery.
- **Three Pre-Planning Questions**: What matters most, when is energy best, what to protect or decline.
- **Awareness Before Strategy**: Ask whether routines support the life you want before optimizing them.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Efficiency gains get absorbed by more demand** — When a task becomes cheaper or faster, people tend to take on more of it rather than bank the savings, so total load can rise. · *model:* [[Jevons Paradox]]
→ explains: Efficiency Paradox, Longer Days

**T2. Define the goal before optimizing the means** — Optimizing a schedule is only useful once you know which priorities, energy patterns, and limits it should serve; otherwise efficiency pursues the wrong ends.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the author's core argument about AI-generated schedules?
**A**: A perfectly organized AI schedule is not necessarily one that fits your life. People must decide their priorities, what is enough, and their energy patterns, and treat the plan as a draft.

**Q2**: What evidence does the article cite on AI and workday length?
**A**: ActivTrak's study of 9,140 knowledge workers found AI users had workdays up to 90 minutes longer, and heavy users switched apps about 15% more with shorter focus periods. The author notes it does not prove AI causes longer days.

**Q3**: How should you use AI when planning your week?
**A**: First answer three questions: what matters most this week, when you have the energy for it, and what to protect or say no to. Then ask AI to build a realistic schedule around those answers.
