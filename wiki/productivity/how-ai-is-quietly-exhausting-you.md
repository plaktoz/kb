## Summary

AI coding tools shorten the path to an answer but replace deep-focus problem solving with dozens of rapid micro-decisions, driving "AI brain fry" and decision fatigue. BCG research shows productivity peaks at two AI tools and drops with a fourth, while Berkeley research found AI-driven time savings get reinvested into more work rather than rest — a phenomenon called "workload creep."

## Core Concepts

[[Decision Fatigue]] is amplified by AI tools like Cursor and GitHub Copilot, which replace reflective research with continuous accept/reject/regenerate micro-decisions. [[Boston Consulting Group|BCG]] research found 14% of high-oversight AI users experience "[[AI Brain Fry]]," correlating with job-change intent and more mistakes. [[METR]] data shows experienced developers using AI tools actually work more slowly despite believing they're 25% faster — an illustration of the broader [[Productivity Paradox]]. UC Berkeley's concept of "[[Workload Creep]]" describes how AI-driven time savings get reinvested into additional tasks rather than rest, silently extending the workday until fatigue degrades decision quality.

## Key Takeaways

- **AI brain fry**: 14% of high-oversight AI users report mental fog and inability to focus (BCG).
- **Tool-count sweet spot**: Productivity gains peak at 2 AI tools and decline by the 4th, as tool-switching overhead grows.
- **Perceived vs. actual speed**: METR data shows experienced developers using AI tools work more slowly, despite believing they're ~25% faster.
- **Workload creep**: Time saved by AI gets reinvested into more tasks rather than rest, stretching the workday unnoticed.
- **Fixes**: Rethink workflows before adding AI, give managers a leading adoption role, set explicit "AI practice" rules, track cognitive load, and explain the reasoning behind AI-driven changes (transparency cuts fatigue by 28%, per BCG).

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Decision Fatigue]]**: Each AI-suggestion evaluation (accept/reject/regenerate) carries a cognitive cost; stacking dozens of these micro-decisions depletes the same finite decision-making resource that governs quality later in the day.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When "AI usage" or "speed" becomes the proxy metric leaders optimize for, workers add more AI-mediated tasks rather than actually producing better outcomes — exactly the workload creep the Berkeley researchers documented.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: AI tools don't reduce the total amount of work; they change its nature into constant micro-decisions, which causes fatigue that offsets or reverses apparent productivity gains.

**Q2**: What specific data shows AI tools can slow experienced workers down?
**A**: METR data found experienced developers using AI tools actually worked more slowly, even while believing their task completion time had decreased by nearly a quarter.

**Q3**: What is one concrete step a manager could take to reduce AI-driven fatigue on their team?
**A**: Personally help the team learn AI tools and set explicit team rules ("AI practice") such as pauses before important decisions and sequential rather than parallel task execution — teams with hands-on manager involvement show lower cognitive fatigue per BCG.
