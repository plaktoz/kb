## Summary

Katie Parrott argues that AI-assisted work creates a form of cognitive outsourcing: when AI handles judgment calls, the brain stops encoding work meaningfully, producing false confidence in outputs the user never truly evaluated. She identifies fluency bias and automation bias as compounding mechanisms, and notes that cognitive forcing functions reduce overreliance but are resisted because people dislike friction. Three practical countermeasures follow from the diagnosis.

## Core Concepts

- [[Cognitive Outsourcing]] — delegating judgment to AI means the brain never forms a durable memory of the work
- [[Automation Bias]] — humans trust reliable automated systems with less scrutiny; the more reliable, the less checked
- [[Fluency Effect]] — easy-to-read text is rated as more *true*; AI output is maximally fluent, systematically inflating perceived accuracy
- [[Cognitive Forcing Functions]] — interventions requiring users to form a judgment *before* seeing AI output; effective but disliked
- [[AI-Assisted Work]] — any workflow where AI drafts, suggests, or decides, with a human nominally reviewing

## Key Takeaways

- **Cognitive gap**: Parrott redid a completed client task — AI work left no lasting memory trace.
- **Automation research**: Pilots trusted incorrect automated cockpit recommendations even when instruments disagreed.
- **Reliability paradox**: The more reliable a system, the more likely humans let it pass unchecked (2010 review).
- **Fluency trap**: Identical text rated more accurate when easier to read; AI output exploits this maximally.
- **Review illusion**: "The feeling of having reviewed is easy to produce. The act of reviewing is harder."
- **Countermeasure 1 — Think before you look**: Write a rough position (even five bullets) before prompting AI.
- **Countermeasure 2 — Build in a gap**: Separate generation from review across time or device to reset attention.
- **Countermeasure 3 — Explain each acceptance**: Write one sentence justifying why AI language fits; a 2026 study found this cut mistaken acceptances roughly in half.
- **Design implication**: Tools should build in friction, make AI provenance visible, and treat human judgment as a real workflow stage.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Automation Bias]]**: The more reliable a system, the less humans check it — the exact mechanism Parrott documents as AI tools become fluent and dependable, gradually replacing rather than supporting judgment.
- **[[Fluency Effect]]**: Processing ease is mistaken for truth; because AI output is maximally readable, it systematically bypasses the critical scrutiny that rougher drafts would trigger.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When "having reviewed" becomes the workflow checkpoint, workers optimize for the *feeling* of review rather than its substance — the review becomes a rubber stamp.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central risk of AI-assisted work according to Parrott?
**A**: The central risk is not the machine thinking for you — it is the machine making it feel as if you already thought, creating false confidence without genuine engagement.

**Q2**: How does the fluency effect compound AI overreliance?
**A**: Easy-to-read text is rated as more true than identical hard-to-read text; because AI output is maximally fluent, it triggers false confidence in accuracy even when the content is wrong.

**Q3**: What does the "explain each acceptance" countermeasure involve, and how effective was it?
**A**: Before keeping AI-generated language, the user writes one sentence justifying why it fits their intent; a 2026 study found this intervention cut mistaken acceptances roughly in half.
