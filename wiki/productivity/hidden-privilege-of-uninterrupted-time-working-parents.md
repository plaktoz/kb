## Summary

Inequality between working parents isn't only about who logs more hours of household labor. It's also about which parent gets uninterrupted time to work and what happens when family life breaks into it. Research shows that when a school calls about a sick child, a father has a childcare problem, while a mother can *become* the childcare problem. The steady stream of small family tasks fragments the "default parent's" focus, and that cost is compounded by the invisible work of noticing, anticipating, planning, and remembering.

## Core Concepts

- **Hours vs. focus**: "Three hours of work is different than three hours of focus." Counting hours of chores misses how much uninterrupted time each parent can give to work.
- **[[Default Parent]]**: The parent who handles daytime family matters, such as scheduling conferences, buying cleats, or judging whether a rash needs a pediatrician visit, in between pieces of focused work.
- **Interruption cost**: The harm comes less from the minutes lost than from the projects interrupted. Hard assignments get chopped into chunks, good ideas get abandoned halfway, and work spills into the evening. This connects to [[Context-Switching Cost]] and [[Deep Work]].
- **Cumulative burden**: Each item looks trivial alone, which is exactly why the total load goes unnoticed.
- **[[Mental Load]] and *Fair Play***: [[Eve Rodsky]]'s book argues that family life consists largely of invisible cognitive labor: noticing, anticipating, planning, and remembering.
- **Planning vs. execution split**: In a study of 322 mothers of young children, mothers did about 73% of the planning and organizing of household tasks and 64% of the actual execution.

## Key Takeaways

- **Asymmetric Disruption**: A sick-child call gives fathers a problem; mothers become the problem.
- **Focus Over Hours**: Inequality includes who gets uninterrupted work time, not just chore hours.
- **Fragmented Projects**: Interruptions break hard tasks into chunks and derail half-formed ideas.
- **Hidden Workarounds**: Default parents still deliver on time, but by working around interruptions.
- **Cumulative Effect**: Small family tasks look trivial alone but eat large chunks of the day.
- **Invisible Planning**: Mothers did 73% of household planning but 64% of execution.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Focused work depends on unbroken stretches of time, not just total hours** — Interruptions cost more than the minutes they take, because they break up complex work and force it to be restarted, finished in pieces, or dropped. Two people with equal hours can have very different capacity for demanding work. · *model:* [[Context-Switching Cost]]

**T2. Coordination work is real labor that time-based accounting misses** — Noticing, anticipating, planning, and remembering take sustained effort, and because each piece is small, the total stays invisible when work is measured by tasks performed or hours spent. · *model:* [[Mental Load]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What dimension of inequality between working parents does the article say gets overlooked?
**A**: Who gets uninterrupted time to work, and what happens when family life disrupts it. Three hours of work is not the same as three hours of focus.

**Q2**: What did the study of 322 mothers find about household responsibilities?
**A**: Mothers did about 73% of the planning and organizing of household tasks and 64% of the actual execution. The invisible work of making sure tasks get done falls on them even more heavily than the tasks themselves.

**Q3**: How should couples or employers rethink fairness in workload, given this argument?
**A**: Look beyond hours and visible tasks to who absorbs interruptions and who carries the planning and remembering. Uninterrupted focus time shouldn't belong to only one parent.
