## Summary
A new Wharton Human-AI Research and GBK Collective survey finds that daily AI use among U.S. managers jumped from 46% to 62% while excitement fell to 51% — the first wave in the four-year study where usage and enthusiasm moved in opposite directions. The cooling is not a backlash but a shift from experimentation to the harder work of verifying AI outputs, assigning accountability, and redesigning workflows, a burden that lands on an already stretched middle layer. Departments seeing strongly positive returns are those that measure AI by growth and rebuild their work around it, echoing how factory electrification only paid off once production was reorganized.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Wharton Human-AI Research]] / [[GBK Collective]] survey**: Annual separate-sample survey of managers at large U.S. companies; this year added entry- and mid-level employees for the first time.
- **[[Prasanna Tambe]]**: Wharton professor and codirector of Wharton Human-AI Research; argues firms "overindex" on what [[AI Agents]] can do rather than what can be verified.
- **[[Jeremy Korst]]**: Wharton lecturer, GBK partner, founder of [[Mindspan Labs]]; frames the cooling as the move from "Oh, wow" to making AI work inside organizations.
- **From creating to managing outputs**: 72% of managers say their focus shifted to managing [[AI-Generated Outputs]], putting [[AI Verification]] at the center of the job.
- **[[Cognitive Surrender]]**: Research by [[Gideon Nave]] and [[Steven Shaw]] on whether reviewers genuinely check AI work or just sign off.
- **[[AI Accountability]]**: No consensus on who answers for autonomous AI errors — governance teams, managers, or employees.
- **[[Human-in-the-Loop]]**: Most departments still require human approval before AI-executed work runs.
- **[[Middle Management]] strain**: Checking, approving, and prioritizing lands on a layer with little spare capacity.
- **[[Factory Electrification]] analogy**: Swapping steam for electric motors yielded small gains until [[Henry Ford]]-style redesign rebuilt production around distributed power.
- **[[Jobless Growth]]**: [[Goldman Sachs]] economists argue AI's labor-market consequences may surface only in a recession, echoing [[Robert Hall]]'s work.
- **Gen Z convergence**: A [[Gallup]] survey shows young AI natives use AI heavily but with falling excitement — managers are converging on that ambivalence.

## Key Takeaways
- **Usage vs. Excitement**: Daily manager AI use 46% → 62%; excitement fell 8 points to 51%.
- **Mood, Not Backlash**: Optimism steady at 59%; 80% report positive returns, only 28% strongly.
- **Output Management Shift**: 72% now manage AI outputs; 82% among VPs and above.
- **Verification Gap**: An agent doing a task differs from verifying it did it correctly.
- **Accountability Vacuum**: One in five managers/directors say nobody is clearly accountable for AI errors.
- **Autonomy Levels**: 48% human-approves-before-execution; 35% suggestions only; 17% autonomous.
- **Middle-Manager Strain**: More capability creates a "new treadmill" of prioritization for middle layers.
- **Hiring Impact**: A third froze or slowed hiring; 24% expect fewer entry-level hires.
- **Training Perception Gap**: 78% of managers say training is adequate vs. 56% of junior staff.
- **Growth vs. Efficiency Metrics**: Growth-measured departments report strong returns 35% vs. 13% efficiency-only.
- **Workflow Redesign**: Strong-return departments changed structure (64% vs. 47%) and workflows (84% vs. 69%).
- **Electrification Analogy**: Real gains came from rebuilding factories, not swapping motors — over decades.
- **Recession Lock-In**: Big economic shifts tend to lock in during recessions, per Hall and Goldman.
- **Gen Z Ambivalence**: 48% of employed Gen Z say AI workplace risks outweigh benefits.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. The bottleneck moves from production to verification** — When generating output becomes cheap and instant, the limiting factor shifts to judging whether that output is correct and who answers for it. · *model:* [[Theory of Constraints]]
→ explains: Output Management Shift, Verification Gap, Accountability Vacuum, Middle-Manager Strain

**T2. General-purpose technology pays off only after the work is reorganized around it** — Substituting a new technology into an old process yields small gains; large returns require redesigning structure and workflows, which lags adoption. · *model:* [[Productivity Paradox]]
→ explains: Growth vs. Efficiency Metrics, Workflow Redesign, Electrification Analogy, Mood, Not Backlash

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the latest Wharton/GBK survey of managers?
**A**: For the first time in the four-year study, managers' AI usage rose (46% to 62% daily) while excitement fell (to 51%), reflecting a shift from amazement to the practical challenges of implementation rather than a backlash.

**Q2**: What distinguishes departments reporting strongly positive AI returns?
**A**: They measure AI by growth metrics (35% strong returns vs. 13% for efficiency-only) and were more likely to have changed management structure (64% vs. 47%) and significantly redesigned workflows (84% vs. 69%), without more staff cuts.

**Q3**: How should an organization apply the electrification lesson to its AI rollout?
**A**: Rather than just swapping AI into existing tasks, it should rebuild workflows around the technology and explicitly design verification and accountability roles — e.g. generalists running workflows and experts running verification.
