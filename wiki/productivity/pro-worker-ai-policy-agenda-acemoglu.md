## Summary

Economist [[Daron Acemoglu]] argues that generative AI risks repeating 40 years of automation-driven inequality unless development is deliberately redirected toward "[[Pro-Worker AI]]" — models that expand worker capability and create new tasks rather than replace labor — and lays out concrete tax, antitrust, and training policies to make that redirection happen.

## Core Concepts

- [[Pro-Worker AI]] — models designed to expand workers' capabilities and give them new tasks, restoring the link between productivity growth and wage growth
- [[Tax Asymmetry]] — US labor income can carry up to a 30% tax/spending burden per $100 paid, versus under 5% for $100 of automation equipment, making automation profitable even when less productive than a human worker
- [[Artificial General Intelligence Race]] — Silicon Valley's pursuit of AGI centers on replacing human labor across sectors entirely, in contrast to the narrower, task-specific models pro-worker AI would require
- [[J.C.R. Licklider]] — 1960 prediction that computers would augment human cognition with context-sensitive information, which Acemoglu argues generative AI has only now fulfilled

## Key Takeaways

- **Most Silicon Valley funding chases automation** because it reduces payroll and dependence on organized labor — a lucrative but inequality-amplifying default
- **Tax code reform is a "simpler fix"**: equalizing the tax burden between labor and automation spending would remove a major profit motive for over-automating
- **Electricians, teachers, and health workers** are cited as examples where narrow, high-quality-data models (not general-purpose LLMs) could raise wages and demand for skilled but non-college-educated labor
- **Unions should advocate for pro-worker AI investment**, not just bargain for higher wages, since wage demands alone often just push companies toward more automation
- **Reducing tech monopoly power** (antitrust enforcement, better-funded independent media) is framed as necessary to open space for pro-worker AI to develop against automation-first incumbents

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Incentive Design]]**: Acemoglu's central argument is a first-principles case that technology's social impact is downstream of the incentives (tax code, funding models, antitrust environment) shaping its development — AI's effect on workers isn't fixed by its capabilities alone, but by which incentives currently reward automation over augmentation.
