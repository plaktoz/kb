## Summary

A Harvard Kennedy School / Norwegian School of Economics survey of ~9,000 Norwegian employees finds workers average 4.7 hours a week in meetings (12% of work time, 14% of wage spending), yet meeting intensity correlates with higher firm wage premiums, revenue, and individual wage growth. Whether that time is investment or waste hinges on purpose, preparation, and participation — not on how many hours a company logs.

## Core Concepts

- **Meeting Intensity**: Norwegian workers average 4.7 hours/week in meetings, with managers and highly skilled professionals meeting far more than others.
- **[[Roger Schwarz]]**: Organizational psychologist who frames good meetings around one question — does the group genuinely depend on each other to get the work done — and identifies withdrawal and reactive behavior as the two failure modes of bad meetings.
- **[[Patrick Lencioni]]**: Author of *Death by Meeting*, who classifies meetings into four types (daily check-in, progress, strategic, quarterly review) and caps total meeting time at 15% of staff time.
- **[[Nancy Rothbard]]**: Wharton professor who notes prepared attendees are more disappointed by bad meetings than anyone else, and that overloaded bosses often call meetings just to force attention that individual work would secure more efficiently.
- **[[Matthew Bidwell]]**: Wharton professor who argues complex, interdependent work needs real-time, in-person coordination because body language and tone convey information faster than rules or email.
- **Organizational Capital**: Researchers' term for the accumulated decisions, relationships, and shared understanding that good recurring meetings build over time.

## Key Takeaways

- **Time cost**: 4.7 hours/week in meetings equals 12% of time but 14% of wage spending.
- **Prep nearly doubles the cost**: Progress meetings average 4.5 hours plus 4.6 hours of prep weekly.
- **Correlates with pay**: Higher firm wage premiums, revenue, and revenue-per-employee track with more meetings.
- **Individual payoff**: More meeting time predicts faster salary growth and more reported learning.
- **15% ceiling**: Lencioni's threshold roughly matches Norway's observed 12% average.
- **Culture matters less than execution**: Firm effects explain more variation in meeting hours than in perceived meeting productivity.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Selection Effect]]**: Employees with greater competence or more complex responsibilities may simply land in meeting-heavy roles, which could explain the wage-meeting correlation without meetings causing anything — the exact confound the researchers themselves flag.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Meeting time correlates with higher wages, revenue, and learning at both the firm and individual level, suggesting good meetings function as an investment in organizational capital rather than simply wasted time — but the research can't separate good meetings from bad ones.

**Q2**: What data point most directly supports the "investment" framing?
**A**: At the employee level, more meeting time predicts faster salary growth even after controlling for role, industry, and starting salary — a stronger association than any other use of work time measured.

**Q3**: How should a manager apply this to their own team's calendar?
**A**: Classify meetings by whether they produce a decision versus just convey information (move the latter to text), and use Schwarz's dependency question to prune the guest list before scheduling.
