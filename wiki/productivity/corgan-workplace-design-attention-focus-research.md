## Summary

[[Corgan]], in partnership with the University of Arkansas, published research (*Focus by Design*) using EEG measurements to examine how workplace design elements — lighting, visibility, movement, and sensory input — affect attention, particularly for people with inattentive-type [[ADHD]]. The study found that attentiveness is not static but responsive to environmental cues, and that thoughtfully designed spaces can measurably improve focus for a broad range of employees, not just neurodivergent ones.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Corgan]]**: Global architecture and design firm whose research group, Corgan—Hugo, led the study, funded by the AIA Upjohn Research Initiative and recognized with a Certificate of Research Excellence from the Environmental Design Research Association.
- **[[Inattentive-Type ADHD]]**: Characterized by difficulty sustaining focus, staying organized, and filtering distractions; used as an early lens into workplace design effects that impact all employees to varying degrees.
- **[[Electroencephalogram (EEG)]]**: Used to measure participants' brain activity before, during, and after design interventions in open workstations and private offices.
- **Environmental design levers**: Lighting, acoustic treatment, visual boundaries, sit-stand furniture, and scent were all tested as interventions affecting sustained attention.

## Key Takeaways

- **Lighting effect**: Dimmer, indirect lighting produced calmer, more focused states with higher sustained EEG attention than bright, direct lighting.
- **Visibility tradeoff**: Backs-to-activity seating raised concentration but also raised insecurity by 67%; partial visual boundaries minimized cognitive load best.
- **Movement in moderation**: Brief posture changes (sit-stand desks, footrests) aided focus; continuous or exaggerated movement reduced it.
- **Sensory balance**: Attention responds to the overall sensory mix (light, sound, scent) rather than any single environmental factor.
- **Neurodiversity scale**: 15-20% of the global population is neurodiverse, making ADHD-informed design broadly relevant to workplace strategy.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of Corgan's workplace design research?
**A**: Attention is not fixed — it responds to environmental cues, and workplace design elements like lighting, visibility, and movement measurably shape focus, especially for people with inattentive-type ADHD.

**Q2**: What method did researchers use to measure attention, and what did it show about lighting?
**A**: They used EEG brain activity measurements alongside self-reported feedback; EEG recordings showed higher sustained attention under diffused, indirect lighting compared to bright, direct lighting.

**Q3**: How should this research inform workplace design decisions?
**A**: Design for balanced, adaptable settings — controlled visibility, moderate movement options, and diffused lighting — rather than optimizing for a single sensory factor, since this benefits both neurodivergent and general employees.
