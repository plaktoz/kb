## Summary

[[Hubstaff]]'s State of the Workweek 2026 survey of 1,068 US computer-based employees finds that 46% of AI users say expected workloads have risen since adopting AI, while 45% spend at least an hour a week correcting colleagues' AI-generated work. Alongside this, 69% report burnout in the past year and 64% of those whose managers knew say nothing changed. The article argues that individual time savings are an incomplete measure of AI's value and that leaders should measure capacity across teams.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Hubstaff]] State of the Workweek 2026**: Survey of 1,068 full-time US employees in computer-based roles; perceptions, not objective measurements.
- **[[Workslop]]**: AI-generated output that colleagues must review, edit or correct, shifting effort from the producer to the reviewer.
- **[[AI Dividend]]**: The capacity AI frees up, which employers must deliberately allocate to output, quality, development or reduced workload.
- **[[Workload Creep]]**: Rising performance expectations that absorb time saved by AI.
- **[[Employee Burnout]]**: 69% experienced it in the past year; 55% told managers they were fine when overwhelmed.
- **Employer Visibility Gap**: 54% believe their employer sees half or less of their real working week.
- **[[Jared Brown]]**: Hubstaff CEO who argues leaders must measure actual capacity rather than assume AI gains.
- **[[Culture Amp]] benchmark**: Earlier research showing 71% feel more productive with AI yet workloads do not fall.

## Key Takeaways

- **Rising Expectations**: 46% of AI users report more work expected; only 5% report less.
- **Time Saved**: 76% of AI users save at least one hour weekly; 32% save four or more.
- **Hidden Rework**: 45% spend an hour or more weekly correcting others' AI output.
- **Use of Saved Time**: Only 26% take on extra work; 39% finish earlier.
- **Burnout Scale**: 69% had burnout in 12 months; 22% extended or ongoing.
- **Inaction**: 64% of burned-out employees whose manager knew saw no change.
- **After-Hours Work**: 71% check messages off-hours; 28% worked during their last holiday.
- **Retention Risk**: 30% seriously considered quitting over workload.
- **Caveat**: Survey does not prove AI causes burnout.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Costs shifted onto others are invisible in individual-level measurement.** — A gain measured for one worker can be partly offset by cleanup work borne by another, so productivity must be measured across the whole team. · *model:* [[Externalities]]
→ explains: Hidden Rework, Time Saved

**T2. Unclaimed capacity gets absorbed by higher expectations unless it is deliberately allocated.** — Time saved by AI is not automatically banked as slack; without a decision on its use, expectations climb faster than output.
→ explains: Rising Expectations, Use of Saved Time, After-Hours Work

**T3. Decisions made on partial visibility of work misjudge capacity.** — When managers see only part of the real workload and employees withhold strain, staffing and expectations are set on incomplete data. · *model:* [[Information Asymmetry]]
→ explains: Burnout Scale, Inaction

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about AI and workloads?
**A**: AI saves individuals time, but expectations are rising and colleagues spend time correcting AI-generated work, so the gains are not translating into lighter workloads or healthier employees.

**Q2**: What data shows AI-created work moving onto colleagues?
**A**: 45% of AI users spend at least an hour a week correcting others' AI-generated output, and 53% receive work needing significant correction at least monthly.

**Q3**: How should employers act on the findings?
**A**: Measure AI productivity across teams rather than individuals, decide deliberately how saved time is used, review real workloads and staffing, and give managers authority to respond to burnout.
