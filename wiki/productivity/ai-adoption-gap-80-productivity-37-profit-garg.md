## Summary

At the Singapore Business Review Technology Enterprise Summit on November 12, 2026, Utsav Garg of Alvarez & Marsal said about 80% of AI users report significant personal productivity gains while only 37% believe their companies will see a profit and loss impact. He argued returns are not automatic and recommended a 10/20/70 split of AI spend across algorithms, technology and data, and people and processes. The article cautions that the figures are directional and that Garg's firm sells process advisory work.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI Adoption Gap]]**: The distance between individuals feeling faster with AI and companies expecting P&L impact (80% vs 37%).
- **[[Utsav Garg]]**: Managing Director and Head of Southeast Asia and Australia at [[Alvarez & Marsal]].
- **10/20/70 Framework**: 10% of AI investment on algorithms, 20% on technology and data, 70% on people and processes.
- **141 vs 3 comparison**: Two organisations with the same technology built 141 and 3 AI solutions over two years; framed as execution, not tooling.
- **[[Productivity J-Curve]]**: Measured gains stall while complements such as process, skills and management are built; tied to [[Solow's Paradox]] and [[Paul David]]'s electrification history.
- **Measurement problem**: Firms track usage (seats, prompts, self-reported savings) rather than outcomes (cycle time, cost per transaction).
- **[[Incentive Alignment]]**: If rewards do not change when workflows are automated, teams have little reason to rebuild them.
- **Catch-up playbook**: Baseline first, shift budget, tie incentives to outcomes, get CEO backing, ship small, govern as you go (the article's own synthesis).

## Key Takeaways

- **Perception Gap**: 80% report personal gains; 37% expect P&L impact.
- **Budget Split**: 70% of AI spend belongs on people and processes.
- **Execution Spread**: Same technology produced 141 solutions at one firm, 3 at another.
- **Not Automatic**: Garg says the upward curve of returns requires work.
- **Demand Is Not Profit**: Real AI demand does not guarantee profits.
- **Weak Evidence**: Sample sizes unpublished; 80% is self-reported, 37% is belief.
- **Source Bias**: A consultancy recommending process redesign sells that service.
- **Governance Risk**: Fast rollouts without access rules invite data exposure.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. A general-purpose technology pays off only after complementary organisational change** — Gains show up late because processes, skills and management must be rebuilt around the technology; the tool alone does not move output. · *model:* [[J-Curve]]
→ explains: Budget Split, Not Automatic, Perception Gap

**T2. People optimise for what the organisation rewards** — If incentives do not change when a workflow is automated, teams have no reason to rebuild it, so identical technology yields very different results. · *model:* [[Incentive Alignment]]
→ explains: Execution Spread, Not Automatic

**T3. Activity metrics cannot prove outcomes** — Usage counts and self-reported savings can rise while operating results stay flat; proving value needs a baseline and a named owner of the result.
→ explains: Weak Evidence, Perception Gap

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the AI adoption gap Garg describes?
**A**: About 80% of AI users report significant personal productivity gains, but only 37% believe their companies could see a profit and loss impact. Returns are not automatic and require organisational work.

**Q2**: What is the 10/20/70 framework and what evidence supports a process-first view?
**A**: It puts 10% of AI investment into algorithms, 20% into technology and data, and 70% into people and processes. Supporting it, two organisations with the same technology built 141 and 3 AI solutions respectively.

**Q3**: How should a late-adopting company act on this?
**A**: Record baselines for a few processes, shift budget toward training and workflow redesign, tie incentives to outcomes, and ship many small solutions with governance built in.
