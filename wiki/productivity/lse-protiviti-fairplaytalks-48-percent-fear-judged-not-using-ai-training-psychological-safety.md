## Summary
The [[London School of Economics]] Inclusion Initiative and [[Protiviti]] survey of 3,001 professionals finds 48% fear being judged for not using AI enough versus 20% for using it, while 74% now use AI and reinvest 72% of the 19% weekly time they save. This coverage adds generational splits, training-quality effects, and a psychological-safety link, and finds no consistent tie between AI adoption and perceived job performance. It closes with ten steps employers can take to reduce AI-related pressure.

## Core Concepts
- **[[AI Proficiency Pressure]]**: Employees increasingly treat AI use as a measure of professional credibility; the report notes no universal benchmark for appropriate use.
- **[[Workload Creep]]**: Efficiency gains become the justification for higher output expectations; [[Grace Lordan]] says where savings go matters as much as their size.
- **[[AI Training]]**: Trained staff show 93% adoption versus 50%; useful training lifts confidence to 92% versus 74% for unhelpful training.
- **[[Psychological Safety]]**: Top-third safety scorers rate performance highly 89% of the time versus 66% in the bottom third; both are self-reported.
- **[[Standardised AI Workflows]]**: Employees with them report saving 27% of the week versus 14%.
- **[[Generational Differences in AI Use]]**: [[Millennials]], [[Generation Z]], [[Generation X]] and [[Baby Boomers]] differ in pressure and training access.
- **[[Human Oversight]]**: Employers should ensure staff check AI output and know when independent judgement is needed.

## Key Takeaways
- **Fear Gap**: 48% fear judgment for under-using AI; 20% for using it.
- **Adoption**: 74% use AI at work, up from 29% in 2024.
- **Reinvestment**: Users save 19% of their week; 72% goes into more work.
- **Generational Split**: 51% of millennials worry versus 35% of boomers.
- **Younger Dual Bind**: 27% of Gen Z also worry about being judged for using AI.
- **Training Gap**: 66% of Gen Z trained versus 40% of baby boomers.
- **Performance Disconnect**: No consistent link between AI adoption and perceived performance.
- **Safety Effect**: 89% versus 66% rate performance highly by psychological safety tier.
- **Observational Caveat**: Training and safety findings are associations, not proven causes.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Optimizing a usage metric decouples it from the outcome** — When adoption frequency is rewarded or judged, people use the tool to show use rather than to improve work, so usage stops tracking quality. The article advises evaluating quality and outcomes instead. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Fear Gap, Performance Disconnect

**T2. Unallocated efficiency gets absorbed by rising expectations** — Freed capacity with no explicit direction fills with more output and broader scope instead of creating slack. · *model:* [[Parkinson's Law]]
→ explains: Reinvestment

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about AI pressure at work?
**A**: 48% of employees worry about being judged for not using AI enough, more than twice the 20% who worry about being judged for using it, even though greater AI use shows no consistent link to perceived performance.

**Q2**: What evidence shows AI time savings are not reducing workloads?
**A**: Users report saving 19% of their working week but reinvest 72% of that time into additional work, which researchers call workload creep.

**Q3**: How should employers respond?
**A**: Define what good AI use looks like, evaluate work quality rather than usage frequency, provide role-specific training, protect psychological safety, and review whether saved time goes to higher-value work.
