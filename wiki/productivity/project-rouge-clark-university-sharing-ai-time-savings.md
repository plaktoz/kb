## Summary
Clark University's advancement division ran a 50-day experiment, "Project Rouge", giving employees 100% pay for 80% of hours against a target of 120% of baseline output, so that staff share in AI-driven productivity gains. Every measure improved by more than 20% while scheduled hours fell about 20%, though the author stresses the gains cannot be attributed to AI or any single change. The proposition is that sharing time savings gives employees a reason to find the next efficiency.

## Core Concepts
- [[Project Rouge]]: 32-hour, four-day week with staggered days off and an explicit performance requirement.
- [[Productivity Gain Sharing]]: the idea that employees who help create gains should share in the value, as in [[Ron Shaich]]'s Panera Joint Venture Partnership Program.
- [[Four-Day Work Week]]: precedents include [[Ford Motor Company]]'s 1926 40-hour week and Microsoft Japan's 2019 paid Fridays off.
- [[AI Agents]]: used for travel planning, receipt processing and email summarization.
- [[AI Literacy]]: three years of the EverTrue-Clark Advancement AI Lab experimenting before tying hours to output.

## Key Takeaways
- **Incentive Problem**: If efficiency only yields more work, employees lack reason to seek the next gain.
- **Results**: Every measure up over 20%; donor meetings up nearly 40%; gift-processing time down 22%.
- **Survey**: 31 of 32 responded; all wanted to continue; 81% wanted more AI resources.
- **Attribution Caveat**: Processes, technology and incentives changed together, so no single cause is proven.
- **Microsoft Japan**: Working days fell 25.4%, sales per employee rose 39.9%, with multiple factors.
- **Subtract First**: Eliminate unnecessary meetings, reports and emails before applying AI.
- **Preconditions**: Coverage model, clear performance expectations and defined non-negotiable safeguards.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Gains captured entirely by the organization remove the individual's reason to create them** — When extra efficiency just produces more work, workers have no stake in finding it; sharing part of the value realigns effort with the organization's goal. · *model:* [[Incentive Alignment]]
→ explains: Incentive Problem, Results, Survey

**T2. Simultaneous changes cannot be attributed to any one cause** — When processes, technology and incentives change together, the result reflects the combination, so crediting the most visible new tool is unwarranted. · *model:* [[Confounding Variable]]
→ explains: Attribution Caveat, Microsoft Japan

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What problem does sharing AI's time savings aim to solve?
**A**: If an employee who finishes work faster is simply given more, they have little reason to find further efficiencies. Sharing gains through time off creates a stake in finding them.

**Q2**: What did Project Rouge measure, and why can't the results be credited to AI alone?
**A**: Every measure improved over 20% on about 20% fewer hours, with donor meetings up nearly 40% and gift-processing time down 22%. Processes, technology and incentives all changed at once, so the design tested the combination.

**Q3**: What should leaders do before tying reduced hours to productivity targets?
**A**: First eliminate unnecessary work, then set a coverage model, performance expectations and non-negotiable safeguards. Organizations early in their AI journey may need to build AI literacy first.
