## Summary

MIT Sloan compiles time-management tactics from Bob Pozen and other high performers: write down and prioritize goals explicitly, protect sleep as a productivity input rather than lost time, defend a distraction-free "morning power hour" for the highest-priority task, and handle email in scheduled batches rather than continuously.

## Core Concepts

- [[Morning Power Hour]] — a protected first-thing block for a single top-priority task, with email avoided until it's over (Laura Vanderkam)
- [[Sleep as Cognitive Input]] — 98–99% of people require 7–9 hours of sleep; one bad night costs 5–8 IQ points (Tara Swart)
- [[Batched Email Processing]] — checking email only every 1–2 hours (Pozen) or twice daily with an autoresponder (Tim Ferriss), rather than continuously
- [[To-Don't List]] — explicitly listing activities you commit to avoiding, since productivity is defined as much by what you don't do (Jocelyn K. Glei)

## Key Takeaways

- **Goal-setting gap**: most professionals haven't written down and prioritized their goals, so their time allocation doesn't actually match their stated priorities.
- **Productivity metric**: "what you get accomplished, not how many hours you're in the office" (Pozen).
- **Sleep discipline**: treated as an active cognitive-cleansing process, not downtime — skipping it measurably degrades decision quality.
- **Do more of what you love**: Google's Thomas Davies reports higher energy on days containing tasks he genuinely enjoys, using their absence from his calendar as a signal to rebalance.
- **Hard boundary example**: Pozen protects 7pm family dinner as a non-negotiable stop to force work off.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the "morning power hour" and why does it need to come before email?
**A**: A protected first block of the day dedicated to one top-priority task; checking email first tends to consume the window before the priority task gets started.

**Q2**: Why does the article treat sleep as part of productivity rather than time lost to it?
**A**: Because memory consolidation and cognitive "cleansing" happen during sleep, and one bad night's sleep measurably costs 5–8 IQ points — sleep is an active process supporting next-day performance, not idle time.

**Q3**: What is a "To-Don't" list and what is it meant to protect?
**A**: A deliberate list of 6–10 activities (e.g., checking email first thing, sleeping next to your phone) you commit to not doing, because avoiding them protects time and attention for your highest-value work.
