## Summary

Cal Newport draws a direct parallel between the PC revolution's productivity paradox and today's AI boom: despite enormous promise, computers added only 0.2 percentage points annually to business output growth between 1987–1993, and white-collar productivity stagnated. Newport argues AI is likely to follow the same pattern — real conveniences, but a leap to transformative productivity gains is far from guaranteed, especially compared to industrial innovations with clearer causal chains like the steam engine.

## Core Concepts

- [[Productivity Paradox]] (Solow's paradox): heavy technology investment without measurable productivity gains
- [[Edward Tenner]]'s "Why Things Bite Back" as the framework: technology often has unexpected costs
- [[AI Productivity Claims]] vs. the [[PC Revolution]] as historical analogue
- [[Knowledge Work]] as structurally resistant to measurement and optimization

## Key Takeaways

- **Historical precedent**: PCs added only 0.2pp/year to output growth despite massive adoption
- **1991 finding**: Economist noted "white-collar productivity has stagnated" during peak PC adoption
- **Newport's caution**: "In the digital world, productivity doesn't always match our expectations"
- **Steam vs. software**: Industrial machines had clearer causal chains; digital tools are murkier
- **Convenience ≠ productivity**: Adoption will continue; gains may not follow
