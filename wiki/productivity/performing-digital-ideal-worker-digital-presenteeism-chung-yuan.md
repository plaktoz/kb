## Summary

Using UK survey data from 2024/2025, this Productivity Institute working paper finds that mothers, ethnic minority employees, and occasional homeworkers are more likely to engage in digital presenteeism. Long-hours "ideal worker" culture is the strongest driver of always-on behaviour, with flexibility stigma adding pressure, especially for mothers and hybrid workers. The authors urge output-based performance management and cultural change, as right-to-disconnect policies alone are insufficient.

## Core Concepts

- [[Digital Presenteeism]]: feeling compelled to keep a constant, instant, visible online presence, even when unnecessary or outside working hours.
- [[Ideal Worker Norm]]: the expectation that a committed worker prioritises work above all else and is always available.
- [[Flexibility Stigma]]: negative attitudes toward anyone who sets boundaries around work time or uses family-friendly arrangements.
- [[Always-On Culture]]: constant connection to work, enabled by smartphones and homeworking.
- [[Workplace Telepressure]]: urge to respond quickly to messages from clients, coworkers, or supervisors.
- [[Right to Disconnect]]: policy tool that is necessary but insufficient alone.
- [[Self-Determination Theory]]: explains how external pressures become internalised as personal motivation.
- [[Heejung Chung]] and [[Shiyu Yuan]]: authors, King's College London.

## Key Takeaways

- **Prevalence**: 40% read work messages after hours weekly; 24% actively respond weekly.
- **Felt Pressure**: 49% sometimes feel pressure to be constantly visible online; only 26% never do.
- **Who Is Affected**: Mothers and BME workers show more digital presenteeism than comparison groups.
- **Hybrid Effect**: Occasional homeworkers feel stronger urges to stay digitally visible.
- **Culture Dominates**: Ideal worker norms strongly predict active and passive presenteeism (p < 0.001).
- **Flexibility Stigma**: Mostly affects felt pressure, with effects varying by demographic group.
- **Internalised Motivation**: 53% cite internal factors; mothers more often, reflecting internalised norms.
- **HRM Levers**: Output-based evaluation, manager training, and cultural change beyond formal policy.
- **Limits**: Cross-sectional, self-reported, UK-only data restrict causal claims.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Visible availability becomes a signal of commitment where it is rewarded** — When organisations equate long hours and responsiveness with devotion, workers perform digital presence to demonstrate commitment. · *model:* [[Signaling Theory]]
→ explains: Culture Dominates, Prevalence, Felt Pressure

**T2. Those whose commitment is more doubted must signal more** — Groups facing bias and weaker bargaining positions compensate with extra visible dedication. · *model:* [[Signaling Theory]]
→ explains: Who Is Affected, Hybrid Effect, Flexibility Stigma

**T3. Rewarding a visibility proxy over output distorts behaviour** — Performance systems that measure availability rather than results push workers to produce availability, so evaluation should be output-based. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: HRM Levers, Culture Dominates

**T4. Imposed norms, once internalised, feel like personal choice** — Motivation that appears internal can be internalised external pressure, so policy alone will not fix it. · *model:* [[Self-Determination Theory]]
→ explains: Internalised Motivation, HRM Levers

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the paper's central finding about digital presenteeism?
**A**: Work culture, especially long-hours ideal worker culture, is the strongest driver of digital presenteeism, and the burden falls unevenly on mothers, ethnic minority workers, and hybrid workers. Flexibility stigma adds pressure, particularly for mothers and hybrid workers.

**Q2**: Why did mothers report mostly internal motivation for digital presenteeism?
**A**: The authors interpret it through self-determination theory: apparently internal motivation may be deeply internalised external organisational pressure rather than genuine autonomy. Mothers were more likely to cite internal motivation (odds 1.784, p < 0.05).

**Q3**: What should organisations do to reduce the unequal burden of always-on culture?
**A**: Shift to output-based performance evaluation, train managers (especially in hybrid settings), and promote cultures that support flexible working. Right-to-disconnect policies are necessary but not sufficient without broader cultural change.
