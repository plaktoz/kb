## Summary
Low employee productivity usually stems from unclear goals, poor management, low recognition, or friction-heavy work settings, not from laziness. Productivity is an output-to-input ratio distinct from performance and busyness, so it must be measured with role-specific metrics plus qualitative check-ins. Tools help only when built on clear goals, shared knowledge, sustainable hours, and consistent recognition.

## Core Concepts
- [[Employee Productivity]]: a ratio of output (results) to input (time, resources, effort), distinct from [[Performance vs. Busyness]].
- [[Productivity Metrics]]: tickets resolved per labor hour, average time per ticket, [[Technician Utilization Rate]], project tasks per labor hour.
- [[Baseline Measurement]]: capture SLA compliance, first-call resolution, and CSAT before changing anything.
- [[Role Clarity]] and [[Knowledge Silos]]: clear ownership aids accountability, but silos waste time.
- [[Flexible Work Schedules]] and [[Work Hours Limits]]: autonomy over timing and a 40-45 hour ceiling.
- [[Meeting Reduction]] and [[Asynchronous Communication]]: shifting status updates out of live meetings.
- [[Workplace Automation]] and [[AI Knowledge Tools]]: PSA/RMM platforms, Zapier, Microsoft Copilot, Glean, Fathom.
- [[Employee Recognition]] and [[Employee Engagement]]: sustaining good work through visible acknowledgment.
- Productivity heuristics: the 3-3-3 rule and the five Ds (Do, Delegate, Defer, Delete, Diarize).

## Key Takeaways
- **Four Root Causes**: Unclear goals, poor management, low recognition, and inefficient work settings drag productivity down.
- **Productivity Definition**: Output relative to input; not the same as performance or busyness.
- **Overwork Backfires**: Past a point, fatigue and stress reduce output; 40-45 hours is optimal.
- **Health Ceiling**: Over 45 weekly hours greatly increases irritability, anxiety, and depression.
- **Role-Specific Metrics**: Tie measures to deliverables, not just hours worked or tickets closed.
- **Metric Caveats**: Lower time per ticket or higher utilization can sacrifice quality and capacity.
- **Qualitative Check-ins**: Pulse surveys and standups explain dips that dashboards cannot.
- **Baseline First**: Capture SLA, first-call resolution, and CSAT before introducing changes.
- **Purpose Effect**: Gallup: 50% of employees with strong purpose are engaged versus 9% with low purpose.
- **Fewer Meetings**: MIT Sloan Review: cutting meetings 40% drove a 71% productivity increase.
- **Flexible Schedules**: BJIR research links schedule control to productivity gains of up to 50%.
- **Knowledge Silos**: 30% of developers said silos disrupted them at least 10 times weekly.
- **Tools Need Process**: Tools remove friction but cannot replace clear goals and good management.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Past a threshold, more input lowers output** — Extra hours beyond a sustainable level produce fatigue and stress that reduce output rather than raise it, so productivity is a ratio, not a volume of effort. · *model:* [[Diminishing Returns]]
→ explains: Overwork Backfires, Health Ceiling, Flexible Schedules

**T2. Measuring a proxy distorts the behavior it stands for** — A single metric rewarded in isolation (tickets closed, time per ticket, utilization) gets gamed or pushed at the expense of quality, so it needs paired quality checks and context. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Metric Caveats, Role-Specific Metrics, Qualitative Check-ins

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about how to improve employee productivity?
**A**: Gains come from removing ambiguity, friction, and exhaustion (unclear roles, siloed knowledge, overwork), not from pushing people harder or adding oversight.

**Q2**: Why might a lower average time per ticket or higher technician utilization not mean better productivity?
**A**: Rushing tickets sacrifices quality, and very high utilization leaves no capacity for documentation, training, incidents, and breaks. Reopen rates and CSAT serve as quality checks.

**Q3**: What should a manager do before introducing a new tool or policy, and why?
**A**: Capture a baseline of SLA compliance, first-call resolution, and CSAT. Without a clear "before" picture, you cannot tell whether the change moved anything or the numbers would have shifted anyway.
