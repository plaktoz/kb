## Summary

[[Sammy Gecsoyler]] kept a daily to-do list for a month to tackle the clutter, finances, health, and productivity that left him feeling overwhelmed. The biggest payoff wasn't the decluttering or the cleared inbox. It was a regained sense of control. Psychotherapist [[Vicky Reynal]] supplies the guidance throughout: break tasks into small steps, don't punish yourself for what's left undone, and use lists to carry a growing [[Mental Load]].

## Core Concepts

- **Daily To-Do List Ritual**: He wrote the list each morning before checking his phone or email, which helped him rebuild a [[Morning Routine]] and avoid the constant stream of notifications.
- **[[Task Decomposition]]**: On Day 1 he tried to sort all his clothes at once and got through about half. Reynal says a good list is "achievable, realistic and has concrete objectives," and small steps make tasks less frightening.
- **Tick-Off Reward**: Crossing off even trivial items (coffee, breakfast) gave him momentum and a "hit of dopamine."
- **To-Do List Pitfalls**: Reynal warns lists turn destructive when too ambitious or when you "beat yourself up over what you didn't achieve."
- **Paper vs. Digital**: He switched to [[Todoist]] on Day 15 but drifted into other apps and [[Doomscrolling]], then went back to pen and paper. Reynal calls the choice personal preference.
- **[[Admin Night]]**: People meet to clear backlogs of bills, texts, and subscriptions. A study Reynal was involved in suggests one a month could save £2,400 a year.
- **Done List**: When a hectic day stopped him writing his list in the morning, he wrote down tasks he had already finished, which Reynal says taps "further into that feeling of achievement."
- **Sequencing Hard Tasks**: Easy ticks can "spark a virtuous cycle," but Reynal advises against leaving the toughest tasks until your energy is at its lowest.
- **Aspirational Identity Tension**: While decluttering he felt torn between the person he once imagined becoming, the reality, and the person the lists were turning him into, so he kept some ambitious books.

## Key Takeaways

- **Start Small**: Break mammoth tasks into small, concrete, realistic steps.
- **Phone Last**: Writing the list before checking devices sharpened focus and built routine.
- **Momentum From Trivia**: Ticking off tiny tasks provides a push to keep going.
- **Avoid Self-Punishment**: Overambitious lists and guilt over unfinished tasks backfire.
- **Paper Beat App**: For this writer, digital lists invited notifications and doomscrolling.
- **Done Lists Count**: Logging completed tasks on chaotic days reinforces a sense of achievement.
- **Admin Nights Pay**: One a month could save £2,400 a year, per a cited study.
- **Shopping Lists Help**: A planned list produced balanced, protein-aware meal prep.
- **Don't Defer the Hardest**: Tackle tough tasks before energy is depleted.
- **Real Win Is Control**: The lasting benefit was feeling in control, not the tidied flat.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Cognitive Offloading]]**: Reynal argues that as life gets more complex, it becomes harder to hold everything in our heads. Moving tasks onto paper takes that burden off working memory, which is why simply writing the first list made him feel better.
- **[[Progress Principle]]**: Small visible wins drive motivation. Ticking off even trivial items gave him the push to keep going, the same "virtuous cycle" of confidence Reynal describes.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the month-long to-do list experiment ultimately deliver?
**A**: Its main benefit was a sense of control over a life that had felt overwhelming, more than the clearing out, email replies, or tidying themselves. Reynal frames to-do lists as a tool for holding a growing mental load in place.

**Q2**: What does Reynal say makes a to-do list work, and what makes it backfire?
**A**: A good list is achievable, realistic, and has concrete objectives, with large tasks broken into small, less frightening steps. Lists become destructive when they're too ambitious or when you beat yourself up over what you didn't get done.

**Q3**: How could you apply the experiment's lessons to your own daily planning?
**A**: Write the list before touching your phone, break big jobs into small steps, and tackle the hardest tasks before your energy runs low. On chaotic days, list what you've already done to keep the sense of achievement, and pick paper or an app depending on which keeps you focused.
