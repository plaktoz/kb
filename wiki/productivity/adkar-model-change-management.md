## Summary

The ADKAR model is an individual-focused change management framework developed by [[Jeff Hiatt]] of [[Prosci]] in 2003, distilled from analysis of over 700 organizations. It identifies five sequential conditions — Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement — that every person must achieve for organizational change to stick. Prosci research finds that roughly 70% of change initiatives fail due to people-related factors, not technical or budget issues.

## Core Concepts

- **[[ADKAR Model]]** — Five sequential stages: Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, Reinforcement; each stage must be met before the next is effective
- **[[Change Management]]** — Systematic approach to transitioning individuals, teams, and organizations from a current state to a desired future state
- **[[Prosci]]** — Research firm that developed ADKAR; provides scoring tools (1–5 per stage) for diagnosing where individuals are blocked
- **[[Jeff Hiatt]]** — Founder of Prosci and creator of the ADKAR framework
- **[[Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve]]** — The psychological phenomenon that drives regression without Reinforcement; new behaviors decay without active retention systems
- **[[Kotter 8 Steps]]** — Competing model focused on organizational-level urgency and leadership; contrasts with ADKAR's individual focus
- **[[Lewin 3 Stages]]** — Unfreeze/change/refreeze model; suited for planned, controlled changes with a defined end state
- **[[LMS Platforms]]** — Learning management systems become central in the Knowledge stage for delivering personalized role-based learning paths
- **[[Gamification]]** — Particularly effective in the Ability stage via branching scenarios and simulations to bridge knowing and doing

## Key Takeaways

- **Awareness**: Employees must grasp the risk of *not* changing, not just that change is coming
- **Desire**: Hardest stage; hinges on personal values and fears — leaders act as change agents
- **Knowledge**: Avoid one-size-fits-all modules; personalize learning paths by role and depth needed
- **Ability**: Course completion does not equal execution capacity; evaluate transfer at 30/60/90 days
- **Reinforcement**: Without ongoing systems, regression within 90 days is nearly inevitable
- **Failure pattern**: Skipping early stages (especially Desire) is the most common implementation error
- **Timeline**: Mid-scale projects take 3–12 months; large-scale transformations 18–24 months
- **Scale challenge**: Individual diagnostics become resource-intensive without digital tooling
- **Not a replacement**: ADKAR complements but does not replace Agile or PMBOK project management

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Ebbinghaus Forgetting Curve]]**: Without deliberate Reinforcement systems, new behaviors decay predictably — ADKAR's fifth stage exists precisely to counteract this neurological baseline, making retention an engineered outcome rather than an assumed one.
- **[[First Principles Thinking]]**: ADKAR rejects the assumption that announcing change creates it; it decomposess the problem to the irreducible unit — the individual person — and asks what that person actually needs at each stage rather than what the organization wants to broadcast.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of the ADKAR model about why change initiatives fail?
**A**: ADKAR argues that roughly 70% of change failures are people-related, not technical or budget issues — and that lasting change requires every individual to sequentially achieve Awareness, Desire, Knowledge, Ability, and Reinforcement.

**Q2**: Why is the Desire stage considered the hardest, and what tools address it?
**A**: Desire hinges on personal values, fears, and interests rather than information alone, making it resistant to top-down communication. It is best addressed through mentoring, executive Q&A sessions, and social learning where leaders act as change agents.

**Q3**: How should organizations apply ADKAR when different people are at different stages simultaneously?
**A**: A prior ADKAR assessment (using Prosci's 1–5 scoring per stage per person) diagnoses exactly where each employee group is blocked, enabling targeted, stage-specific interventions rather than a single uniform rollout.
