## Summary

A Berkeley Haas ethnography, a WebMD survey of 3,872 workers, and a UCLA game-theory paper all suggest AI does not automatically give workers time back. Workers most sure AI makes them productive show 4.5 times the burnout risk, though the survey is self-reported and correlational. [[Auyon Siddiq]]'s model explains how relative ranking can turn AI gains into a rat race, so changing the scoreboard matters more than wellness spending.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Rat Race at Work]]** — better technology plus pay tied to rank makes everyone work harder just to hold position
- **[[Auyon Siddiq]]** — UCLA Anderson; working paper "The Rat Race at Work: Technology and Burnout" (October 2026)
- **[[Xingqi Maggie Ye]]** and [[Aruna Ranganathan]] — Berkeley Haas eight-month ethnography at a 200-person tech company; proposed an "AI practice"
- **[[WebMD Health Services 2026 Workplace and Employee Survey]]** — n = 3,872; 45% of strong-agree AI users in the high burnout tier versus 9-10% of neutral or disagreeing
- **[[Forced Ranking]]** — Meta's 15-20% lowest-category target; Microsoft and Amazon use similar systems
- **[[Work Intensification]]** — faster pace, broader scope, and work extended into more hours
- **[[Employee Wellness Programs]]** — in the model, they do not slow the race

## Key Takeaways

- **Survey Limits**: The 4.5x figure measures belief in productivity, not output, and is correlational.
- **Intensification**: People worked faster, took on more tasks, and extended work into more hours unprompted.
- **Rat Race Model**: Everyone would be better off working less, but the scoreboard forces intensity.
- **Wellness Spending**: A profit-focused firm funds burnout prevention yet ends up with more burnout than one easing competition.
- **Large Gains Flip It**: When productivity gains are large, even well-intentioned firms struggle to resist pushing people.
- **Where It Bites**: Software, consulting, sales and law; least in fixed-target or shift-bound jobs.
- **Manager Levers**: Ease forced-ranking quotas, weight collaboration, and don't raise targets when conditions improve.
- **AI Practice**: Deliberate pauses, batched non-urgent AI output, and protected time for conversation.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Relative rewards turn shared gains into a race** — When rewards depend on rank, a productivity gain for everyone raises the bar for everyone, so effort rises without anyone getting ahead. · *model:* [[Red Queen Effect]]
→ explains: Rat Race Model, Wellness Spending, Manager Levers, Where It Bites

**T2. Co-occurrence does not establish direction of effect** — A one-time, self-reported survey shows two things travel together but not which causes the other, since heavy workloads could drive both. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Survey Limits

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core argument about AI and workers' time?
**A**: AI doesn't automatically give time back; evidence suggests it makes many people busier. The strongest explanation is pay and ranking structures, not the software.

**Q2**: How does Siddiq's model explain why a better tool can leave workers worse off?
**A**: Better technology makes each hour more productive, part of pay depends on comparison with coworkers, and burnout ends production. If everyone becomes more productive, the bar to stay ahead rises, so all work harder to hold their place.

**Q3**: What should a manager do before setting next year's targets for AI-using teams?
**A**: Write down what the team will do with the time AI saves. If raising the bar anyway, change how people are ranked first, such as easing quotas and weighting collaboration.
