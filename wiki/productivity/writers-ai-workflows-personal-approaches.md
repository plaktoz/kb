## Summary

Five professional writers share distinct, evolving AI workflows — revealing that no universal approach exists and that using AI effectively in writing is itself a learnable skill. Most writers use AI heavily for research, ideation, and structure while keeping human hands on final prose. The same tool that sharpens one writer's thinking can degrade another's output.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI-Assisted Research]]** — agents handle transcription, gap-finding, timeline organization at scale
- **[[Alexandra Samuel]]** — journalist who generated 52,000 rough words in 10 days using AI as a sounding board, not author
- **[[Kevin Roose]]** — built a queryable "outboard brain" from 150+ interviews; estimated AI compressed 3–5 years of research into one; used a "council of Claudes" persona panel for feedback
- **[[Maggie Appleton]]** — designer/anthropologist who built a custom spatial writing canvas via tldraw SDK and MCP server; uses AI to surface overlooked research connections
- **[[Emilia David]]** — fine-tuned a custom GPT to evaluate pitches from a target reader's perspective (senior AI engineer) to surface angles and suppress FOMO
- **[[Dan Shipper]]** — CEO of Every who actively uses AI to generate sentences, then reacts and rewrites iteratively; views AI as helping him identify what he means
- **[[Human-AI Collaboration]]** — the spectrum from AI-as-sounding-board to AI-as-sentence-generator
- **[[Editorial Judgment]]** — narrative arc and subjective editorial calls remain firmly human; AI described as "alarmingly bad" at these

## Key Takeaways

- **Research**: AI agents excel at transcription, gap-finding, and timeline organization.
- **Ideation**: Custom audience personas stress-test story angles before committing.
- **Structure**: AI is "alarmingly bad" at subjective editorial calls (Roose).
- **Writing**: Most writers won't let AI generate final prose; Shipper is the exception.
- **Editing**: AI is useful first pass for catching inconsistencies but requires human oversight.
- **Feedback quality**: ~95% of "council of Claudes" panel feedback was "slop" (Roose).
- **Skill asymmetry**: Writing with AI is a skill — the same tool can sharpen or degrade different writers.
- **Workflow diversity**: No universal approach; each workflow is personal and still evolving.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When AI output (word count, draft speed) becomes the metric, writers risk optimizing for the proxy rather than the actual goal — this is why most writers in the piece maintain a hard rule against AI-written final prose.
- **[[Cognitive Offloading]]**: The "outboard brain" approach Roose used deliberately externalizes memory and retrieval to AI, freeing working memory for synthesis and judgment — the exact division of labor first-principles reasoning would prescribe.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding about AI workflows across these five professional writers?
**A**: No universal approach exists — writing with AI is itself an evolving personal skill, and the same tool can either sharpen or degrade a writer's output depending on how it is used.

**Q2**: How did Kevin Roose use AI in his research process, and what limitation did he identify with AI feedback?
**A**: He built a queryable "outboard brain" from 150+ transcribed interviews and used AI to compress years of research, but found that roughly 95% of feedback from his "council of Claudes" persona panel was "slop."

**Q3**: What practical implication does Emilia David's workflow offer for writers who struggle with story selection?
**A**: Fine-tuning a custom AI persona modeled on your target reader can surface angles you'd dismiss and suppress FOMO-driven coverage — she used this to identify a vendor lock-in angle in an Anthropic release she had initially passed on.
