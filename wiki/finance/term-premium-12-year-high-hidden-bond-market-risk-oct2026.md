## Summary

The 10-year Treasury [[Term Premium]], the extra charge investors demand to hold a long bond instead of rolling over short ones, has hit a 12-year high. On that measure, long-horizon investing looks riskier now than during the inflation crisis, the trade war or the pandemic. Analysts offer three explanations: long-term risk really is higher, investors are more averse to risk, or the model is picking up technical noise.

## Core Concepts

- **Term premium**: In a world without uncertainty, buying a 10-year bond would cost the same as rolling 2- or 5-year bonds over the same decade. The term premium estimates the extra cost of locking money up for the long haul. Two models show it at its highest since spring 2014.
- **Negative term premium**: The premium was negative throughout the pandemic and as recently as two years ago. In that state, near-term risk is priced higher than long-term risk.
- **Candy-bar analogy**: A hungry child left alone for 10 minutes is more likely to eat the candy than one checked every two minutes. A longer, unmonitored horizon carries more risk.
- **"It's riskier" view**: Several risks are pushing bond yields to multi-decade highs:
  - [[AI Capex]] measured in trillions
  - [[IMF]] warnings that government debt is becoming unsustainable
  - inflation reignited by the [[Iran War]]
  - [[Geopolitical Risk]]: France, the UK, the Middle East, US–China AI rivalry and the midterms
- **"Investors think it's riskier" view**: [[Thierry Wizman]] (Macquarie) argues a higher premium may reflect greater risk aversion rather than greater risk. [[Nigel Green]] (deVere) says investors are quietly buying insurance while prices look calm.
- **"Model is wrong" view**: The rise may reflect technical factors such as quarter-end fund rebalancing. [[Neil Shearing]] (Capital Economics) notes that the premium is a residual, so its drivers are hard to identify.
- **Geopolitics blind spot**: [[Joe Brusuelas]] (RSM US) says economists' models rarely capture geopolitics and fall behind the curve once events turn kinetic.

## Key Takeaways

- **12-Year High**: The 10-year term premium is at its highest since spring 2014 on two models.
- **Rollover Equivalence**: Without uncertainty, a long bond would cost the same as rolled short bonds.
- **Sign Flips**: The premium was negative during the pandemic and two years ago.
- **Real Risk Case**: AI spending, unsustainable debt, the Iran war and geopolitics are lifting long-term risk.
- **Inflation Trap**: AI- and fuel-driven inflation could persist even as growth slows.
- **Perception Case**: A higher risk premium may reflect risk aversion, not more actual risk.
- **Lurch Warning**: When investors are nervous but prices are calm, adjustments tend to lurch, not glide.
- **Technical Noise**: Quarter-end portfolio rebalancing could explain the rise instead.
- **Residual Metric**: The premium is the part of yields not explained by the expected path of short rates.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Longer commitment under uncertainty demands extra compensation** — If the future were certain, any way of splitting the same horizon would cost the same. Uncertainty breaks that equivalence: the longer money is locked up without a chance to reassess, the more investors charge for it. When near-term risk dominates, the charge can turn negative. · *model:* [[Expectations Hypothesis]]
→ explains: Rollover Equivalence, 12-Year High, Sign Flips, Real Risk Case

**T2. A risk price is not a risk measurement** — A premium reflects both how much risk exists and how averse investors are to it. Because it is estimated as a model residual, it can also pick up noise unrelated to either, so a rise does not on its own show that underlying risk has risen. · *model:* [[Map Is Not the Territory]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the term premium's 12-year high appear to signal?
**A**: It suggests investors see investing over the next decade as riskier than at any point since 2014, more so even than during the inflation crisis, trade war or pandemic. No one knows exactly why.

**Q2**: Why is the term premium hard to interpret?
**A**: It is the part of a bond yield that the expected path of short-term rates cannot explain. Its drivers therefore can't be identified directly, and it may reflect real risk, risk aversion, or technical factors like quarter-end rebalancing.

**Q3**: How should an investor act on a rising term premium?
**A**: Treat it as a possible warning, not proof: it could mean real risk, nervous sentiment or model noise. Proceed with caution, especially since calm prices amid widespread hedging can lead to abrupt adjustments when bad news arrives.
