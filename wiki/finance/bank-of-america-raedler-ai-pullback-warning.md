## Summary

Sebastian Raedler, Bank of America's head of European equity strategy, argues markets are pricing the [[AI Capex]] boom as a near-certainty rather than a probability distribution, with record profit-margin expectations and 20-year-low risk premia leaving little room for disappointment. His base case is a 5%-10% pullback, but a full business-cycle downturn could produce a 40%-50% decline if AI monetization, the labor market, energy prices, or credit markets deteriorate.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Risk Premium]]** — currently at a 20-year low across credit and equity markets, meaning investors are barely compensated for holding risk; Raedler's primary concern over stretched valuations or earnings alone.
- **[[AI Capex]] boom** — spending has grown from an annualized $300B (~1% of US GDP) to a consensus expectation of $900B, buoying profit margins and GDP growth.
- **Tech sector earnings concentration** — the tech sector is expected to contribute 25% of global earnings, versus a 15% decade average and 13% at the dot-com peak.
- **[[Business Cycle]] maturity** — a very long, mature recovery with unemployment troughing and rising, historically a red flag masked by AI-driven growth.
- **Defensive rotation opportunity** — European consumer staples, the sector most negatively correlated with AI proxies, seen as oversold and positioned to outperform if risk premia widen.

## Key Takeaways

- **Base case**: 5%-10% pullback as risk premium normalizes from its 20-year low.
- **Tail risk**: a genuine recession could bring a 40%-50% decline, though not Raedler's base case.
- **Four underpriced vulnerabilities**: AI monetization/funding sustainability, US labor market weakness, energy price spikes, and rising credit defaults.
- **Microsoft's OpenAI-heavy backlog** is flagged as a funding-sustainability risk, since OpenAI is not yet profitable.
- **Raedler stays underweight European equities** overall but sees opportunity in beaten-down staples and domestic cyclicals (small-caps, German equities).

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Base Rate Thinking]]**: Raedler's core objection is that markets price the AI trade as a certainty rather than weighing it against the historical base rate that capital-spending booms often turn into busts — a probability-weighted view, not a binary bull/bear call.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Raedler's core claim about the current market environment?
**A**: Markets are pricing the AI investment boom as a near-certain success, ignoring history's tendency for capital-spending booms to end in busts, with elevated margin expectations and near-zero risk premia leaving no cushion for bad news.

**Q2**: What specific data point does he use to show earnings expectations are stretched?
**A**: The tech sector is expected to contribute 25% of global earnings, compared to a 15% average over the past decade and just 13% at the peak of the dot-com bubble.

**Q3**: Where does Raedler see opportunity despite his bearish overall stance?
**A**: In beaten-down defensive sectors like European consumer staples (down over 30%, most negatively correlated with AI) and domestic cyclicals such as German small-caps, which are priced for much worse news than the broader market.
