## Summary

The US Treasury sell-off pushed the [[10-Year Treasury Yield]] to 5.34% on Thursday, its highest level since 2002 and the dot-com bust, while the [[30-Year Treasury Yield]] reached a 24-year high of 5.69%. Unlike a typical flight to safety, investors are leaving bonds because the US economy looks too strong: AI spending is propping up growth, fuel costs are pushing inflation higher, and the Fed is expected to keep raising rates. Higher yields are raising borrowing costs for mortgages and auto loans, and rates are climbing worldwide.

## Core Concepts

- **[[10-Year Treasury Yield]]**: Hit an intraday 5.34% (highest since 2002), then closed at 5.24%, still the highest since 2007.
- **[[30-Year Treasury Yield]]**: Rose as high as 5.69%, a 24-year high.
- **[[Bond Market Sell-off]]**: A counterintuitive bust driven by economic strength and inflation, not by investors seeking safety.
- **[[AI Infrastructure Spending]]**: Annual spending now measured in trillions of dollars and expected to keep growing through the decade, fueling an overheating economy.
- **[[Federal Reserve Rate Hike]]**: The Fed raised rates last month for the first time since 2023; bondholders expect more increases.
- **[[Inflation]]**: Prices rising well above the Fed's target, driven by higher gas, diesel and oil prices; bondholders are demanding compensation.
- **[[Mortgage Rates]]**: The 30-year home loan rate topped 7% for the first time since early 2025.
- **[[Fiscal Sustainability]]**: Massive government spending from both parties is putting the US on an unsustainable fiscal path.
- **[[Neil Wilson]]**: Saxo strategist who said "There is carnage in the bond market."
- **Global contagion**: The 30-year [[UK Gilts]] yield hit 6% (first since 1998); the French 10-year reached 4.95% (highest since 2002).

## Key Takeaways

- **Record breach**: 10-year yield hit 5.34%, highest since 2002 (dot-com bust).
- **Close**: 10-year ended Thursday at 5.24%, highest since 2007.
- **Long end**: 30-year Treasury yield touched 5.69%, a 24-year high.
- **Inverted safe-haven logic**: Investors are fleeing bonds instead of buying them for safety.
- **Hot data**: Stronger-than-expected GDP and above-target inflation reports landed Wednesday.
- **Labor market**: September unemployment expected to hold at 4.1%, near "full employment."
- **Consumer strength**: Spending stays robust, fueled by a strong stock market.
- **Borrowing pain**: 30-year mortgage rate topped 7%, first time since early 2025.
- **Auction pressure**: Buyers demand higher rates at government bond auctions as inflation rises.
- **Global spread**: UK 30-year gilt at 6%; French 10-year at 4.95%.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Inflation Risk Premium]]**: Bondholders sell and demand higher yields at auctions to make up for rising fuel-driven prices eroding fixed payments, which is why yields rise as inflation does.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why is the current bond sell-off described as counterintuitive?
**A**: Investors normally buy US Treasuries for safety when times are tough. This time they are selling because the economy is too strong, inflation is too high, and the Fed is expected to keep raising rates.

**Q2**: What milestones did Treasury yields hit on Thursday, October 1, 2026?
**A**: The 10-year yield hit 5.34% intraday, its highest since 2002, then closed at 5.24%, the highest since 2007. The 30-year yield reached 5.69%, a 24-year high.

**Q3**: How does the bond sell-off affect ordinary Americans?
**A**: Rates tied to the bond market, such as mortgages and auto loans, keep rising. The 30-year mortgage rate topped 7% for the first time since early 2025, which cuts household borrowing power.
