## Summary

The 10-year Treasury yield briefly surpassed 5% on September 15, 2026 — its first time at that level since October 2023 — driven primarily by rising oil prices with Brent crude topping $109/barrel and renewed inflation fears. Goldman Sachs revised its forecast to anticipate a Fed rate hike at the upcoming FOMC meeting, noting market-implied probability had risen to nearly 90%. Strategist Ed Yardeni argued a rate hike could actually ease long-term yield pressure by restoring Fed credibility.

## Core Concepts

- **[[10-Year Treasury Yield]]**: Benchmark borrowing rate that briefly crossed 5%, a level not seen since October 2023; the 30-year also touched 5.38% before retreating.
- **[[Federal Reserve Rate Hike]]**: Goldman Sachs revised its forecast to expect a hike at the upcoming FOMC meeting; Polymarket bettors placed the probability at 80%.
- **[[Brent Crude Oil]]**: Hit a session high above $109/barrel, acting as the primary catalyst for renewed inflation expectations and the bond selloff.
- **[[Yen Carry Trade]] Unwinding**: Ed Yardeni cited an unwinding of the yen carry trade as a contributing factor to the global bond selloff alongside AI infrastructure debt issuance.
- **[[AI Infrastructure Debt]]**: Increased debt issuance by governments and corporations funding AI infrastructure added supply pressure to the bond market.
- **[[David Mericle]]** — Goldman Sachs chief economist who revised the rate hike forecast and argued market pricing at ~90% was "high enough that the FOMC will likely want to avoid the market reaction" from staying put.
- **[[Ed Yardeni]]** — Strategist noting that despite rising global yields, equity markets have remained resilient supported by climbing corporate earnings.

## Key Takeaways

- **Yield milestone**: 10-year Treasury briefly crossed 5% for first time since October 2023.
- **Oil as catalyst**: Brent crude above $109/barrel fueled inflation concerns driving the selloff.
- **Goldman rate hike call**: Goldman Sachs revised forecast; market pricing at ~90% probability of a hike.
- **Credibility argument**: Yardeni says a rate hike could reduce long-term yields by restoring Fed credibility.
- **Global spillover**: Rising yields seen in Australia and UK as well — a coordinated global bond selloff.
- **Equities resilient**: Stock markets held up globally, supported by climbing corporate earnings.
- **Carry trade factor**: Yen carry trade unwinding contributed to bond selling pressure.
- **Polymarket odds**: 80% probability of a September Fed rate hike per prediction markets.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Second-Order Effects]]**: A Fed rate hike — typically seen as bearish for bonds — could paradoxically lower long-term yields if it restores credibility and anchors inflation expectations, illustrating how short-term pain can relieve long-term pressure.
- **[[Reflexivity]]**: Market pricing of a hike at ~90% itself becomes the argument for the Fed to hike — Mericle's logic is that staying put at 90% market odds would cause more disruption than hiking, creating a self-reinforcing dynamic where expectations shape the policy decision.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What milestone did the 10-year Treasury yield reach on September 15, 2026, and what was the primary driver?
**A**: The 10-year yield briefly surpassed 5% for the first time since October 2023, driven primarily by Brent crude oil hitting above $109/barrel, which stoked inflation concerns and expectations of a Fed rate hike.

**Q2**: What was Goldman Sachs's specific reasoning for revising its forecast to anticipate a Fed rate hike?
**A**: Goldman Sachs chief economist David Mericle argued that market pricing of a hike had risen to nearly 90% — "high enough that the FOMC will likely want to avoid the market reaction" from staying put, making inaction riskier than hiking.

**Q3**: How did Ed Yardeni interpret the relationship between a potential rate hike and long-term bond yields?
**A**: Yardeni argued that a rate hike could actually ease long-term yield pressure by restoring Fed credibility, implying that demonstrating commitment to fighting inflation would anchor expectations and reduce the risk premium embedded in longer-dated bonds.
