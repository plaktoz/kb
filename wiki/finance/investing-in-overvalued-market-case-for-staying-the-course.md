## Summary
The S&P 500's CAPE ratio has reached its highest level since the year 2000, creating investor anxiety about a potential downturn. Despite historically extreme valuations, the author argues that long-term investors should stay the course because time horizon, diversification, and dollar-cost averaging make continued investing the rational choice. A diversified, low-cost index fund portfolio is expected to reward patience over the next decade and beyond.

## Core Concepts
- **[[CAPE Ratio]]** — cyclically adjusted P/E ratio now at its highest since 2000; used as a long-run market valuation gauge.
- **[[S&P 500]]** long-term return history — approximately 10% annual average since 1928, including all major crises.
- **[[Dollar-Cost Averaging]]** — fixed regular investments that automatically buy more shares during dips, turning downturns into accumulation opportunities.
- **[[Diversification]]** — spreading exposure across [[VTI]] (3,500+ US stocks) and international ETFs to reduce concentration in richly-valued mega-cap tech.

## Key Takeaways
- **CAPE at historic high**: S&P 500 CAPE ratio at its highest since 2000 dot-com peak.
- **Long-run resilience**: S&P 500 averaged ~10% annual returns since 1928 through every major crisis.
- **Broader diversification**: [[VTI]] holds 3,500+ stocks vs S&P 500's 500 — reduces mega-cap tech concentration.
- **International ETFs**: Spreading into international funds reduces exposure to richly-valued US equities.
- **DCA in downturns**: Fixed periodic investment automatically buys more shares when prices fall.
- **Accumulation phase advantage**: Market dips are net-positive for investors still building their portfolio.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Time Horizon as Risk Reducer]]**: Short-term volatility is noise for patient investors — the longer the horizon, the more the law of large numbers smooths out valuation-driven drawdowns, making entry point less critical than staying invested.
- **[[Dollar-Cost Averaging]]**: Mechanically investing fixed amounts decouples emotional decision-making from market timing, converting the feared downside (a crash) into a benefit (lower average cost basis).

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim?
**A**: Even though the S&P 500's CAPE ratio is at its highest since 2000, long-term investors have good reasons to keep investing because time horizon, diversification, and dollar-cost averaging offset valuation risk.

**Q2**: Why does dollar-cost averaging make downturns beneficial for certain investors?
**A**: Regular fixed-dollar investments automatically purchase more shares when prices are lower, so market dips reduce the average cost basis — a net benefit for investors still in the accumulation phase.

**Q3**: What practical portfolio changes does the article suggest to manage overvaluation risk?
**A**: Broaden beyond the S&P 500 by holding total-market funds like VTI (3,500+ stocks) and international ETFs, which reduce concentration in the richly-valued mega-cap tech names that dominate the index.
