## Summary

Research from [[Imperial College]] and [[Emlyon Business School]], along with a [[University of Toronto]] study of 654 fraud cases, finds that VC-backed startups commit securities fraud at higher rates than non-VC-backed companies. Researcher [[Tim Weiss]] identifies a three-stage escalation model called "façading," where founders move from exaggeration to fabrication to manufacturing entire false realities. Structural factors — founder-controlled boards, overheated fundraising markets, and investor tolerance for prior misconduct — are identified as key enablers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Façading]]**: Weiss's three-stage model of startup dishonesty — Surface, Reinforced, and Deep — describing how deception escalates from spin to full fabrication.
- **[[Founder-Controlled Boards]]**: Governance structure correlated with twice the fraud rate compared to investor-controlled boards.
- **[[Co-Creation of Fraud]]**: The dynamic where investors enable or perpetuate fraud by continuing to back founders with known misconduct allegations.
- **[[SEC Enforcement]]**: The study's primary data source; Weiss proposes routine SEC audits past a significant investment threshold.
- **[[VC Governance]]**: Systemic critique of how venture capital incentives and culture reduce accountability for founders.
- **[[Overheated Markets]]**: Startups launched during bull-run conditions are 19% more likely to later commit fraud.

## Key Takeaways

- VC-backed companies face fraud charges more often than non-VC-backed peers.
- Startups founded in overheated markets are **19% more likely** to commit fraud later.
- Founder-controlled boards commit fraud at **twice the rate** of investor-controlled boards.
- Post-IPO, VC-backed firms face more securities lawsuits than PE-backed peers within two years.
- Alleged prior fraud does little to block future fundraising rounds.
- Longer private company timelines reduce external scrutiny and enable deception.
- Weiss proposes SEC audits at significant investment thresholds and investor liability for governance failures.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Moral Hazard]]**: Investors face no liability for backing fraudulent founders, so they continue doing it — the cost of bad governance is externalized onto public markets and later investors.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When growth targets become the metric VC firms optimize for, founders optimize for the appearance of growth rather than its substance — the exact mechanism driving façading.
