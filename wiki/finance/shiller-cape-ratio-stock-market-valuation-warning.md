## Summary

The S&P 500's Shiller (Cyclically Adjusted) P/E Ratio has crossed 30 during a sustained bull market for only the sixth time since January 1871 — a mark whose previous five occurrences each preceded a major market decline, including the 1929 crash, the dot-com bust, and the COVID crash. Despite the historically dire short-term signal, the article notes bear markets have historically been much shorter than bull markets, arguing valuation stretch is a caution signal for timing, not a reason to abandon long-term equity exposure.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Shiller P/E Ratio]]** (Cyclically Adjusted P/E, CAPE): Uses trailing 10-year earnings rather than trailing 12-month earnings, making it resistant to recession-driven earnings collapses that distort the standard P/E ratio; averages 17.4 over its 155-year backtested history.
- **[[CAPE Ratio Historical Warning Signal]]**: Six instances since 1871 where CAPE exceeded 30 during a continuous bull market for 2+ months: 1929 (Dow -89% peak-to-trough), 1999–2001 dot-com (S&P -49%, Nasdaq -78%), 2018 (S&P -20% in Q4), 2020 COVID crash (S&P -34% in 33 days), 2022 bear market (Nasdaq -33%), and the current instance (CAPE peaked at 42.84 in June 2026, the second-highest reading in 155 years).
- **[[Bull vs. Bear Market Asymmetry]]**: Since the Great Depression, the average S&P 500 bear market lasted ~286 days (9.5 months) versus an average bull market of ~1,023 days (3.6x longer); 14 of 27 bull markets have exceeded the length of the longest bear market on record.

## Key Takeaways

- **Current CAPE ratio (~40.5 as of July 27, 2026)** is more than double its 155-year historical average of 17.4.
- **All five prior instances of CAPE exceeding 30 during a sustained bull market ended in a significant decline** — a 100% historical hit rate, though a small sample size (n=5).
- **No 20%+ S&P 500 downturn has taken longer than 630 calendar days to reach its trough**, meaning downturns, while sharp, have historically been comparatively short-lived.
- **The article's framing**: valuation stretch is informative about eventual risk but says nothing about timing — CAPE can't predict when a peak occurs or what triggers it.
- **Long-term positioning still favored statistically**: bull markets have historically lasted roughly 3.6x longer than bear markets on average.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Framing today's valuation purely through the lens of "this happened 5 times before and each ended badly" risks over-weighting a small historical sample (n=5) as a forecasting tool — a reminder to separate a genuinely elevated base rate of risk from a false sense of predictive precision about timing.
