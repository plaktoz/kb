## Summary

The article presents a cash-secured put options strategy on [[IBM]] stock, which has fallen roughly 30% from its 52-week high after a disappointing Q2 2026 earnings report. By selling a put at a $165 strike expiring September 2027 and pairing the premium with T-bill income, an investor can target an 8.8% annualized return while waiting to buy IBM at a ~33% discount to its then-current price.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Cash-Secured Put]]**: Selling a put option while holding the cash needed to purchase the underlying shares if assigned, generating premium income with limited downside.
- **[[IBM]]**: Legacy enterprise technology company whose software segment drives ~80% of annual revenue with $24.6B in annualized recurring revenue (ARR), up 8% YoY; undergoing a z17 mainframe refresh cycle.
- **[[Options Premium Income]]**: The ~$843 premium collected per contract ($165 strike, 100 shares) equates to ~4.9% annualized yield on the $16,500 cash collateral.
- **[[T-Bill Laddering]]**: Parking the cash collateral in Treasury bills yielding ~3.9% to supplement the put premium, producing a blended ~8.8% total annualized return.
- **[[Effective Cost Basis]]**: If assigned, the net purchase price is ~$157/share (strike minus premium), representing a ~33% discount to the ~$231 pre-decline price.

## Key Takeaways

- **Trade Setup**: Sell IBM put at $165 strike, expiry 9/17/2027, collect ~$843 premium per contract.
- **Blended Yield**: ~4.9% put premium + ~3.9% T-bill income = ~8.8% total annualized return.
- **Discount Entry**: Assignment yields IBM shares at ~$157 effective cost, a 33% discount.
- **Bull Case**: IBM software ARR of $24.6B, up 8% YoY; z17 mainframe at ~130% of predecessor pace.
- **Key Risk**: Q2 transaction processing revenue fell 9% — unclear if spending shift is deferred or permanent.
- **Portfolio Role**: Recommended as complement to a diversified core portfolio, not a standalone strategy.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Margin of Safety]]**: The ~33% discount baked into the effective entry price embodies Graham's principle of buying at a significant buffer below estimated value — the put strategy enforces the discount mechanically.
- **[[Dual Outcome Framing]]**: The strategy explicitly maps two discrete outcomes (put expires worthless vs. assignment) and constructs a positive expected value in either scenario, reflecting a first-principles payoff analysis rather than binary directional speculation.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core argument for using a cash-secured put on IBM rather than buying shares outright?
**A**: Selling the put generates ~8.8% annualized income (premium + T-bills) while waiting, and if assigned, delivers IBM shares at a ~33% discount to the pre-decline price — improving the risk/reward versus an immediate purchase.

**Q2**: How is the 8.8% annualized return achieved mechanically?
**A**: The ~$843 option premium on a $16,500 cash collateral yields ~4.9% annualized; parking that collateral in T-bills at ~3.9% adds the remaining ~3.9%, combining for ~8.8% total.

**Q3**: What is the primary risk the article identifies, and why does it matter?
**A**: Q2 transaction processing revenue declined 9%, and it is uncertain whether that customer spending shift is temporary (deferred) or permanent (destroyed) — if permanent, IBM's revenue trajectory is structurally impaired and the bull case weakens.
