## Summary

The Federal Open Market Committee held the benchmark rate at 3.75%-4% at its October meeting, with energy costs from the Iran war threatening to keep inflation elevated and a December hike possible. Consumers should expect little relief: credit card rates are at their highest since December 2025, 30-year mortgages are above 7%, and used-car loans exceed 10%. Experts advise paying down high-interest debt and shopping for high-yield savings above 4%.

## Core Concepts

- [[Federal Open Market Committee]]: held rates at 3.75%-4% after September's 25-basis-point hike.
- [[Kevin Warsh]]: Fed chair who may weigh a December hike to bring inflation to the [[Inflation Target]] of 2%.
- [[Federal Funds Rate]]: the overnight bank lending benchmark whose moves ripple through consumer borrowing and savings.
- [[Prime Rate]]: about 3 points above the Fed benchmark; credit cards track it and reprice within one to two billing cycles.
- [[Mortgage Rates]]: 30-year fixed rates follow long-term [[Treasury Yields]] and inflation expectations, not the Fed benchmark directly.
- [[Auto Loan Rates]]: new-car APR flat near 7%; used-car rates above 10%; year-end subsidized offers favor top-tier credit.
- [[Federal Student Loan Rates]]: fixed for life; new loans range 6.52% to 9.07%.
- [[High-Yield Savings Accounts]]: top online accounts pay over 4%.
- [[Mark Zandi]]: says high-net-worth consumers are spending freely on AI-stock gains, while lower- and middle-income consumers are more cautious.

## Key Takeaways

- **Rate Hold**: Fed kept 3.75%-4% range; December hike under consideration.
- **War Cost**: Zandi estimates the Iran war cost the average household about $2,000.
- **Credit Cards**: Rates up three straight months, highest since December 2025.
- **Mortgages**: 30-year fixed above 7% for over a month.
- **Auto Loans**: Holiday rate relief is temporary and limited to top-tier credit.
- **Savings**: Top online accounts above 4%; shopping around pays.
- **Advice**: Pay down high-interest debt, negotiate terms, and use higher savings yields.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Loan rates reprice according to their benchmark's time horizon** — Variable short-term rates follow the policy rate quickly, while long-term fixed rates follow long-term yields and inflation expectations. Credit cards reprice within one to two cycles; mortgages don't move in lockstep with the Fed.
→ explains: Credit Cards, Mortgages

**T2. Monetary policy restrains demand but cannot fix supply shocks** — Higher rates curb spending and limit firms' ability to pass along costs, but cannot increase oil supply. This leaves energy-driven inflation sticky and borrowing costs high.
→ explains: Rate Hold, War Cost

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Fed decide in October, and what is the outlook for consumers?
**A**: The FOMC kept the benchmark at 3.75%-4%, but energy costs from the Iran war may push it to consider a December hike. Consumers are unlikely to see relief from high borrowing costs soon.

**Q2**: Why do credit card and mortgage rates respond differently to the Fed?
**A**: Credit cards are variable and track the prime rate, so changes pass through within one to two billing cycles. Fixed mortgages follow long-term Treasury yields driven by inflation expectations and the economic outlook.

**Q3**: What can households do in this environment?
**A**: Pay down high-interest debt, negotiate better loan terms, and shop for high-yield savings accounts paying over 4%. These steps can also improve access to better financing if rates later ease.
