## Summary
Core PCE inflation rose 3.26% (about 3.3%) year over year in October 2026, its fastest pace since November 2023, with headline PCE at 3.84% (about 3.8%). Energy, tariffs and AI-driven demand are pushing prices further from the Fed's 2% target, and markets price a near-certain quarter-point hike on December 8-9. Higher expected policy rates put the most valuation pressure on rate-sensitive and growth stocks.

## Core Concepts
- [[Core PCE]] is the [[Federal Reserve]]'s preferred inflation gauge, excluding food and energy; the [[Bureau of Economic Analysis]] published the October figures on November 25, 2026.
- [[CME FedWatch]] shows markets fully pricing a quarter-point hike on December 8-9, with two more increases priced for 2027.
- [[Kevin Warsh]], Fed Chair, said "It's our responsibility to get inflation down."
- [[Heather Long]] of Navy Federal Credit Union said inflation is "bigger than an energy shock."
- [[Tariffs]], energy prices after the late-February attacks on Iran, and the [[AI Buildout]] drive demand and prices.
- [[Equity Valuation]] pressure from a higher expected path of policy rates.

## Key Takeaways
- **Core PCE**: October core PCE hit 3.26% year over year, up from 3.14% in September.
- **Headline PCE**: Headline PCE rose 3.84%, moving further from the 2% target.
- **Surprise**: Core came in 0.1 percentage point above the Dow Jones consensus.
- **Components**: Goods inflation 4.45% and energy 22.45% year over year; services 3.58%.
- **Hike Odds**: About 97% odds of a quarter-point hike on December 8-9.
- **Growth**: Revised Q3 GDP grew at a 4.3% annualized rate, leaving the Fed room to hike.
- **Yields**: The 10-year yield was 4.91% on November 24, below its 5.31% October 5 peak.
- **Easing Condition**: Monthly core gains must fall toward 0.13-0.14% to ease pressure; October was 0.29%.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Higher expected policy rates compress valuations** — A rising expected path of policy rates raises the discount rate applied to future earnings, so assets whose value lies furthest in the future, such as growth stocks, bear the most pressure. · *model:* [[Discounted Cash Flow]]
