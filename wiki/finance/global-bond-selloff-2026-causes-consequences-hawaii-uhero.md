## Summary

Long-term government bond yields rose sharply worldwide in 2026, with the [[10-Year Treasury Yield]] closing above 5% in September for the first time since 2007, raising borrowing costs for governments, businesses, and households. Decomposition shows almost all of the rise is in real yields and expected future short-term rates rather than inflation expectations, driven by heavy [[Government Debt]] issuance, demographics, AI-related corporate borrowing, energy-driven inflation risk, and a shrinking, more leveraged buyer base. For [[Hawaii]], the consequences are 7%+ mortgage rates that freeze the housing market, costlier public and business borrowing, and a weak [[Japanese Yen]] that deters Japanese visitors.

## Core Concepts

- **Global rise in yields**: US 10-year went from just under 4% (end of February, before the [[Iran War]]) to above 5% in September; 30-year above 5.6%. Yields in Britain, Germany, and France rose too; [[Japanese Government Bonds]] at their highest since 1996.
- **[[Yield Curve]] in four phases**: The 10s–2s spread narrowed from ~0.7 to under 0.3 points (Feb–June), widened to ~0.5 (mid-August), narrowed to ~0.2 around the [[Federal Reserve]]'s Sept 17 quarter-point hike to 3.75–4.00%, then widened to ~0.4 by month-end.
- **Fed credibility**: A Fed that convinces investors it will contain inflation can lower long-term yields; doubts about its resolve or independence push long yields up regardless of short rates.
- **Transmission**: Lenders price loans off Treasuries of similar maturity plus a risk premium — auto loans follow 3–5 year yields, mortgages follow the 10-year; credit cards follow the [[Prime Rate]].
- **Yield decomposition**: Long yield = expected real short-term rates + expected inflation + [[Term Premium]].
  - **[[Breakeven Inflation]]** (Treasury vs [[TIPS]]): 10-year yield up ~1.1 points since end-2025, breakeven up only ~0.1 — the rise is in the real yield.
  - **Model-based split**: Most of 2026's rise is in expected short-term rates; the term premium rose earlier, from below zero to ~0.75 points by end-2025.
- **Supply drivers**: US deficit ~5.5% of GDP with unemployment near 4% (vs ~1% average pre-2020 when unemployment was below 5%); debt held by the public ~100% of GDP; declining confidence that governments will address deficits.
- **Demand drivers**: Retiring [[Baby Boomers]] draw down savings; tech firms' AI data-center bond sales more than doubled to over $200 billion ([[Crowding Out]] of a finite savings pool); foreign holdings fell to ~30% of public Treasuries from near half in the early 2010s, ending the [[Global Savings Glut]].
- **[[Carry Trade]] unwind**: Higher Japanese yields make borrowing yen to buy Treasuries less attractive; Japan is the largest foreign holder at ~$1 trillion.
- **Leveraged marginal buyers**: [[Hedge Funds]] financing Treasury purchases in the [[Repo Market]] are prone to rapid selling in volatility, risking a disorderly rise.
- **Risk debate**: "New normal" view — 5% was common in the 1980s–90s and the rise has been orderly vs the 2022 [[UK Gilt Crisis]]; "brewing crisis" view — US interest spending (~4% of GDP) now exceeds defense, and refinancing will grow it.
- **Policy response**: The [[US Treasury]] doubled long-bond [[Treasury Buybacks]] from $2B to $4B per operation, funded by bills — a mechanical fix, not a fiscal one. Watch [[Kevin Warsh]]'s guidance as new Fed chair.
- **Hawaii impacts**: On a ~$1.18M median O'ahu home with 20% down, payment is ~$6,270/month at 7% vs ~$3,970 at 3%; the ~$2,300 gap deters locked-in owners from selling ([[Mortgage Rate Lock-In]]). The yen hit a ~40-year low in late July.

## Key Takeaways

- **10-Year Milestone**: US 10-year closed above 5% in September, first since 2007.
- **Global Contagion**: UK, German, French, Japanese long yields rose alongside Treasuries.
- **Real-Yield Driven**: Yield up ~1.1 points; breakeven inflation up only ~0.1.
- **Higher Expected Rates**: Models attribute most of the rise to expected short rates.
- **Fiscal Supply**: Deficit ~5.5% of GDP at near-full employment; debt ~100% of GDP.
- **Thinning Demand**: Foreign share of Treasuries fell to ~30% from near half.
- **AI Borrowing**: Tech bond sales more than doubled to over $200 billion.
- **Leveraged Buyers**: Repo-funded hedge funds could turn orderly rises disorderly.
- **Fed Credibility**: Trust in the Fed determines whether long yields fall or rise.
- **Mortgage Pain**: 30-year mortgage rates topped 7%, highest since early 2025.
- **Lock-In Effect**: ~$2,300/month payment gap discourages O'ahu owners from selling.
- **Weak Yen**: Rate gap keeps yen weak, deterring Japanese visitors to Hawaii.
- **Interest Burden**: US interest spending (~4% of GDP) exceeds defense spending.
- **Buybacks Insufficient**: Doubled Treasury buybacks don't fix the fiscal imbalance.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Long-term yields are the sum of expected real rates, expected inflation, and a term premium** — Any move in a long-term yield can be attributed to changes in investors' expected path of real short-term rates, their inflation expectations, or the extra compensation they demand for locking up money long; diagnosing a sell-off requires separating these parts.
→ explains: Real-Yield Driven, Higher Expected Rates, Fed Credibility

**T2. Bond yields are set by the balance of supply and demand for debt** — When borrowers must sell more bonds into a buyer base that is shrinking or more price-sensitive, they must offer higher yields to clear the market, and rising yields in one major market spread to linked markets. · *model:* [[Supply and Demand]]
→ explains: Fiscal Supply, Thinning Demand, AI Borrowing, Global Contagion, Buybacks Insufficient

**T3. Benchmark yields set the price of all other borrowing** — Lenders price loans off government yields of similar maturity plus a risk premium, so a rise in benchmark yields propagates to mortgages, business credit, public borrowing, and exchange rates.
→ explains: Mortgage Pain, Lock-In Effect, Weak Yen, Interest Burden

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central explanation for the 2026 bond sell-off?
**A**: Yields rose mainly because investors expect short-term rates to settle higher and demand more real return, driven by large deficits at near-full employment, demographics, AI corporate borrowing, inflation risk, and a thinning, more leveraged buyer base — not by a big jump in inflation expectations.

**Q2**: What evidence shows that inflation expectations were not the main driver?
**A**: Since the end of 2025 the 10-year Treasury yield rose about 1.1 percentage points while TIPS-based breakeven inflation rose only about 0.1 point, so almost all of the increase was in the real yield.

**Q3**: How do higher US yields hit Hawaii specifically?
**A**: Mortgage rates above 7% raise the monthly payment on a median O'ahu home by about $2,300 versus 3% and deter locked-in owners from selling, while the wide US–Japan rate gap keeps the yen weak and makes Hawaii travel costlier for Japanese visitors.
