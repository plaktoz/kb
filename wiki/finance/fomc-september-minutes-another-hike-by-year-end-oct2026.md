## Summary

Minutes from the [[FOMC]]'s September 2026 meeting, released Oct. 7, show that most officials expect another rate hike by year end because inflation has run above target for more than five years. The minutes gave no sign of whether that hike would come at the Oct. 28 or Dec. 9 meeting, and officials stressed that decisions depend on incoming data. Softer-than-expected [[PCE Price Index|PCE]] data and comments from several officials suggest an October move is unlikely, even though inflation expectations and [[Treasury Yields]] have surged.

## Core Concepts

- **[[FOMC Minutes]]**: "most participants" judged another increase in the [[Federal Funds Rate]] "would likely be appropriate by year end," while saying they approach each meeting "with an open mind."
- **Sept. 16 hike**: a unanimous quarter-point increase, even though several key officials had earlier signaled reluctance.
- **[[Risk Management]] rationale**: "many participants" called a higher rate path "prudent" as insurance against stubborn inflation from strong demand or further [[Supply Shocks]].
- **[[Dot Plot]]**: the median projects one more hike in 2026 and none in 2027; 16 of the 18 officials who submitted forecasts expect another increase.
- **[[Kevin Warsh]]**: Fed Chair since May; he has not submitted a forecast. He described the hike as removing "a dose of accommodation," which Wall Street read as a sign of more increases to come.
- **[[PCE Price Index]]**: August core 3% and headline 3.4%. Both are well above the 2% target but below expectations, helped partly by changes in how some inputs are calculated.
- **Economic backdrop**: the labor market is "close to maximum employment," growth has picked up, and officials see a risk that inflation stays sticky.
- **[[Inflation Expectations]]**: a [[New York Fed]] survey shows consumers' fears of rising prices over the next year are at their highest since May 2023.
- **Treasury yields**: at their highest since 2002. Officials attributed this to expected Fed hikes, the [[AI Build-Out]], solid growth, and uncertainty around the Treasury's buyback program.
- **[[Scott Bessent]]**: Treasury Secretary; in August he announced a ramp-up in buybacks of long-dated debt, which has had little effect on yields.

## Key Takeaways

- **Hike expected**: Most officials see another increase as likely by year end.
- **Timing open**: The minutes give no hint whether the hike comes Oct. 28 or Dec. 9.
- **Broad consensus**: 16 of 18 forecasters expect one more 2026 hike, then none in 2027.
- **October unlikely**: Core PCE at 3% came in below expectations, easing urgency.
- **Insurance logic**: Many officials framed tighter policy as protection against persistent inflation.
- **Yields at 2002 highs**: The Fed points to rate expectations, AI capex and growth.
- **Buyback dud**: Bessent's long-dated debt buybacks have barely moved yields.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Asymmetric Risk]]**: The minutes call extra tightening "insurance" because officials judge that letting inflation stay above target for good would cost more than a slightly too-high rate path.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the September 2026 FOMC minutes reveal about the Fed's rate path?
**A**: Most officials expect another hike to be appropriate by year end. The minutes gave no indication of timing and said decisions would depend on incoming data.

**Q2**: Why do markets now see an October hike as less likely, even after Warsh's hawkish press conference?
**A**: August PCE showed core inflation at 3% and headline at 3.4%, both below expectations. Several officials have also said since the meeting that the Fed doesn't need to rush.

**Q3**: What do the minutes suggest about what is driving long-term Treasury yields to 2002 highs?
**A**: Officials linked the rise to expected Fed hikes, the AI build-out and solid growth, and staff also pointed to uncertainty around the Treasury's buyback program. Bessent's buybacks have done little to bring yields down.
