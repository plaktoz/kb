## Summary

A global government-bond sell-off pushed the [[10-Year Treasury Yield]] to 5.34%, its highest since the dotcom bust, with yields in Britain, France, Germany, and Japan also at multi-decade highs. The drivers — stubborn [[Inflation]], soaring oil prices from the Middle East conflict, mounting [[Government Debt]], and competition from AI-driven corporate borrowing — are unlikely to ease soon, suggesting a sustained period of elevated borrowing costs. Fed officials see no clear path back to 2% inflation, reinforcing expectations of further rate hikes.

## Core Concepts

- **[[10-Year Treasury Yield]]**: Global borrowing benchmark; touched 5.34%, moving inversely to bond prices.
- **[[Global Bond Sell-Off]]**: Borrowing costs in the UK, France, Germany, and Japan also at levels unseen in decades.
- **[[Fiscal Deficits]]**: Ongoing deficits force more debt issuance, raising bond supply and pushing yields higher.
- **U.S. interest burden**: Government interest costs already exceed $1 trillion a year.
- **[[Crowding Out]]**: Government bonds compete for capital with surging corporate borrowing for [[AI Infrastructure]] buildout.
- **[[Federal Reserve]] tightening**: Fed hiked in September and is widely expected to hike again.
- **[[PCE Inflation]]**: Headline PCE rose 3.4% in August; core PCE rose 3%, well above target.
- **[[Strait of Hormuz]] energy shock**: Reduced traffic pushed oil above $100/barrel; diesel topped $6, gasoline up more than $1.
- **[[Mortgage Rate]]s**: Climbed above 7%, weighing heavily on home sales.
- **People**: [[Mark Williams]] (Boston University Questrom), [[Neel Kashkari]] (Minneapolis Fed), [[Michael Barr]] (Fed Governor).

## Key Takeaways

- **10-year yield hit 5.34%**, highest since the dotcom bust.
- **Global contagion**: UK, France, Germany, Japan yields at multi-decade highs.
- **Interest costs exceed $1 trillion/year** for the U.S. government.
- **August PCE at 3.4%**; core PCE at 3%.
- **Oil above $100/barrel** as Strait of Hormuz traffic slows.
- **Diesel above $6**, feeding production and transport costs economy-wide.
- **Strong labor market**: Unemployment near 4% with few layoffs supports spending.
- **Barr**: Only two months of 2%-consistent core PCE in 20 months.
- **Kashkari**: Inflation around 3%, elevated for more than five years.
- **Mortgage rates above 7%** are already hurting home sales.
- **Spillover risk**: Williams calls it a "risk-off" shock reassessing other asset classes.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Supply and Demand]]**: Persistent deficits increase the supply of government bonds while AI-related corporate issuance competes for the same capital, so yields must rise to attract enough buyers.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core argument about bond yields?
**A**: The forces pushing yields higher — stubborn inflation, high oil prices, mounting government debt, and competition from AI corporate borrowing — are unlikely to ease soon, so elevated borrowing costs may persist.

**Q2**: How do budget deficits mechanically push bond yields up?
**A**: Deficits force governments to issue more debt, increasing the supply of bonds and requiring higher yields to attract buyers; U.S. interest costs already exceed $1 trillion a year.

**Q3**: How do elevated Treasury yields affect consumers and other markets?
**A**: Treasury yields benchmark lending across the economy, so mortgages (now above 7%), auto loans, and business borrowing get costlier, and sustained pressure could spill into stocks and other assets.
