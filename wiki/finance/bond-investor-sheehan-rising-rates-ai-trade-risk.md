## Summary

[[John Sheehan]] of [[Osterweis Capital Management]] argues that rising rates, driven by sticky inflation, government borrowing, and AI capital demand, will not reverse soon. He sees new bond supply capping any bond rally and possibly pushing some borrowers into default. He keeps duration short at about 1.75 years to earn higher coupons while limiting losses if rates rise further.

## Core Concepts

- **Rising yields across the curve**: [[Treasury Yields]] have risen at the short end as the [[Federal Reserve]] hikes and at the long end as bond investors demand more return.
- **Feedback loop in borrowing**: Falling yields invite new issuance, which pushes rates back up, and rising interest expense forces more government borrowing. See [[Feedback Loops]].
- **AI buildout financing**: Heavy borrowing by AI builders could curb spending and raise debt costs, challenging the economics of [[AI Capex]] returns.
- **Fed policy path**: [[Kevin Warsh]] acknowledged the September rate hike's impact on neocloud volatility in October. Sheehan argues a 25-basis-point hike has limited effect on AI firms and that policy remains accommodative.
- **Market pricing**: Per the CME FedWatch tool, markets price a 35% chance of two more hikes by the October 2027 meeting and a 37% chance of at least three.
- **Duration management**: Sheehan holds about 1.75 years of [[Duration Risk]], versus a benchmark of about 3 years.
- **Source**: Interview at Business Insider's "The Long Play" event in New York, October 29, 2026, by [[Business Insider]].

## Key Takeaways

- **Supply Caps Rallies**: New bond issuance rises whenever yields fall, limiting bond upside.
- **Defaults Likely Eventually**: Continued issuance pressure will likely push some firms into trouble; high-yield issuers most exposed.
- **Hike Impact Limited**: A 25-basis-point hike should not drive AI-firm trouble, given the Fed's accommodative stance.
- **Hike Odds Split**: Markets price a 35% chance of two more hikes by October 2027.
- **Long Rates Stay High**: AI borrowing, government debt, and oil keep long yields from falling materially.
- **Debt Feedback Loop**: Rising interest expense forces more government borrowing, which pushes yields higher.
- **Short Duration Strategy**: Holding about 1.75-year duration locks in higher coupons while limiting rate risk.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Borrowing that rises with its own cost feeds back into the cost** — When issuance expands as yields fall, and interest expense forces more borrowing, rates are pushed up again rather than settling. · *model:* [[Feedback Loops]]
→ explains: Supply Caps Rallies, Debt Feedback Loop, Long Rates Stay High

**T2. Rising rates both erode existing bonds and raise yields on new ones** — Holding bonds already owned loses value when rates rise, while new purchases earn higher coupons; the exposure to the first is set by duration. · *model:* [[Duration Risk]]
→ explains: Short Duration Strategy

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about interest rates?
**A**: Rates are likely to stay elevated, because sticky inflation, government borrowing, and AI capital demand keep pushing yields higher, and Sheehan sees little that will lower long rates materially.

**Q2**: How does the borrowing cycle described in the article reinforce higher rates?
**A**: When yields fall, companies issue more bonds, which pushes rates back up; higher interest expense also means the government must borrow more, creating a feedback loop.

**Q3**: How does the article suggest a bond investor should position in this environment?
**A**: Keep duration short, around 1.75 years versus a 3-year benchmark, to capture higher coupons on new bonds while limiting losses if rates rise further.
