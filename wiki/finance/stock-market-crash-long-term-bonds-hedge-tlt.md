## Summary

Rather than fleeing to cash when a market downturn looms, the article argues that long-term Treasury bonds are a superior hedge because their prices tend to rise when interest rates fall during economic contractions. The 30-year Treasury currently yields 5.25%, providing income that cash cannot match, and bonds are largely uncorrelated with stocks. For tech- and AI-heavy portfolios, mixing in fixed income creates a more resilient set of uncorrelated returns.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Long-Term Treasury Bonds]]** — U.S. government bonds with 20–30 year maturities; bond prices rise when interest rates fall, which typically happens during recessions.
- **[[TLT]]** — the iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF; provides easy brokerage-account access to long-duration Treasury exposure.
- **[[Uncorrelated Returns]]** — assets that do not move in lockstep with equities; bonds and stocks have historically low correlation, smoothing overall portfolio volatility.
- **[[Bear Market]]** — defined here as a 20%+ decline; has occurred roughly 33 times since 1900, making downturns statistically inevitable.
- **[[Interest Rate Risk]]** — the inverse relationship between bond prices and interest rates; falling rates during contractions cause bond prices to rise, benefiting holders.
- **[[Asset Allocation]]** — the deliberate mix of equities and fixed income to manage risk; the article targets investors concentrated in [[Technology]] and [[AI]] stocks.

## Key Takeaways

- **Bonds beat cash**: 30-year Treasuries yield 5.25% — cash earns less and has no upside.
- **No tax drag**: Staying in bonds avoids triggering capital gains taxes from selling winners.
- **Inverse rate relationship**: Bond prices rise when rates fall — recessions cause rate cuts.
- **Low correlation**: Bonds and stocks are largely uncorrelated, reducing portfolio drawdown.
- **TLT is the vehicle**: [[TLT]] gives straightforward 20+ year Treasury exposure via any brokerage.
- **Bear markets are inevitable**: 33 drops of 20%+ since 1900; timing is unpredictable, hedging is prudent.
- **Partial shift only**: Author recommends "mixing in some fixed income," not abandoning equities.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Diversification]]**: Combining assets with low correlation (stocks + long bonds) reduces the variance of the overall portfolio without proportionately sacrificing expected return — the only free lunch in finance.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: Holding cash as a defensive move forfeits both the 5.25% yield on Treasuries and the price appreciation bonds generate when rates fall in a downturn — making cash the worse hedge on both dimensions.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central recommendation for investors worried about a market crash?
**A**: Consider shifting some equity exposure — especially in tech and AI names — into long-term Treasury bonds rather than cash, because bonds provide yield and tend to appreciate during economic contractions.

**Q2**: Why do long-term bond prices rise during a stock market downturn, and what current yield does the 30-year Treasury offer?
**A**: Bond prices move inversely to interest rates; during economic contractions the Federal Reserve typically cuts rates, pushing bond prices higher. The 30-year Treasury currently yields 5.25%.

**Q3**: How should an investor practically access long-term Treasury exposure, and how much of a portfolio shift does the author suggest?
**A**: The iShares 20+ Year Treasury Bond ETF ([[TLT]]) is available through standard brokerage accounts; the author suggests only mixing in some fixed income rather than making dramatic portfolio changes.
