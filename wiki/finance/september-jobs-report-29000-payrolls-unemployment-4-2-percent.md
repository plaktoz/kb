## Summary

U.S. [[Nonfarm Payrolls]] rose just 29,000 in September 2026, against an 84,000 forecast. Unemployment rose to 4.2%, mostly because 485,000 people joined the labor force, and July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000. Wage growth slowed to 3% year over year, the lowest since May 2021, and markets rallied on the view that the soft data locks in a [[Federal Reserve]] hold at the Oct. 27–28 meeting, even as GDP growth stays strong.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Nonfarm Payrolls]]**: The [[Bureau of Labor Statistics]] establishment-survey jobs count. It added a seasonally adjusted 29,000 in September, well short of the Dow Jones consensus.
- **[[Household Survey]] vs. [[Establishment Survey]]**: The household survey, which sets the jobless rate, looked much stronger than the payrolls survey: household employment +406,000 and labor force +485,000.
- **[[Labor Force Participation Rate]]**: Rose 0.2 percentage point to 61.8%, its highest since May. This explains why unemployment rose despite more people working.
- **[[U-6 Unemployment Rate]]**: The broader measure that includes discouraged workers and people working part-time for economic reasons. It edged down to 7.6%, the lowest since January 2025.
- **[[Low-Hire, Low-Fire Economy]]**: The labor market has low weekly jobless claims and layoffs at a four-year-low rate, but little hiring.
- **[[Wage Disinflation]]**: Average hourly earnings rose only 0.1% for the month, and the 3% annual gain is "being wiped out entirely by inflation," according to [[Heather Long]].
- **[[FOMC]] rate path**: The Fed raised rates a quarter point in September. Markets now expect a hold in October and the next hike in December.
- **[[CME FedWatch]]**: Market-implied odds of an October hold jumped to 82.8% after the report.
- **[[Core PCE]]**: The Fed's preferred inflation gauge shows core inflation at 3% a year, well above the 2% target. Policymakers see inflation as the bigger threat.
- **[[Thomas Simons]]** ([[Jefferies]]): Called the print "the nail in the coffin for an October hike." He reads August's surge as a rebound from weak June and July hiring.

## Key Takeaways

- **Headline miss**: +29,000 payrolls versus an 84,000 forecast.
- **Unemployment**: 4.2% versus 4.1% expected, driven by a labor-force influx.
- **Revisions**: August cut to +133,000; July flipped to −10,000; net −60,000.
- **Household strength**: Employment +406,000; labor force +485,000.
- **Participation**: 61.8%, the highest since May.
- **Broader unemployment**: 7.6%, the lowest since January 2025.
- **Wages**: +0.1% month over month, 3% year over year (lowest since May 2021) versus 0.3%/3.1% expected.
- **Hours**: The average work week was unchanged at 34.6 hours.
- **Job gainers**: Health care +17,000, construction +11,000, manufacturing +9,000.
- **Job losers**: Government −17,000, temp help −11,000, information −10,000, financial −7,000.
- **AI angle**: Information-services job losses come amid worries about AI's impact on jobs.
- **Market reaction**: Stock futures rose sharply and Treasury yields slumped from levels not seen since the early 2000s.
- **Macro contrast**: Q1/Q2 GDP revised to 2.5%/2.2%; the Atlanta Fed tracks Q3 at 3.7%.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: At first glance, a weak jobs report is bad news. Traders looked one step further: soft hiring lowers the odds of an October hike, which pushes yields down, so stocks rose on the "bad" data.
- **[[Regression to the Mean]]**: Simons argues August's payroll surge was "nothing more than a rebound" from very weak June and July hiring, so extrapolating that momentum into September was a mistake.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the September 2026 jobs report show about the U.S. labor market?
**A**: Payrolls rose just 29,000 against an 84,000 forecast, unemployment rose to 4.2%, and July and August were revised down a combined 60,000. That points to a surprising soft spot in the labor market.

**Q2**: Why did unemployment rise even though the household survey showed strong employment gains?
**A**: The labor force grew by 485,000, more than household employment's 406,000 gain, and participation rose to 61.8%. New entrants pushed up the jobless rate.

**Q3**: What does the report mean for Fed policy and markets?
**A**: Traders read the weak data as good news because it nearly guarantees a Fed hold at the Oct. 27–28 meeting (82.8% odds). Stocks rose and yields fell, though markets still expect a December hike because core inflation sits at 3%.
