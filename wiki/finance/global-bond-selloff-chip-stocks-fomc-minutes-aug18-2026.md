## Summary

US and global bond yields hit multiyear highs on August 18, 2026, driven by fiscal-deficit concerns, inflation fears, and heavy AI-infrastructure debt issuance, dragging chip stocks down sharply even as investors stayed calm ahead of Wednesday's FOMC minutes and a heavy retail-earnings week.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI Capex Debt Competition]]**: The AI industry's massive borrowing to fund data-center buildout is competing with traditional nation-state borrowers for capital, a factor pushing global bond yields higher alongside fiscal deficits and inflation.
- **[[FOMC Minutes]]**: The July 28-29 meeting minutes, released Wednesday Aug 19, are expected to detail the three-dissenter split within the Fed over whether to hike rates — a continuation of the "good family fight" Fed Chair Kevin Warsh described.
- **[[Retail Earnings as Consumer Proxy]]**: Target, TJX, Lowe's, and Analog Devices report Wednesday, adding to the week's read on consumer and business spending health.

## Key Takeaways

- **Yield milestones**: US 30-year Treasury yield hit its highest intraday level since 2007 (as high as 5.337%); Japan's 10-year bond yield hit its highest since at least 2006; German 30-year bund yields highest since 2011; France's 30-year bond highest since 2008.
- **Equity impact**: Nasdaq fell 1.3%; the PHLX Semiconductor index dropped more than 5%, with Intel, Marvell, ARM Holdings, Coherent, and Teradyne among the hardest hit.
- **Geopolitical overlay**: An unknown projectile struck a cargo ship in the Strait of Hormuz after the 60-day US-Iran truce expired with no signs of a new deal; Trump said no talks are planned. Brent crude edged up to $91.02/barrel.
- **Muted equity reaction**: Despite the bond selloff, stocks reacted with relative calm — investors are betting continued economic growth and strong corporate earnings can absorb higher borrowing costs.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Crowding Out]]**: When one large borrower (here, the AI industry) competes for the same pool of capital as governments, it can push up the price of borrowing (yields) for everyone — a classic economic crowding-out dynamic playing out at global scale.
