## Summary
Drawing on 50 years of S&P 500 history, the article argues that the single best response to a potential market downturn is simply to do nothing — stay the course rather than attempting to time the market. Warren Buffett's emphasis on investor temperament over IQ is presented as the critical differentiator between successful and unsuccessful long-term investors. Regular dollar-cost averaging into a broad index ETF, combined with the discipline to hold through volatility, has a compelling multi-decade track record.

## Core Concepts

- **[[S&P 500]]** — 50-year history of bull-bear-bull cycles that trend upward over time; the benchmark for buy-and-hold evidence.
- **[[Buy and Hold]]** — the strategy of purchasing broad index exposure and resisting the urge to sell during downturns.
- **[[Market Timing]]** — attempting to buy and sell based on short-term price movements; identified as the primary "urge" that destroys long-term returns.
- **[[Dollar-Cost Averaging]]** — investing a fixed amount at regular intervals regardless of price level; turns volatility into a systematic cost-reduction mechanism.
- **[[Warren Buffett]]** — cited for his view that temperament, not IQ, is the key investor advantage; recommends most investors buy S&P 500 index ETFs such as [[SPY]] or [[VOO]].
- **[[Investor Temperament]]** — the psychological capacity to hold through short-term volatility without acting on fear or greed.

## Key Takeaways

- **Do nothing**: Winnie the Pooh's "doing nothing leads to the best of something" is validated by 50-year market history.
- **Temperament over IQ**: Buffett says a 130 IQ with good temperament beats a 160 IQ investor who can't control urges.
- **S&P 500 zigzags upward**: Bull and bear cycles recur, but the long-term trend is unambiguously higher.
- **Avoid market timing**: Buying and selling to exploit short-term moves is "difficult, if not impossible" over the long term.
- **Regular buying beats cash-holding**: Continuing to invest during downturns via DCA is superior to sitting on the sidelines.
- **Index ETFs recommended**: Buffett's own advice points to [[SPY]] or [[VOO]] as the practical vehicle for most investors.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Inversion]]**: Instead of asking "what should I do during a bear market?", invert — what actions cause underperformance? Market timing. Eliminating that bad action is itself the answer, which is why "do nothing" is a complete strategy.
- **[[Regression to the Mean]]**: The S&P 500's 50-year zigzag pattern shows that bear markets are temporary deviations; the long-run mean trajectory is upward, making short-term reactions self-defeating.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about the best response to a potential market downturn?
**A**: The single best response is to do nothing — stay the course with your existing investment plan rather than trying to react to short-term price movements.

**Q2**: What specific evidence supports the "do nothing" strategy, and what does Buffett say makes investors underperform?
**A**: Fifty years of S&P 500 history show that buying and holding through bull-bear cycles produces strong long-term results; Buffett argues that poor investor temperament — acting on fear or greed — rather than lack of intelligence is what gets people into trouble.

**Q3**: How should an investor practically implement the "do nothing" philosophy?
**A**: Continue dollar-cost averaging into a broad S&P 500 index ETF (such as SPY or VOO) on a regular schedule regardless of market conditions, and resist the urge to time the market by selling during downturns.
