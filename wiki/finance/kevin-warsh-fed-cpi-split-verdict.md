## Summary

July's CPI report split economists into three camps — those reading it as confirming disinflation, those calling the print misleading due to energy price lag effects, and those arguing the Fed's policy rate is simply too tight regardless of the data.

## Core Concepts

- [[Disinflation Trend]] — Claudia Sahm and David Rosenberg both read narrowing price breadth (core CPI at a 1.6% three-month annualized rate) as evidence inflation is genuinely cooling.
- [[Energy Price Lag Effect]] — Peter Schiff's argument that CPI's monthly-average methodology makes July's print reflect May's depressed oil prices, not July's actual rebound.
- [[Fed Policy Restrictiveness]] — James Thorne's view that the Fed's rate sits above neutral even as housing and business investment already show strain.

## Key Takeaways

- **Headline CPI**: 3.4% year-over-year in July, down from 3.5% in June; core CPI eased to 2.5% from 2.6%.
- **Narrowing breadth**: Share of the CPI basket with 5%+ price rises fell back to 1997-2005 average levels — seen as the most encouraging signal.
- **Skeptic's case**: Elevated energy costs from the Iran conflict make the July print an unreliable read on where prices are actually heading.
- **Structural critique**: Charlie Bilello notes CPI has run at a 4.0% annualized rate since Jan 2020, 13% above the Fed's 2% target trend.
- **Markets read**: Mohamed El-Erian ties elevated Treasury yields more to heavy debt supply ahead than to inflation or Fed policy itself.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Economists are sharply divided on how to interpret July's CPI report — as good news on disinflation, as a misleading print, or as proof the Fed is still too restrictive.

**Q2**: What specific data point did Claudia Sahm call "the best news" in the report?
**A**: The narrowing breadth of inflation — the share of the CPI basket with prices rising 5%+ or more fell back to 1997-2005 average levels.

**Q3**: How might this data influence the Fed's rate decision, and what's the market implication?
**A**: It modestly reduces the odds of a hike and supports holding rates steady, though Treasury yields staying elevated is attributed more to debt supply than to the inflation data itself.
