## Summary

[[Fed Governor Lisa Cook]] argues AI is a general-purpose technology on par with the steam engine or electricity, and lays out its dual-mandate implications: AI-driven investment is adding inflationary pressure now (via chips, energy, and construction labor demand) that a future productivity boom may only partially offset, while the labor market has so far remained resilient despite AI adoption. She also highlights that small businesses are adopting AI faster than prior technologies like the PC or internet, a hopeful sign for broad-based productivity gains.

## Core Concepts

- [[Lisa Cook]] — Federal Reserve Governor who frames AI as a [[General-Purpose Technology]] comparable to the steam engine, electricity, and the internet.
- [[AI and Inflation]] — AI-related goods (chips, computers, software) have surged in price; some of this reflects sector-specific demand shifts rather than economy-wide pressure, but data-center investment's reliance on broadly-used inputs (construction labor, energy) is spreading price pressure economy-wide.
- [[Productivity Boom]] — could ease inflationary pressure if it raises supply capacity more than demand, but Cook expects only modest, delayed disinflation, not enough to offset near-term price pressure.
- [[AI and the Labor Market]] — unemployment and layoffs remain low despite AI adoption, though coding jobs, translation, and entry-level roles show early signs of AI-driven labor demand reduction.
- [[Paul Romer]] — Cook's Berkeley growth theory professor whose thesis (investment in ideas drives unbounded growth) she sees materializing via AI adoption, calling the current period a "Romer moment."
- [[AI Diffusion Across Firms]] — Federal Reserve Small Business Credit Survey found nearly half of small employer firms use AI, with 71% reporting increased productivity; AI adoption is outpacing PC and internet adoption at comparable stages.
- [[FOMC]] — voted to raise rates 25 basis points in September 2026, with total inflation at an estimated 3.8% and core inflation at 3.4% over the trailing 12 months.

## Key Takeaways

- **AI as GPT**: Cook classifies AI as a general-purpose technology on par with electricity and the internet, spreading innovation across the economy.
- **Two-track inflation effect**: Sector-specific AI price surges should self-resolve, but broadly-used inputs (labor, energy) are spreading pressure economy-wide.
- **$2 trillion pipeline**: Companies have spent only a small fraction of $2 trillion in announced AI investment plans.
- **Utility costs up**: Electricity and water costs are each up ~5% year-over-year, partly attributable to AI data-center demand.
- **Labor market resilient so far**: Unemployment (4.1% in August) and layoffs remain low despite AI adoption picking up.
- **Early labor effects visible**: Coding, translation jobs, and new-grad hiring show early signs of AI displacing labor demand.
- **Small business AI adoption**: Nearly half of small employer firms use AI; 71% report productivity gains; adoption is faster than PC or internet uptake.
- **Small firms drive jobs**: Firms under 500 employees are 99%+ of U.S. businesses and drove 61% of net new job creation since 1995.
- **Blunt policy tools**: The Fed's tools can't target narrow sectors, and unemployment from AI-driven skill mismatch would leave the Fed with limited options (cutting rates risks fueling inflation).

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Long and Variable Lags]]**: Cook explicitly invokes this monetary-policy concept to explain why AI's economic benefits and costs may not arrive simultaneously, mirroring past technological disruptions like the mechanical loom and the PC.
- **[[Aggregate Supply-Aggregate Demand Framework]]**: Cook uses this classic macroeconomic model to explain when a productivity boom would ease versus fail to ease inflation — the relief depends on whether new supply outpaces the new demand that productivity gains themselves generate.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Governor Cook's core framing of AI's economic significance?
**A**: She frames AI as a general-purpose technology comparable to the steam engine, electricity, and the internet, with implications spanning inflation, the labor market, and business formation.

**Q2**: What specific data point does Cook cite showing labor market resilience despite AI adoption?
**A**: The unemployment rate held at 4.1% in August 2026, and both unemployment and layoffs have stayed relatively flat over the past two years even as AI adoption increased.

**Q3**: What does Cook's small-business findings imply for how policymakers should think about who benefits from AI?
**A**: Since nearly half of small employer firms already use AI with 71% reporting productivity gains, and adoption is outpacing prior technologies, broad-based AI diffusion (not just large tech firms) could meaningfully boost job creation and living standards if competition among model producers stays robust.
