## Summary
Fed Vice Chair Philip Jefferson uses the 1990s IT boom as a template to argue that AI's contribution to aggregate productivity is so far limited but could grow substantially, with uncertain timing. He says AI-driven investment and equity wealth are already lifting demand and prices, and that its effect on the neutral rate and labor market complicates monetary policy. He sees growth near potential, 4.1% unemployment and inflation well above the 2% target, so inflation drives his policy stance.

## Core Concepts
- [[Solow Productivity Paradox]]: Solow's 1987 remark that computers show up everywhere but in productivity statistics, mirrored today by [[Generative AI]].
- [[Total Factor Productivity (TFP)]]: gains from reorganizing production, not just adding capital, drove the post-IT-boom rise outside the IT sector.
- [[Capital Deepening]]: firms intensify use of cheaper capital goods produced by higher-productivity industries.
- [[AI Adoption]]: 40-50% of working adults use AI at work, about 25% of firms use it in at least one function, and about 40% in knowledge-intensive sectors.
- [[Neutral Rate of Interest (r*)]]: higher productivity growth could raise investment demand and cut saving, pushing r* up and making a given policy rate less restrictive.
- [[Structural vs. Cyclical Unemployment]]: monetary policy can address cyclical but not structural unemployment from skills mismatch.
- [[Data Center Investment]]: large AI buildout lifts aggregate demand and prices of memory and logic chips.
- [[Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC)]] and the [[Dual Mandate]]: maximum employment and price stability.
- [[Philip Jefferson]]: Fed Vice Chair and speaker.

## Key Takeaways
- **Productivity Model**: Basic research, applied research, lower prices, capital deepening, then reorganization raising TFP.
- **Adoption Gap**: AI is not yet everywhere; only a modest fraction of users use it daily.
- **Macro Evidence**: Labor productivity grew 2.2% over 2020-2025 versus 1.5% earlier, but began before 2022 chatbots.
- **Task vs. Firm Gains**: One study found AI boosted sales per employee just 0.5% over three years.
- **Offsetting Costs**: AI-generated code has more errors, so debugging offsets efficiency gains.
- **Demand Effects**: Equity wealth and data center spending lift demand before supply gains arrive.
- **Labor Risk**: Faster displacement than retraining could raise structural unemployment.
- **Policy Stance**: GDP grew 3.4% in Q3; PCE inflation 3.6%, core 3.1% in September.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. General-purpose technology gains arrive with a long lag** — Measured productivity trails invention and early adoption because applied research, capital deepening and business reorganization must come first; real-time data can miss it. · *model:* [[J-Curve]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Jefferson's overall judgment on AI and aggregate productivity?
**A**: The evidence is consistent with a limited contribution so far but potential to grow substantially, in line with past technological breakthroughs. The magnitude and timing remain highly uncertain.

**Q2**: Why might task-level AI gains not show up at the firm level?
**A**: Most adopting firms use AI in only a few functions and are still experimenting, and gains in one domain bring offsetting costs or bottlenecks elsewhere, such as more debugging of AI-generated code.

**Q3**: How could AI affect monetary policy?
**A**: Permanently higher productivity growth could raise investment demand and cut saving, pushing up the neutral rate and making a given policy rate less restrictive. Rapid displacement could also raise structural unemployment, which monetary policy cannot fix.
