## Summary

Research from Imperial College, Emlyon Business School, and the University of Toronto finds that VC-backed startups commit securities fraud at higher rates than non-VC-backed peers, with startups launched during overheated markets 19% more likely to later commit fraud. Researcher Tim Weiss describes a three-stage escalation called "façading" — from exaggeration to fabricated evidence to manufactured false realities. Structural factors like founder-controlled boards and prolonged private timelines reduce scrutiny, while investors can "co-create fraud" by continuing to back founders despite prior misconduct.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Venture Capital]] and fraud co-creation**: VCs who back founders with known misconduct allegations enable escalating dishonesty
- **[[Façading]]**: Tim Weiss's three-stage model of startup dishonesty — surface, reinforced, and deep
- **[[Corporate Governance]]**: Founder-controlled boards are twice as likely to produce fraud vs. investor-controlled boards
- **[[Securities Fraud]]**: SEC and DOJ prosecutions of tech founders 2000–2023 used as the research dataset
- **[[Market Overheating]]**: Startups launched during market bubbles face elevated fraud risk due to unrealistic growth pressure
- **[[Startup Failure Culture]]**: VC ecosystem's embrace of failure regardless of cause insulates bad actors from accountability

## Key Takeaways

- **19% higher fraud risk** for startups launched during overheated markets
- **Founder-controlled boards** are twice as likely to commit fraud vs. investor-controlled boards
- **Three-stage façading**: surface exaggeration → fabricated evidence → fabricated technical capabilities
- **Post-IPO exposure**: VC-backed firms face more securities lawsuits than PE-backed peers within two years
- **Longer private timelines** reduce external scrutiny and allow fraud to compound
- **Alleged fraud rarely blocks future fundraising** — the "embrace failure" culture is indiscriminate
- **Proposed fix**: routine SEC audits once startups hit significant investment thresholds
- **Investor liability**: Weiss argues investors should bear liability for governance failures and unrealistic growth pressure

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Moral Hazard]]**: When investors absorb downside risk and reward bold growth narratives, founders face asymmetric incentives — the expected cost of fraud is low while the cost of missing growth targets is high, systematically pushing toward dishonesty.
- **[[Principal-Agent Problem]]**: Founder-controlled boards eliminate the oversight mechanism meant to align founder behavior with investor and public interest; without a credible monitor, self-dealing flourishes.
- **[[Broken Windows Theory]]**: Investors tolerating early-stage exaggeration ("aspirational pitching") signal that dishonesty is acceptable, allowing it to escalate through reinforced and deep façading unchecked.
