## Summary
A study of 31,044 US adults aged 40 and older found that eating the first meal later in the day, or the last meal after midnight, was associated with higher mortality risk. The authors stress this is an observational association, not proof of causation, and reverse causation is possible.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Meal Timing]]**: Time of first and last calorie intake and the eating window between them.
- **[[NHANES]]**: US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey data, 1999 to 2018, linked to deaths through 2019.
- **[[Circadian Rhythm]]**: Meal timing as a cue for peripheral clocks; misalignment may disrupt lipid and glucose metabolism.
- **[[Observational Study]] limits**: A single 24-hour recall and unmeasured factors such as [[Chronotype]] and sleep.
- **[[Reverse Causation]]**: Late or very early eating may reflect poor health, shift work or socioeconomic circumstances.
- **[[Zhilei Shan]]**: Senior author at Huazhong University of Science and Technology.
- **[[Time-Restricted Eating]]**: Eating-window length showed no consistent link to mortality once first-meal timing was considered.

## Key Takeaways
- **Sample**: 31,044 adults; 7,129 deaths over a median 8.6 years.
- **Later first meal**: Versus 7-8 am, risk rose 10% (8-10 am), 19% (10 am-noon), 29% (after noon).
- **Cardiovascular risk**: First meal after noon linked to 46% higher cardiovascular death risk.
- **Life expectancy**: Later first meals were linked to 2.46 fewer years at age 50.
- **U-shaped last meal**: Finishing before 7 pm linked to 13% higher risk; after midnight, 27% higher.
- **Lowest risk**: Modeled lowest risk was around 8 pm, not a universal optimum.
- **Eating window**: Length alone did not consistently predict mortality.
- **Practical advice**: Keep regular routines, avoid very late first meals and frequent late-night eating.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Association in observational data is not causation** — A statistical link between exposure and outcome does not show that changing the exposure changes the outcome. Unmeasured factors can drive both. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Later first meal, Practical advice

**T2. The outcome can cause the exposure** — Poor health, poor sleep or shift work may shift when people eat, so the clock may be a marker of health rather than a cause. · *model:* [[Reverse Causation]]
→ explains: U-shaped last meal, Later first meal

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's core finding?
**A**: Adults whose first meal came later in the day, or whose last meal came after midnight, had a higher risk of death. It is an association, not proof that changing meal times changes outcomes.

**Q2**: What pattern did the last-meal timing show?
**A**: It was U-shaped: compared with finishing at 7-8 pm, finishing before 7 pm carried 13% higher risk and after midnight 27% higher, while 8 pm to midnight was not significantly different. Early finishers tended to be older and sicker, so health may explain the link.

**Q3**: How should people act on these findings, according to the senior author?
**A**: Shan says not to make extreme changes based on this alone. A practical reading is to keep regular eating and sleeping routines, avoid routinely delaying the first meal very late, avoid frequent late-night eating, and prioritize overall diet quality.
