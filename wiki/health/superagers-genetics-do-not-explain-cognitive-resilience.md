## Summary

A study of 142 [[SuperAgers]] — adults in their 80s and 90s with memory comparable to people decades younger — found no meaningful genetic advantage over cognitively average older adults on either the APOE gene or polygenic Alzheimer's risk scores, ruling out the hypothesis that exceptional late-life memory is simply inherited low genetic risk. The finding redirects research toward unexplained biological, behavioral, and environmental factors that might protect cognition independent of Alzheimer's disease risk itself.

## Core Concepts

- **[[SuperAging]]** — a term established in 2008 defining older adults whose memory performance matches or exceeds that of people 20-30 years younger
- **[[APOE Gene]]** — the strongest known single genetic risk factor for Alzheimer's disease, tested here alongside three polygenic risk scores
- **[[Polygenic Risk Score]]** — a composite score combining thousands of genetic variants associated with a condition's inherited risk

## Key Takeaways

- **No genetic edge found**: SuperAgers were statistically indistinguishable from average-cognition peers on APOE and polygenic Alzheimer's risk
- **Rare protective variants also absent**: previously identified resilience-associated rare variants weren't more common in SuperAgers either
- **Reframes the research question**: absence of Alzheimer's risk factors is not the same as presence of protective factors for exceptional cognition
- **Next steps**: researchers plan to combine cognitive testing with brain imaging, blood biomarkers, immune profiling, and sleep/activity monitoring to find what actually explains SuperAging

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Absence of Evidence Fallacy]]**: the study's core methodological point — that not having a known risk factor is not equivalent to possessing an active protective mechanism — is a specific, well-evidenced application of the broader principle that absence of evidence isn't evidence of absence (or, here, of a different underlying cause).
