## Summary

Researchers at the University of Maryland School of Medicine and the Institute of Human Virology developed the first successful human challenge model for [[Dengue]] serotype 4 (DENV-4), published in The Lancet Microbe. All 14 volunteers given a low 95-PFU dose developed detectable virus and mild-to-moderate symptoms, and the response gave durable, serotype-specific protection at least a year later. The model offers a controlled way to test vaccines and antivirals for a serotype that field trials have been unable to evaluate.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Human Challenge Model]]**: Deliberately exposing healthy volunteers to a virus in a controlled setting to test countermeasures.
- **[[Dengue Serotypes]]**: DENV-1 to DENV-4; infection protects against the same serotype but not necessarily the other three.
- **[[DENV-4]]**: Circulates slowly and unpredictably, sometimes decades between outbreaks, so phase 3 trials gave no reliable efficacy estimates.
- **[[Serotype-Specific Immunity]]**: Primary infection produced antibody responses, expanded antibody-producing cells and antiviral gene activation, largely absent on rechallenge.
- **[[Vaccine Development]]**: Challenge models let candidates be screened before larger clinical trials.
- **[[Kirsten Lyke]]** and **[[Joel Chua]]**: Principal investigator and immunology lead of the study.

## Key Takeaways

- **First DENV-4 Model**: First successful human challenge model for dengue serotype 4.
- **Low Dose**: 95 plaque-forming units, at least seven times less than earlier dengue studies.
- **Full Infection**: All 14 participants had detectable virus and mild-to-moderate symptoms.
- **Common Symptoms**: Rash and fatigue in every participant, then headaches.
- **Durable Protection**: Initial exposure protected against rechallenge at least one year later.
- **Safety Containment**: Participants stayed in a hotel while viremic to block mosquito spread.
- **Consortium Goal**: Builds on DENV-1 and DENV-3 models toward models for all four serotypes.
- **Caveat**: Small group of healthy adults; further research is needed.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Immunity to a pathogen variant is specific to that variant** — Infection with one dengue serotype generally gives lasting protection against that serotype, not the other three, so each must be studied and covered separately.
→ explains: Durable Protection, Consortium Goal

**T2. Controlled exposure yields evidence that uncontrolled field trials cannot** — When natural incidence is too rare or unpredictable to measure efficacy, deliberately controlled exposure of a small cohort can answer the question. · *model:* [[Controlled Experiment]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the researchers achieve?
**A**: They developed the first successful human challenge model for dengue serotype 4, published in The Lancet Microbe. It gives a controlled way to test vaccines and treatments for a strain that is hard to study in the field.

**Q2**: What evidence showed the model worked and that immunity was protective?
**A**: All 14 participants given 95 PFU developed detectable virus and mild-to-moderate symptoms. After the primary infection they showed strong antibody and antiviral gene responses that were largely absent on rechallenge, consistent with protection lasting at least a year.

**Q3**: Why does this matter for dengue vaccine development?
**A**: DENV-4 circulates too unpredictably for field trials to measure vaccine efficacy, and no antiviral exists. Challenge models let candidates be tested before larger trials, as part of a consortium effort covering all four serotypes.
