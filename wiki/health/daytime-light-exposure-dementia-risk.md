## Summary

A prospective cohort study of 87,577 adults found that brighter daytime light exposure is associated with lower dementia risk. Getting less than 42 minutes of bright daytime light per day predicted dementia better than six established risk factors, while nighttime light showed no significant link. The authors stress this is an association, not proof that more daylight prevents dementia.

## Core Concepts

- [[Dementia]]: 741 of the participants developed it over a median 8.1-year follow-up.
- [[Daytime Light Exposure]]: measured with wrist accelerometer devices; over 1,000 lux average linked to 16% lower risk.
- [[Lux]]: unit of light brightness; 1,000 lux is roughly an overcast day outdoors, and 5,000 lux or more gave further reductions.
- [[Nighttime Light Exposure]]: no statistically significant link to dementia risk.
- [[Prospective Cohort Study]]: design used in the *General Psychiatry* paper by Hongliang Feng and colleagues at Guangzhou Medical University.
- [[Dementia Risk Indicator]]: daytime light proposed as a novel indicator of risk.

## Key Takeaways

- **Sample Size**: 87,577 adults wore light-sensing wrist devices.
- **Risk Reduction**: Average daytime light above 1,000 lux linked to 16% lower dementia risk.
- **Brighter Better**: At least 5,000 lux for longer periods linked to further reductions.
- **Threshold**: Under 42 minutes of bright daytime light daily is a stronger predictor than six established risk factors.
- **Night Null**: Nighttime light exposure showed no significant association with dementia.
- **Caveat**: Findings show association; they do not prove daylight prevents dementia.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Association does not establish causation** — An observed link between exposure and outcome does not show that changing the exposure changes the outcome, so the finding marks daylight as a risk indicator worth further study. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
