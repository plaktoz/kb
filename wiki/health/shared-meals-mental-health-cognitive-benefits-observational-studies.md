## Summary
Two studies link eating with others to better emotional well-being and, in older adults, to better diets and larger brain volumes in memory-related regions. Even one communal meal in the past week was associated with measurable mental-health improvement. Both studies are observational, so they show associations rather than causation.

## Core Concepts
- [[Shared Meals]]: lunch or dinner with family or acquaintances, tracked over the previous week.
- [[Scientific Reports Study]]: over 150,000 people globally; diners with others reported higher life satisfaction, more positive and fewer negative emotions.
- [[npj Aging Study]]: 727 cognitively healthy older adults in Japan; solo eaters had lower nutrient intake and smaller brain volumes in memory and cognition regions.
- [[Nutrition and Cognitive Health]]: diets lacking protein, fiber, vitamins, and minerals among those who ate alone.
- [[Endorphins]]: proposed by some experts as a mechanism that lifts mood and lessens stress.
- [[Observational Studies]]: can indicate links but not establish direct causal relationships.

## Key Takeaways
- **Sample Size**: Scientific Reports study covered over 150,000 individuals worldwide.
- **Emotional Benefit**: Eating with others tied to higher life satisfaction and fewer negative feelings.
- **Single Meal**: Even one shared meal linked to measurable improvement.
- **Diet Quality**: Solo eaters in Japan showed lower protein, fiber, vitamin and mineral intake.
- **Brain Volume**: Solo eaters had smaller memory-related brain regions.
- **Unresolved Cause**: Diet, reduced social contact, or other variables could explain differences.
- **Endorphin Hypothesis**: Shared meals may release mood-lifting endorphins, per some experts.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core finding of the studies?
**A**: People who eat with others report better emotional well-being, and older adults who eat alone have poorer diets and smaller brain volumes in memory-related regions.

**Q2**: What evidence supports the link in the Japanese study?
**A**: In 727 cognitively healthy older adults, solo eaters had diets lacking protein, fiber, vitamins and minerals, and brain scans showed smaller volumes in regions critical to memory and cognition.

**Q3**: How should the findings be applied, and with what caution?
**A**: Adding social dining may improve nutritional quality and mental well-being. Because the studies are observational, they cannot prove that shared meals cause the benefits.
