## Summary

A study of 6,905 adults aged 65+ found that waist circumference is a stronger all-cause mortality predictor than BMI. High waist circumference (40+ inches for men, 35+ inches for women) was linked to a 24% higher mortality risk, while overweight and class I/II obesity BMI categories were paradoxically associated with lower mortality in older males. Underweight BMI carried the highest mortality risk overall.

## Core Concepts

The study, published in the *Journal of the American Geriatrics Society*, challenges the primacy of [[BMI]] as a health metric by showing that [[Waist Circumference]] better captures visceral and abdominal fat burden. [[Abdominal Fat]] presses on internal organs, creating metabolic and cardiovascular risk independent of overall body weight. The [[Obesity Paradox]] is illustrated here: in older adults, higher body weight may reflect preserved muscle mass and "less frailty" rather than true metabolic harm. [[Body Composition]] metrics that distinguish fat distribution from total weight are increasingly preferred in geriatric risk assessment.

## Key Takeaways

- **Waist circumference > BMI**: Abdominal fat affects internal organs; waist size captures this, BMI does not.
- **High-risk thresholds**: Waist 40+ inches (men) and 35+ inches (women) tied to 24% higher mortality.
- **Obesity paradox in older adults**: Overweight BMI linked to 46% lower mortality risk in males 65+.
- **BMI's blind spot in women**: Normal BMI can mask redistributed abdominal fat with high cardiovascular burden.
- **Underweight BMI most dangerous**: Lowest body weight category carried the highest overall mortality risk.
- **Study scope**: 6,905 adults aged 65+, data from 2011–2024.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Proxy Metric Failure]]**: BMI was designed as a population-level statistic, not a per-person health tool — when it becomes the clinical proxy for health risk, it systematically misclassifies individuals whose fat distribution diverges from the average, which is exactly what this study documents in older women and men.
- **[[Obesity Paradox]]**: In aging populations, weight can be a protective signal (preserved muscle, reduced frailty risk) rather than a hazard — interpreting any single metric without accounting for population-specific physiology produces misleading risk rankings.
