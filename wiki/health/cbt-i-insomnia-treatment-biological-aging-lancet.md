## Summary

A UCLA Health randomized controlled trial published in *The Lancet Healthy Longevity* found that [[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia]] (CBT-I) was associated with statistically significant slowing of biological aging — roughly 2% per year — as measured by the [[DunedinPACE]] epigenetic clock in adults aged 60+. Participants receiving CBT-I were nearly 3 times more likely to achieve insomnia remission than a sleep-education control group. The proposed mechanism is that treating insomnia reduces chronic stress-response activation, lowering inflammation and enabling cellular repair.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Cognitive Behavioral Therapy for Insomnia]] (CBT-I)**: A structured, non-pharmacological treatment targeting the thoughts and behaviors that perpetuate insomnia; here delivered as an 8-week program.
- **[[Epigenetic Clocks]]**: DNA methylation-based biomarkers that estimate biological age. This study used three: [[DunedinPACE]] (pace-of-aging), [[GrimAge]], and [[PC-PhenoAge]]; only DunedinPACE showed a significant signal at 2 years.
- **[[Biological Age vs. Chronological Age]]**: Biological age reflects cellular health and repair capacity; slowing it suggests reduced downstream disease risk even if chronological age is fixed.
- **[[Sleep-Stress-Inflammation Loop]]**: Disrupted sleep elevates the [[HPA Axis]] stress response, driving chronic inflammation and cellular damage — the pathway CBT-I appears to interrupt.

## Key Takeaways

- CBT-I recipients were ~3× more likely to achieve insomnia remission vs. control.
- DunedinPACE showed ~2% slower biological aging per year in CBT-I group.
- GrimAge and PC-PhenoAge did not show significant change over the 2-year window.
- Mechanism: CBT-I regulates the nervous system, enabling the body's repair processes.
- Study limitations: small (~100 participants), single-site, predominantly older adults.
- Epigenetic clock changes do not directly confirm longer lifespan or disease prevention.
- Sex-based differences and comparisons with drug-based sleep treatments remain unstudied.
- NIH funding constraints may limit follow-up research.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Upstream Intervention]]**: Treating insomnia addresses a root driver of inflammation and cellular aging rather than managing downstream disease symptoms — the same first-principles logic behind lifestyle interventions in preventive medicine.
- **[[Effect Size vs. Mechanism]]**: A 2% annual reduction in biological aging pace is a modest signal; the more actionable insight is that a behavioral intervention (not a drug) can shift an epigenetic biomarker, validating sleep quality as a modifiable aging lever.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this study?
**A**: Treating insomnia with CBT-I — rather than just sleep education — was associated with a statistically significant ~2% annual slowing of biological aging as measured by the DunedinPACE epigenetic clock in adults aged 60+.

**Q2**: Why did only DunedinPACE show a significant effect when GrimAge and PC-PhenoAge did not?
**A**: The study authors suggest GrimAge and PC-PhenoAge may be less sensitive to change over a 2-year window; DunedinPACE measures the current *pace* of aging rather than accumulated biological age, making it more responsive to short-term biological shifts.

**Q3**: What is the practical implication of this finding for aging adults with insomnia?
**A**: Seeking CBT-I treatment for insomnia may offer benefits beyond better sleep — potentially slowing biological aging — supporting the view that sleep quality is a meaningful, modifiable lever in healthy longevity.
