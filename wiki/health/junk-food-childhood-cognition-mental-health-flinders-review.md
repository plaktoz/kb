## Summary
A Flinders University systematic review of 17 Australian studies links energy-dense, nutrient-poor diets in childhood and adolescence to poorer mental health, cognitive and academic outcomes. Healthy dietary patterns were linked to better outcomes, while cardiometabolic results were mixed. The authors call for nutrition education paired with structural changes to affordability and access.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Systematic Review]]**: Pooled 17 studies, including Western Australia's [[Raine Study]], published in Current Nutrition Reports.
- **[[Ultra-Processed Foods]]**: Energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and sugary drinks tied to worse youth outcomes.
- **[[Dietary Patterns]]**: Fruit, vegetables, whole grains, fish, legumes and dairy versus fatty, sweet, processed foods and red meat.
- **[[Childhood Mental Health]]**: One in three Australian children aged 8-13 reports a mental health problem.
- **[[Socioeconomic Determinants of Health]]**: Affordability and access shape what children eat.
- **People**: [[Zegeye Abebe Abitew]] (first author), [[Yohannes Melaku]] (senior author), Flinders University.

## Key Takeaways
- **Diet and Mind**: Poor diets are associated with worse mental health and academic results in young people.
- **Healthy Pattern**: Fruit, vegetable, whole grain and fish diets track with better cognition.
- **Mixed Metabolic Data**: Cardiometabolic outcomes were inconsistent, needing standardised dietary measures.
- **Inflammation**: Antioxidant-rich diets may reduce inflammation, supporting mental and cognitive development.
- **Structural Fix**: Education alone is insufficient given cost-of-living pressure on healthy food access.
- **Modifiable Drivers**: Diet, screen time and inactivity largely drive childhood chronic disease burden.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the Flinders review?
**A**: High intake of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and sugary drinks is associated with worse mental health, wellbeing and academic results in Australian young people. Healthy dietary patterns are associated with better outcomes.

**Q2**: What evidence base did the review draw on, and where was it inconclusive?
**A**: It drew on 17 studies, including the Raine Study and data from South Australia, Victoria and other states. Cardiometabolic outcomes were mixed, and the researchers say standardised methods are needed.

**Q3**: What do the authors recommend as a response?
**A**: Pair nutrition education for parents, educators and young people with structural changes that improve the affordability and access of healthy foods. Education alone ignores cost-of-living pressures.
