## Summary

A JAMA study using Nebraska's prescription-tracking database found that compounded GLP-1s rose from zero in 2018 to 86% of all compounded weight-loss prescriptions in 2025. Many contain additives such as vitamin B12 analogs and levocarnitine that have not been tested for safety alongside GLP-1s. The authors call for better tracking and safety standards, and urge doctors to ask patients about compounded use.

## Core Concepts

- [[GLP-1 Receptor Agonists]]: weight-loss medications whose use has skyrocketed in recent years.
- [[Compounded Medications]]: products mixing GLP-1s with other active substances, some not FDA-regulated.
- [[Vitamin B12 Analogs]] (cyanocobalamin) and [[Levocarnitine]]: common additives marketed as preventing side effects.
- [[Joey Mattingly]]: University of Utah pharmacy researcher and first author of the study.
- [[JAMA]]: journal that published the study.
- [[Cash-Pay Healthcare Markets]]: spending that is invisible to insurance claims data.

## Key Takeaways

- **Market Growth**: Compounded GLP-1 prescriptions went from zero in 2018 to 86% in 2025.
- **Common Additives**: Vitamin B12 analogs and levocarnitine are frequently coformulated with GLP-1s.
- **Unproven Benefit**: Little evidence shows additives prevent GLP-1 side effects.
- **Unknown Safety**: These drug combinations have not been tested for safety.
- **Data Blind Spot**: Cash-pay purchases do not appear in claims data or medication histories.
- **Clinical Advice**: Doctors should ask patients specifically about compounded GLP-1 use.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the JAMA study?
**A**: Compounded GLP-1s now make up a large majority of compounded weight-loss prescriptions, and they commonly contain additives such as vitamin B12 analogs that have not been safety-tested in combination with GLP-1s.

**Q2**: Why did researchers lack a systematic picture of compounded GLP-1 dispensing, and how did the study get around it?
**A**: The market is nearly all cash-pay, so it is invisible to the insurance claims data researchers usually use. The study used Nebraska's unusually comprehensive prescription tracking database, which showed a rise from zero in 2018 to 86% of compounded weight-loss prescriptions in 2025.

**Q3**: What should clinicians do in response?
**A**: Ask patients specifically about compounded GLP-1 use, since many buy online with cash and it will not show up in claims or pharmacy records. The authors also call for better tracking and safety standards.
