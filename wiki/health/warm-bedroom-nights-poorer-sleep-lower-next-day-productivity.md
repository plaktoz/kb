## Summary
A home-based study of 82 Austrian adults over 1,189 nights found that nights warmer than a person's own average bedroom temperature went with slightly worse sleep and slightly lower self-reported focus and productivity the next day. The effects were small, observational, and not shown to run through poorer sleep. Average bedroom temperature across households showed no significant link to sleep or productivity.

## Core Concepts
- [[Bedroom Microclimate]]: Temperature, humidity, and carbon dioxide in the sleeping space, logged every five seconds.
- [[Perceived Productivity]]: Self-rated focus and capability, measured by [[Ecological Momentary Assessment]] on a five-point scale.
- [[Sleep Quality]]: Objective [[Oura Ring]] sleep score, restless periods, [[REM Sleep]], and deep sleep.
- [[Work Recovery Model]]: Rest replenishes the attention and self-control used during the day.
- [[Autonomic Nervous System]]: Tested via daytime heart rate; it did not bridge hot nights and productivity.
- [[Christian Rominger]]: Lead researcher at the University of Hildesheim, with Graz and Vienna colleagues.

## Key Takeaways
- **Sleep Score**: About 0.5 points lost on a 100-point scale per extra degree Celsius.
- **Productivity Dip**: About 0.04 points on a five-point scale per extra degree.
- **Within-Person Effect**: Only warmer-than-usual nights mattered, not household averages.
- **Sleep Stages**: Warmer nights meant more restless periods and shorter REM sleep.
- **Deep Sleep**: Longer deep sleep tied to next-day focus, but not significantly to heat.
- **Carbon Dioxide Surprise**: Higher CO2 nights went with slightly better sleep scores.
- **Limits**: Self-report productivity, young office-worker sample, mild summer, no cooling-habit records.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Observational links don't establish cause** — Tracking people as they live can show that two things move together, but it cannot show that one produces the other. The study stresses it cannot say bedroom heat caused the dips, and that each link was tested separately, so sleep as the route is unproven. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Productivity Dip, Limits

**T2. Rest replenishes limited daytime mental resources** — The authors reason that attention and self-control are spent during the day and restored by adequate sleep, so degraded sleep should mean a weaker start the next morning. · *(model unnamed)*
→ explains: Sleep Score, Productivity Dip

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about bedroom heat?
**A**: When a bedroom was warmer than a person's own usual average, sleep scores dropped slightly and self-reported focus and productivity the next day dipped slightly. The effect did not appear in comparisons of average temperatures across households.

**Q2**: What specific numbers and measures support the result?
**A**: Across 1,189 nights, sleep score fell about half a point per extra degree Celsius and perceived productivity fell about 0.04 points on a five-point scale. Data came from air monitors, Oura Rings, and Apple Watch check-ins.

**Q3**: How cautiously should the results be applied?
**A**: The study was observational, effects were small, and productivity was self-reported, so it does not prove cooling a bedroom protects productivity. The authors suggest future trials testing bedroom cooling strategies.
