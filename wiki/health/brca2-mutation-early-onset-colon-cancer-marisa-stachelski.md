## Summary

Marisa Stachelski was diagnosed with stage 1 colon cancer at 37 after persistent bloating and GI issues led to a colonoscopy revealing a cancerous polyp. Subsequent genetic testing uncovered a BRCA2 mutation — significantly elevating her lifetime risk for multiple cancers — despite no obvious family history. Her case illustrates how early-onset cancer diagnoses should trigger genetic counseling and how BRCA2 carriers face complex, cascading risk-reduction decisions.

## Core Concepts

- **[[BRCA2 Mutation]]**: A hereditary genetic variant that significantly elevates lifetime risk for breast, ovarian, fallopian tube, pancreatic, and prostate cancers; does not independently alter colon cancer screening protocols.
- **[[Colorectal Cancer]]**: Early-onset presentation (before 50) is a key indicator for genetic testing referral; caught at stage 1, the tumor was removed surgically with no further treatment required.
- **[[Genetic Testing]]** and **[[Genetic Counseling]]**: Recommended for early-onset cancers, multiple cancers, strong family history, or known hereditary mutations; a genetic counselor assesses appropriateness.
- **[[Risk-Reduction Surgery]]**: Stachelski chose a nipple-sparing double mastectomy and fallopian tube removal (salpingectomy) over full hysterectomy, balancing cancer prevention against early menopause.
- **[[Fallopian Tube Origin Hypothesis]]**: Emerging research suggests many ovarian cancers originate in the fallopian tubes; a Mount Sinai clinical trial exploring this guided Stachelski's partial surgical approach.
- **[[Cascade Genetic Testing]]**: Family outreach revealed a paternal cousin had been diagnosed with stage 3 colon cancer two months prior — underscoring the importance of testing relatives when a mutation is found.
- **[[Patient Advocacy]]**: Stachelski launched the podcast *BRCA and Beyond* in August 2025, partnering with BRCA Strong, Lynch Syndrome Awareness, the National Society of Genetic Counselors, and FORCE.

## Key Takeaways

- **Symptom Delay**: Severe bloating mimicked pregnancy; dietary changes preceded correct workup by months.
- **Blood in Stool**: A dietitian-ordered stool test first flagged occult blood, prompting colonoscopy.
- **Stage 1 Outcome**: Early detection meant surgery alone was curative — no chemotherapy or radiation.
- **BRCA2 Surprise**: No maternal family history of breast/ovarian cancer; father died of pancreatic cancer but was never tested.
- **Colon Screening Unchanged**: BRCA2 alone does not change colorectal screening guidelines per UT Southwestern specialist.
- **Ovary Delay**: Fallopian tubes removed; ovaries retained to avoid early menopause; ovary removal recommended by age 45.
- **Stomach Cancer Link**: Precancerous stomach tissue found 7 months post-diagnosis; paternal grandmother died of stomach cancer.
- **Surveillance Load**: Annual breast/plastic surgery follow-ups, biennial colonoscopies and endoscopies, annual dermatology screenings, pancreatic cancer trial enrollment.
- **Social Media Impact**: Instagram posts directly prompted a follower to get a mammogram that detected early breast cancer.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Iceberg Model]]**: The visible symptom (bloating) concealed a cascade of underlying genetic risk — the stage 1 cancer and BRCA2 mutation were invisible until the right diagnostic probe was applied, illustrating how surface presentations can mask systemic root causes.
- **[[Optionality]]**: By choosing fallopian tube removal over full hysterectomy, Stachelski preserved a reversible option (delayed ovary removal), trading some risk reduction for the ability to avoid early menopause while evidence and guidelines evolve.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What was the central finding that transformed Stachelski's cancer diagnosis into a broader genetic health story?
**A**: Routine genetic testing following her stage 1 colon cancer diagnosis revealed a BRCA2 mutation, significantly elevating her lifetime risk for breast, ovarian, fallopian tube, pancreatic, and prostate cancers despite no obvious family history.

**Q2**: Why did Stachelski choose fallopian tube removal over a full hysterectomy, and what guideline governs the remaining step?
**A**: She enrolled in a Mount Sinai clinical trial exploring evidence that ovarian cancers may originate in the fallopian tubes, opting to remove them while delaying ovary removal to avoid early menopause at 38; current guidelines recommend ovary removal by age 45.

**Q3**: What practical implication does this case carry for people with early-onset or unexplained cancers?
**A**: Early-onset cancers (before 50), multiple cancers, or a strong family history are indications to seek genetic counseling, since an underlying hereditary mutation like BRCA2 can be present without obvious family history and has cascading implications for screening and prevention.
