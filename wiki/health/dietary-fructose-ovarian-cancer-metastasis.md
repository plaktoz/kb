## Summary
A Wistar Institute study found that ovarian cancer cells surviving chemotherapy release fructose as a signal that helps neighboring tumor cells spread, by suppressing the cholesterol that normally holds cells together. The researchers also found that dietary fructose alone — independent of chemotherapy — can trigger the same spreading signal, raising questions about diet's role in cancer progression and about combining statins with chemotherapy.

## Key Takeaways
- **Core Mechanism**: Chemo-surviving ovarian cancer cells release fructose as a spread-signal.
- **Cholesterol Link**: Fructose suppresses cholesterol, weakening cell-to-cell adhesion.
- **Dietary Signal**: High dietary fructose alone can also promote cancer spread.
- **Exposure Scale**: High-fructose corn syrup makes up 8-20% of daily calories for some.
- **Metastasis Toll**: Spread accounts for roughly 90% of ovarian cancer deaths.
- **Statin Caution**: Statins alone reduced cell adhesion in the study's models.
- **Cross-Cancer Hypothesis**: Researchers suspect pancreatic, colon, and liver cancers may behave similarly.
- **Preclinical Stage**: Findings are unconfirmed in patients; no dietary guidance change yet.

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: What is the core discovery linking fructose to ovarian cancer spread?
**A**: Chemotherapy-surviving ovarian cancer cells release fructose as a signal that promotes metastatic spread in neighboring tumor cells.

**Q2**: Through what cellular mechanism does fructose promote cancer spread?
**A**: Fructose suppresses cholesterol production in nearby cells; since cholesterol acts as adhesive "glue" between cells, lower cholesterol lets cancer cells detach and spread more easily.

**Q3**: What clinical question does this finding raise about a common drug class?
**A**: It raises questions about whether statins — taken by 39 million people in the US and also cholesterol-lowering — could interfere with chemotherapy by promoting the same cell-detachment effect.
