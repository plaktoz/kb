## Summary

A Harvard-led study of over 200,000 adults tracked across four decades found that overall diet quality — not level of food processing — predicts risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and death. Within both "healthy" and "unhealthy" diet categories, outcomes were nearly identical regardless of whether foods were ultra-processed or minimally processed, challenging the common advice to universally avoid [[Ultra-Processed Foods]].

## Core Concepts

- **[[Nova Classification]]** — the food-processing categorization system used to sort foods, which the study argues doesn't account for nutritional value and can mislabel healthy foods (fortified cereal, plant-based dairy) as harmful
- **[[Plant-Based Diet Index]]** — the framework used to separate diet quality from processing level across the three pooled longitudinal cohorts
- **[[Diet Quality vs. Processing Level]]** — the study's central distinction: quality (nutrient density) drives cardiometabolic risk far more than whether a food underwent industrial processing

## Key Takeaways

- **Study scale**: 200,000+ U.S. adults followed from the 1980s through the 2020s across three pooled cohorts
- **Core finding**: healthy diets lowered risk and unhealthy diets raised it, regardless of processing level within each quality tier
- **Practical guidance**: prioritize nutrient-dense foods overall rather than filtering by processing level alone — a healthy plant-based diet with some processed foods still outperforms an unhealthy "minimally processed" diet
- **Caveat**: findings are preliminary, presented at a conference but not yet peer-reviewed
