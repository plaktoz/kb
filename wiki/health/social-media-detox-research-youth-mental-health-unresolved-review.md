## Summary

A scoping review of 45 studies on interventions to reduce social media use, published in [[Communications Psychology]], found widespread methodological limitations and very little long-term data, leaving the effect of [[Digital Detox]] on youth mental health unresolved. Only one study had a participant sample with an average age below 16, a key gap given that governments are pursuing [[Social Media Restrictions for Minors]] using such evidence. The authors stress the review does not show that reducing use is ineffective, only that current evidence is insufficient, and argue for personalized interventions over broad bans.

## Core Concepts

- **Scoping review**: Researchers at [[Stony Brook University]], [[Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center]] and others screened 12,058 records and selected 45 studies published 2015–2025.
- **Mixed meta-analytic evidence**: Prior [[Meta-Analysis|meta-analyses]] found reductions in social media use yield small, inconsistent or no detectable mental-health improvements.
- **Seven-domain framework**: 76 predefined questions across theoretical grounding, intervention design, study design and causal architecture, compliance monitoring, outcome measurement, sampling and generalizability, and [[Open Science]] practices.
- **Causal inference**: Design features determine whether a study can reliably attribute effects to reduced use. See [[Correlation vs Causation]].
- **Heterogeneous effects**: Senior author [[John Torous]] says social media can harm some people, be neutral for others, and help some.
- **Personalized interventions**: Torous argues the future lies in tailored approaches rather than broad bans.
- **Follow-up work**: The team is running a large digital detox study applying the review's lessons.

## Key Takeaways

- **Scale**: 12,058 records screened; 45 studies from 2015–2025 included.
- **Methodological Gaps**: Widespread limitations may partly explain mixed results.
- **Age Gap**: Only one study had average participant age below 16.
- **Long-Term Blind Spot**: Very little data on long-term outcomes of detoxes.
- **Not Ineffective**: Review shows insufficient evidence, not that reduction fails.
- **Varied Impact**: Social media harms some, is neutral for others, helps some.
- **Personalized Over Bans**: Torous favors tailored interventions over broad restrictions.
- **Framework Caveat**: Seven domains are descriptive, not an equally weighted scoring rubric.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Inconclusive evidence is not proof of no effect** — When studies are methodologically weak, mixed or null findings mean the question is unresolved, not that the intervention does nothing. · *model:* [[Absence of Evidence Is Not Evidence of Absence]]
→ explains: Not Ineffective, Methodological Gaps, Long-Term Blind Spot

**T2. Evidence only supports conclusions about the populations it studied** — Findings from samples that differ from a policy's target group cannot reliably justify that policy for the target group. · *model:* [[External Validity]]
→ explains: Age Gap, Framework Caveat

**T3. Average effects hide individual variation** — When an exposure harms some, helps others and leaves the rest unaffected, population-wide averages look small or inconsistent and uniform interventions fit poorly.
→ explains: Varied Impact, Personalized Over Bans

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the review conclude about social media detox research and youth mental health?
**A**: Research quality is limited with little long-term data, so the available evidence is insufficient to support firm conclusions about how reducing social media use affects youth mental health.

**Q2**: What key sampling gap did the review identify?
**A**: Of the 45 studies reviewed, only one had a participant sample with an average age below 16, even though restriction policies target children and younger adolescents.

**Q3**: What does the review imply for social media policy?
**A**: Because effects appear personalized — harmful for some, neutral or helpful for others — Torous argues future approaches should rely on personalized interventions rather than simply broad bans.
