## Summary
A national study of 17,942 LGBTQ+ college students found that those who experienced discrimination were far more likely to report depression, anxiety, and suicidal thoughts, and to avoid needed care. Denial of health care carried the greatest risk. The researchers point to campus counseling services and better clinician training as intervention points.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Healthy Minds Study]]**: The 2024-2025 national survey of college student mental health that supplied the data.
- **[[Discrimination and Health]]**: Interpersonal discrimination, health care denial, and service denial based on sexual orientation or gender identity.
- **[[Suicidal Ideation]]**: Elevated across every subgroup and every type of discrimination examined.
- **[[Emerging Adulthood]]**: A pivotal period for lifelong health, including the shift from pediatric to adult care.
- **[[Campus Counseling Services]]**: A high-use setting for identifying distress and providing affirming care.
- **[[Gender-Affirming Care]]**: Denial of care weighs especially on transgender and nonbinary students.
- **[[Sarah McKetta]]**: Columbia Mailman epidemiologist and co-first author.
- **[[Sarah K. Lipson]]**: Co-first author, Boston University School of Public Health.

## Key Takeaways
- **Scale**: Nearly 18,000 LGBTQ+ students across 119 colleges in 34 states.
- **Prevalence**: One in 10 LGBTQ+ students reported campus discrimination in the previous year.
- **Risk Increase**: Discrimination linked to 84% higher depression, 87% higher anxiety, 78% higher suicidal thoughts.
- **Care Denial**: Health care denial linked to over 200% higher suicidal ideation likelihood.
- **Baseline Need**: Over half met depression criteria; transgender and nonbinary students reported higher anxiety and suicidality.
- **Care Avoidance**: About 5% of LGBQ+ and 13% of transgender and nonbinary students avoided care.
- **Intervention Point**: High counseling use makes campus counseling a route to identify distress.
- **Training**: Better LGBTQ+ health training for clinicians can reduce discriminatory experiences.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Exposure to discrimination raises psychological risk** — The study finds that discrimination exposure increased suicidal ideation across every subgroup and discrimination type examined, so reducing exposure is part of prevention.
→ explains: Risk Increase, Care Denial, Baseline Need

**T2. Life transitions are high-leverage points for intervention** — Behavioral health in emerging adulthood predicts later health, and the loss of established providers during the transition creates barriers, so affirming care at this stage has lasting benefits.
→ explains: Intervention Point, Care Avoidance, Training

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the study conclude about discrimination and LGBTQ+ student mental health?
**A**: Students who experienced discrimination were significantly more likely to have depression, anxiety, and suicidal thoughts, and to avoid needed health care.

**Q2**: What evidence shows the size of the risk from health care denial?
**A**: Among LGBTQ+ students, denial of health care was associated with a more than 200 percent higher likelihood of suicidal ideation. Overall discrimination was tied to 84% higher depression, 87% higher anxiety, and 78% higher suicidal thoughts.

**Q3**: How can colleges act on these findings?
**A**: Treat discrimination as part of suicide prevention, use counseling services to identify distress and provide affirming care, and improve LGBTQ+ health training for clinicians and campus providers.
