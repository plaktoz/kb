# Blood test trends may predict cancer risk before nonspecific symptoms like unexplained weight loss

## Summary

A [[PLOS Medicine]] study of 275,205 patients with unexplained weight loss found that tracking longitudinal trends across 26 common blood tests — rather than a single reading — could flag [[Cancer Risk]] roughly 3 to 5 years before symptoms appeared, with nearly 14,000 patients later receiving a cancer diagnosis within six months. Adjusting for age and sex pushed predictive accuracy to an AUC of 0.82, and specific marker trends correlated with specific cancer types. Clinicians caution the approach needs external validation before real-world use to avoid excess false alarms.

## Core Concepts

- [[Unexplained Weight Loss]] as an early, nonspecific cancer warning sign studied via retrospective cohort of 275,205 patients.
- [[Longitudinal Biomarker Trends]] versus single-timepoint blood test snapshots for [[Early Cancer Detection]].
- 26 common blood tests tracked over time, including [[Hemoglobin]], red blood cells, [[C-Reactive Protein]] (CRP), [[Erythrocyte Sedimentation Rate]] (ESR), plasma viscosity, and hematocrit/RDW.
- Cancer-specific marker signatures: white blood count and neutrophil trends linked to [[Lung Cancer]]; mean cell volume linked to [[Bowel Cancer]] and [[Lymphoma]]; multiple markers linked to [[Prostate Cancer]].
- Individualized baseline reasoning — evaluating each patient's own trajectory rather than population reference ranges.

## Key Takeaways

- **Study scope**: 275,205 patients with unexplained weight loss; ~14,000 diagnosed with cancer within 6 months.
- **Lead time**: Detectable blood marker shifts occurred 3–5 years before unexplained weight loss presented.
- **Predictive power**: Age/sex-adjusted models reached an AUC as high as 0.82.
- **Subtle signals**: Many shifts stayed within normal reference ranges except specific markers by age group.
- **Cancer-specific patterns**: Different marker trends map to lung, bowel/lymphoma, and prostate cancers.
- **Recency matters**: Trends from the most recent year were the most accurate predictors.
- **Limitations**: Relied on patients with existing blood test records; testing bias across groups wasn't measured.
- **Clinical caution**: Overuse risks "false alarms and unnecessary investigations" per Dr. Ketan Thanki.
- **Next step**: External validation needed before this trend-based approach reaches clinical practice.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Base Rate Fallacy]]**: A single abnormal blood value looks alarming in isolation, but comparing it to a patient's own historical trend — rather than a population reference range — better separates true signal from normal individual variation.
- **[[Signal Detection Theory]]**: The tradeoff between catching more true cancer cases earlier and generating more false alarms/unnecessary investigations is exactly the sensitivity-specificity tradeoff this study's AUC of 0.82 attempts to quantify.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the PLOS Medicine study on blood tests and cancer risk?
**A**: Tracking changes in 26 common blood tests over time (rather than a single reading) can flag elevated cancer risk 3–5 years before patients present with unexplained weight loss, reaching an AUC of 0.82 after age/sex adjustment.

**Q2**: What kind of evidence supports linking blood test trends to specific cancers?
**A**: Different marker trends correlated with different cancers — white blood count and neutrophil trends with lung cancer, mean cell volume with bowel cancer and lymphoma, and several markers with prostate cancer — drawn from 275,205 patients, ~14,000 of whom were diagnosed with cancer within six months.

**Q3**: What is the practical implication of this research for clinical practice?
**A**: Clinicians could eventually screen at-risk patients using longitudinal blood test trajectories rather than isolated readings, but Dr. Ketan Thanki cautions that external validation and demonstrable clinical improvement are needed first to avoid triggering unnecessary investigations.
