## Summary

Healthline reports on a 2-year Keck Medicine of USC trial in which high-dose DHA raised brain and blood omega-3 levels but did not improve memory, thinking or brain structure in older adults at higher Alzheimer's risk. Outside and lead experts caution that two years may be too short and that supplements may not work without a broader lifestyle approach. They recommend exercise, sleep, a Mediterranean or MIND-style diet and heart-health management.

## Core Concepts

- [[Omega-3 Fatty Acids]]: Essential nutrients the body cannot make, taken as fish oil or other supplements.
- [[Docosahexaenoic Acid (DHA)]]: The omega-3 tested at 2,000 mg daily; the trial supplement was not made from fish.
- [[Hussein Yassine]]: Lead researcher at the USC Center for Personalized Brain Health.
- [[Dung Trinh]]: Uninvolved internist who interprets the findings.
- [[APOE4]]: Alzheimer's risk gene carried by 47% of participants; DHA reached the brain equally well with or without it.
- [[Omega-3 Index]]: Red blood cell EPA and DHA measure that rose from 4.9% to 11%.
- [[Cerebrospinal Fluid]]: DHA levels there rose 17%, confirming brain delivery.
- [[Mediterranean Diet]] and [[MIND Diet]]: Recommended dietary patterns that emphasise fish.
- [[Alzheimer's Disease Prevention]]: Presented as needing a broad, multi-habit approach.

## Key Takeaways

- **Null Result**: No difference from placebo in memory, thinking or brain structure.
- **Delivery Confirmed**: Omega-3 reached the brain, resolving whether earlier trials failed to deliver.
- **Trial Scale**: 365 enrolled aged 55-80; 225 completed; 2,000 mg DHA or placebo daily.
- **Prevalence**: 21.8% of US adults aged 60+ took omega-3 supplements in 2017-2018.
- **Metabolism Caveat**: Obesity, smoking and inactivity can reduce omega-3 reaching the brain.
- **Duration Caveat**: Alzheimer's develops over decades; two years may not show prevention.
- **Possible Beneficiaries**: Non-fish eaters and people with very low omega-3 levels.
- **Brain Habits**: 150 minutes weekly exercise, 7-9 hours sleep, heart health, social and mental activity.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Raising a biomarker is not the same as improving the outcome** — Omega-3 levels rose sharply in blood and brain fluid without any clinical gain in cognition or brain structure. · *model:* [[Surrogate Endpoint Fallacy]]
→ explains: Null Result, Delivery Confirmed

**T2. Isolated inputs may fail where the whole system succeeds** — Raising one nutrient level may not be sufficient without changing the holistic program, since the body's metabolism and lifestyle shape the effect. · *model:* [[Reductionism]]
→ explains: Metabolism Caveat, Brain Habits

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the 2-year trial find about omega-3 supplements and Alzheimer's risk?
**A**: High-dose DHA did not improve memory, thinking skills or brain structure in older adults at increased Alzheimer's risk. Scores matched the placebo group.

**Q2**: What data showed the supplement reached the brain yet did not help?
**A**: DHA in cerebrospinal fluid rose 17% and the omega-3 index rose from 4.9% to 11%, but clinical outcomes did not change. Lead researcher Yassine called it a massive increase that does not translate into clinical outcomes.

**Q3**: What should older adults do about brain health, given these results?
**A**: Pursue a broader approach: regular exercise, 7-9 hours of sleep, a Mediterranean or MIND diet, heart-health management, and mental and social engagement. People taking omega-3s for other reasons, such as high triglycerides, should decide with their healthcare professional.
