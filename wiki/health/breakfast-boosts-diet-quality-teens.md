## Summary

A six-month clinical trial found that habitually breakfast-skipping teens who started eating breakfast — especially a higher-protein one — improved diet quality, gaining more fiber, iron, zinc, and choline while cutting back on desserts, sweets, and added sugar, even though total daily calorie intake stayed roughly constant.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Habitual Breakfast Skipping]]**: The study population — 128 adolescents aged 14–21 who skipped breakfast about six times a week — reflecting a common but understudied dietary pattern in older teens.
- **[[Protein-Rich Breakfast Intervention]]**: A ~350-calorie breakfast with ~30g protein (vs. 11g in a standard-protein version) produced the largest gains in nutrient intake and reductions in added sugar.
- **[[Nutrient Displacement]]**: Adding breakfast didn't raise total daily calories (~2,200/day across all groups) — it shifted the composition of what teens ate, crowding out desserts and sugar rather than adding net intake.

## Key Takeaways

- **Both breakfast-eating groups gained more fiber, iron, zinc, and choline** than the breakfast-skipping group; the higher-protein group gained additional iron, choline, and overall protein.
- **Breakfast eaters consumed fewer calories from desserts, sweets, and candy**, with the effect most pronounced at the three-month mark.
- **Only the higher-protein breakfast group cut back on added sugar** compared to both the standard-protein and skipping groups.
- Presented at NUTRITION 2026 (American Society for Nutrition annual meeting) as one of the largest and longest tightly controlled breakfast trials in adolescents to date.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A six-month controlled trial found that adding breakfast — particularly a high-protein one — meaningfully improves nutrient intake and diet quality in teens who habitually skip the meal, without increasing total daily calories.

**Q2**: What specific nutritional difference distinguished the higher-protein breakfast group from the standard-protein group?
**A**: The higher-protein group (about 30g protein vs. 11g) gained additional iron, choline, and overall protein intake, and was the only group that reduced added sugar compared to both other groups.

**Q3**: Why is the finding that total calorie intake stayed the same across all three groups significant?
**A**: It shows breakfast's benefit came from displacing lower-quality food choices (desserts, added sugar) later in the day rather than simply adding calories — supporting breakfast as a diet-quality lever rather than a weight-management risk.
