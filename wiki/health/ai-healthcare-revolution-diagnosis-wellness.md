## Summary
Georgia Tech researchers describe AI moving beyond hospital administrative tasks into clinical decision support, earlier mental health risk identification, and expanded care access via smartphones and wearables — while cautioning that questions of trust, equity, and human oversight remain unresolved. "The use of AI is encouraged, but reliance on AI is not," said Jon Duke, director of Georgia Tech's Center for Health Analytics & Informatics.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Clinical Decision Support]]** — AI tools like OpenEvidence give clinicians source-backed answers, plus AI-generated notes and chart summaries within electronic health records
- **[[Proactive Mental Health Monitoring]]** — AI helps public health systems shift from reactive to proactive by identifying subtle behavioral changes in digital/clinical data before crisis points
- **[[Wearable Sensing]]** — smartphones and smartwatches generate more health data than any standalone medical device, making them a natural AI focus, especially for rural/underserved access
- **[[Human-AI Fusion]]** — the framing that AI should strengthen, not replace, clinical judgment and patient relationships

## Key Takeaways
- **Administrative-to-clinical shift**: AI's earliest healthcare wins were efficiency-focused; it is now moving into meaningful clinical decision support.
- **Open question**: whether physicians trained alongside AI will develop the same independent clinical judgment as prior generations.
- **Access gains**: AI-enabled wearables and remote monitoring are framed as especially valuable for rural and underserved populations.
- **Chronic disease caveat**: despite promise, AI "hasn't delivered much in the way of real-world applications" for chronic disease management, per researcher Rosa Arriaga.
- **Risk flagged**: greater AI interaction may increase isolation and parasocial AI relationships, even as RCTs show some patients benefit from AI-delivered "therapy."

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: What is Jon Duke's stated position on AI reliance in clinical practice?
**A**: "The use of AI is encouraged, but reliance on AI is not" — AI should support, not replace, clinical judgment.
**Q2**: Why are smartphones and wearables considered a natural focus for healthcare AI?
**A**: According to researcher Alexander Adams, no stand-alone medical devices generate nearly as much data as smartphones and smartwatches, making them a rich source for AI-driven monitoring.
**Q3**: What risk did researcher Rosa Arriaga raise about growing reliance on AI in healthcare?
**A**: That increased AI interaction could lead to greater isolation and parasocial relationships with AI that erode the social fabric, even as some patients benefit from AI-delivered therapeutic interventions.
