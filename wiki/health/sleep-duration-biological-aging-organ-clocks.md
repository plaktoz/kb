## Summary

A Nature study using 23 organ-specific "aging clocks" across half a million UK Biobank participants found a U-shaped relationship between sleep duration and biological aging: sleeping between 6.4 and 7.8 hours a night was associated with the slowest aging across the body, while both shorter and longer sleep correlated with faster aging and worse chronic-disease outcomes.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Aging Clocks]]**: Machine-learning models estimating biological (not chronological) age from biological data, built for 17 different organs in this study rather than just the whole body.
- **[[U-Shaped Sleep-Aging Curve]]**: Both under-6-hour and over-8-hour sleep correlated with faster biological aging relative to the 6.4-7.8 hour range, across nearly every organ measured.
- **[[Short-Sleep Mechanism]]**: Immune dysregulation, systemic inflammation, disrupted glucose regulation, and elevated sympathetic nervous system activity are proposed mechanisms linking short sleep to faster cellular aging.
- **[[Long-Sleep Confounding]]**: Experts note long sleep may often be a symptom of underlying illness rather than a cause of faster aging — reverse causation is plausible for the "too much sleep" side of the curve.
- **[[Late-Life Depression Mediation]]**: Mediation analysis suggested short sleep directly worsens late-life depression's disease burden, while long sleep's association with depression runs through a separate brain/fat-tissue aging pathway.

## Key Takeaways

- Sleeping 6.4-7.8 hours nightly was associated with the slowest biological aging across brain and body in this study.
- Short sleep (under 6 hours) was linked to depression, anxiety, obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and arrhythmias.
- Both short and long sleep were linked to COPD, asthma, and GI disorders like GERD and gastritis.
- CDC guidance: at least 7 hours for adults 18-60, 7-9 hours for 61-64, and 7-8 hours for 65+.
- Practical tip from outside experts: build in an 8-hour sleep *window* (even if actual sleep lands near 7 hours) and keep a consistent wake time to stabilize circadian rhythm.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A large biobank study using organ-specific aging clocks found that sleeping 6.4 to 7.8 hours a night is associated with the slowest biological aging, while both shorter and longer sleep correlate with faster aging across nearly every organ measured.

**Q2**: What's a specific mechanism proposed for why short sleep accelerates aging?
**A**: Short sleep is linked to immune dysregulation and increased systemic inflammation, which impairs tissue repair and metabolic regulation, plus elevated sympathetic nervous system activity — all plausible drivers of faster cellular aging.

**Q3**: What practical sleep guidance follows from this research?
**A**: Aim for a consistent nightly sleep window in the 6.4-7.8 hour range (roughly 7 hours), keep a stable wake time to support circadian rhythm, and treat persistently short or unusually long sleep as a signal worth investigating rather than ignoring.
