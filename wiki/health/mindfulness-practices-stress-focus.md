## Summary

Mindfulness — defined by Harvard's Ronald Siegel as paying attention to present experience with loving acceptance — is a distinct category within meditation aimed at redirecting attention toward sensory reality rather than silencing thought. Evidence supports its role in easing perceived stress, sustaining attention, and building emotional resilience. Three accessible techniques (mindful breathing, walking, and eating) serve as practical entry points for beginners.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Mindfulness vs. Meditation]]**: Meditation is any mind-training practice; mindfulness is one category within it. The goal is not to silence thoughts but to redirect attention toward sensory experience.
- **[[Mindful Breathing]]**: Focusing on breath rhythm and gently returning attention when the mind drifts — the foundational mindfulness technique.
- **[[Mindful Walking]]**: Moving slowly in a low-stimulation environment while attending to physical sensations underfoot.
- **[[Mindful Eating]]**: Eating slowly without screens, noticing appearance, aroma, texture, and taste one bite at a time.
- **[[Emotional Resilience]]**: Siegel describes the outcome of sustained mindfulness practice as becoming "comfortable riding the roller coaster" — tolerating uncertainty rather than avoiding it.

## Key Takeaways

- **Definition**: Mindfulness = paying attention to present experience with a loving, accepting attitude.
- **Goal**: Redirect attention toward sensory input, not suppress thought.
- **Stress pathway**: Everyday stressors narrow focus and impair decision-making; mindfulness counters this.
- **Three techniques**: Mindful breathing, mindful walking, mindful eating — all require no equipment.
- **Beginner expectation**: A busy, "unruly" mind is normal; patience and social support (group classes, a practice partner) accelerate progress.
- **Social support**: Siegel notes it is "enormously helpful" for building a consistent mindfulness habit.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Attention as a Muscle]]**: Siegel's framing that mindfulness works by gently returning attention each time it wanders treats the act of redirection — not the absence of distraction — as the core training rep, directly paralleling strength training logic.
