## Summary

HHS, through CDC and NIH, awarded nearly $3 million to two Centers of Excellence in Vector-Borne Diseases to pilot wildlife-targeted tick control: acaricide-treated feed for deer and dual-action "super baits" for rodents. The goal is to break the tick life cycle at its wildlife-host source and reduce the roughly 500,000 annual U.S. tickborne disease diagnoses, including [[Lyme Disease]] and [[Alpha-gal Syndrome]].

## Core Concepts

- [[Vector-Borne Disease]] — illness transmitted by ticks, mosquitoes, or other vectors; CDC's Centers of Excellence (COEs), established 2017, coordinate applied research on prevention and control.
- [[Lyme Disease]] — the most prominent tickborne illness this initiative targets, carried by [[Blacklegged Tick]] (*Ixodes scapularis*) nymphs that acquire the pathogen from infected rodents.
- [[Alpha-gal Syndrome]] — a tick-bite-induced red meat allergy also named as a target condition alongside Lyme disease.
- [[Acaricide]] — a chemical that kills ticks, delivered here via treated deer feed and rodent bait boxes rather than direct-to-human application.
- [[Robert F. Kennedy Jr.]] — HHS Secretary who announced the initiative, framing it as targeting ticks "where they feed and reproduce."
- **Lone star tick**: Alongside the blacklegged tick, deer also sustain adult lone star tick populations, making deer-targeted acaricide feed a two-species control strategy.

## Key Takeaways

- **Funding**: Nearly $3 million awarded to New England and Midwest Centers of Excellence in Vector-Borne Diseases.
- **Deer strategy**: Acaricide-treated feed stops adult blacklegged and lone star ticks from completing blood meals needed to lay eggs.
- **Rodent strategy**: "Super baits" combine acaricide and antibiotics to kill ticks and cut pathogen carriage via next-generation bait delivery systems.
- **Scale of problem**: ~31 million Americans may be bitten by ticks yearly; 500,000+ diagnosed with tickborne disease.
- **Approach**: Targets wildlife hosts (deer, rodents) rather than only individual human prevention.
- **CDC framing**: Lyle Petersen, MD, MPH, director of CDC's Division of Vector-Borne Diseases, said the effort "is designed to move tick control forward by evaluating large-scale deployment of wildlife-targeted interventions."

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core aim of this HHS/CDC/NIH funding initiative?
**A**: To reduce tick populations at the source by disrupting their life cycle through wildlife-host interventions, thereby lowering human tick bites and tickborne disease risk, including Lyme disease and alpha-gal syndrome.

**Q2**: How do the two funded strategies mechanically reduce tick populations?
**A**: Acaricide-treated deer feed kills or sickens adult ticks before they can complete the blood meal needed to lay eggs, while rodent "super baits" combine acaricide and antibiotics to kill ticks feeding on rodents and reduce the pathogens rodents carry.

**Q3**: Why target deer and rodents instead of just treating people?
**A**: Deer sustain adult tick populations and rodents are key hosts for juvenile ticks and reservoirs for the Lyme pathogen, so disrupting ticks at these wildlife-host stages can reduce tick numbers community-wide rather than relying solely on individual-level prevention.
