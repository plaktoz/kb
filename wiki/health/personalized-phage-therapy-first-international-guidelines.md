## Summary

An international team led by the German Society for Infectious Diseases has published the first internationally coordinated clinical guidelines for personalized [[Phage Therapy]], with more than 60 recommendations published in Nature Medicine. The guidelines treat phages as an option for infections where established treatments have failed, not a routine therapy, because controlled trials have not yet proven how reliably they work. Early case results and a German hospital program show promise.

## Core Concepts

- [[Bacteriophages]]: viruses that infect and destroy bacteria, typically targeting particular species or even individual strains.
- [[Antibiotic Resistance]]: the growing failure of common drugs, which motivates phages as an alternative.
- [[Phage Therapy]]: personalized treatment using carefully selected phages, considered when standard options fail, are unavailable, or are unsafe.
- **Phage match**: laboratory testing of whether a candidate phage kills the patient's specific bacterial strain, done before treatment.
- [[German Society for Infectious Diseases]] (DGI): led the consensus with 20 professional organizations, 18 experts, and two patient advocacy groups.
- [[Annika Claßen]] and [[Silvia Würstle]]: guideline leads; Würstle oversees phage therapy at [[Frankfurt University Medical Center]].
- [[PhageNET DZIF]]: network for exchanging scientific and clinical phage experience.
- [[Case Registries]]: systematic documentation of every treatment so cases can be compared.

## Key Takeaways

- **First Framework**: First international guidelines for personalized phage therapy, 60+ recommendations.
- **Not Routine**: Guidelines advise against routine use; evaluate each patient individually.
- **Strain Specificity**: A phage killing one strain may not affect another.
- **Phage Match**: Test candidate phages quickly, since bacterial susceptibility can change during infection.
- **Team Decisions**: Physicians, microbiologists, and phage experts jointly decide on treatment.
- **Belgian Series**: In 100 patients, 77% improved clinically; bacteria undetectable in 61%.
- **Evidence Limit**: Observational data cannot show phages caused the improvements.
- **Frankfurt Program**: Ten patients treated successfully since February 2026.
- **Delivery Routes**: Intravenous, inhaled, nasal spray, or sterile hydrogel.
- **Validity**: Guidelines valid January 2026 to June 2030, reviewed annually.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Observational outcomes cannot establish causation** — Improvement in patients after a treatment does not show the treatment caused it; only controlled trials can establish efficacy. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Belgian Series, Evidence Limit, Not Routine

**T2. Highly selective treatments must be matched to their target** — When a treatment works only on specific targets, success depends on testing fit for the individual case before and during use.
→ explains: Strain Specificity, Phage Match, Team Decisions

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central development reported in this article?
**A**: An international team published the first coordinated clinical guidelines for personalized phage therapy, with over 60 recommendations on selecting, testing, manufacturing, administering, and monitoring phages. They do not recommend it as routine treatment.

**Q2**: What evidence supports phage therapy, and what limits it?
**A**: In a Belgian series of 100 patients, 77% improved and the targeted bacteria were undetectable in 61%. Because the data are observational, they cannot show phages caused the improvement.

**Q3**: Why must a "phage match" be done before treatment?
**A**: Phages typically target specific species or strains, so a phage effective against one strain may not work on another. Labs must test it against the patient's own bacteria quickly, since susceptibility can change during infection.
