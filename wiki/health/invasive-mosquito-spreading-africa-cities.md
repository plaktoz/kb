## Summary

A genomic analysis published in Science found that Anopheles stephensi — an invasive, urban-dwelling, malaria-carrying mosquito resistant to all currently used insecticides — has spread across Africa faster than pessimistic scientists had feared since first arriving in Djibouti over a decade ago, and many public health experts now believe it is too late to contain.

## Core Concepts

- [[Anopheles Stephensi]] — an invasive mosquito species originating from South Asia/the Persian Gulf, notable for thriving in urban (not just rural) environments and surviving dry seasons
- [[Insecticide Resistance]] — genomic analysis of specimens from 10 countries found African populations genetically resistant to all chemicals currently used in spraying campaigns
- [[Urban Malaria Risk]] — unlike historically dominant malaria-carrying mosquitoes in Africa, this species thrives in megacities, raising risk for urban populations like Lagos and Kinshasa (~20 million people each) that haven't had to plan around malaria

## Key Takeaways

- **Spread pattern**: arrived in Djibouti (Horn of Africa) over a decade ago, has since pushed west to Ghana and south through Kenya across flat, open, windy terrain.
- **Resistance is total**: genetic resistance to all chemicals currently used in African spraying and mosquito-control campaigns.
- **Containment window has closed**: researchers say there's little remaining talk of containing the invasion — "the genie is completely out of the bottle."
- **Urban vulnerability is new**: because this species isn't deterred by dry seasons and adapts to urban settings, cities that have never had to plan for malaria now face real exposure.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What makes Anopheles stephensi different from the malaria mosquitoes historically found in Africa?
**A**: It thrives in urban as well as rural environments and isn't deterred by dry seasons, unlike the historically dominant species — putting large cities at risk for the first time.

**Q2**: What did the genomic analysis find about insecticide resistance?
**A**: Specimens collected from 10 countries showed the African populations of the mosquito carry genetic resistance to all chemicals currently used in spraying campaigns and other control efforts.

**Q3**: What is the current outlook among public health experts on containing this mosquito?
**A**: Most now believe it's too late to eliminate or even contain it — the species has already moved farther and faster across the continent than pessimistic forecasts anticipated.
