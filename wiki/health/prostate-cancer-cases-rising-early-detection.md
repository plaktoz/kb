## Summary

The American Cancer Society projects 333,830 new prostate cancer cases in the US in 2026, with cases rising roughly 3% annually from 2014–2022 and the sharpest increases in advanced-stage diagnoses. Early detection dramatically improves outcomes — localized prostate cancer carries a nearly 100% five-year survival rate — but screening conversations are happening too late for many high-risk men. ACS guidelines recommend PSA screening as early as age 40 for the highest-risk individuals.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Prostate Cancer]]** — most commonly diagnosed cancer in men; incidence rising 3% per year since 2014
- **[[PSA Test]]** (Prostate-Specific Antigen) — primary blood-based screening tool; may be followed by DRE, MRI, or biopsy
- **[[Early Detection]]** — catching cancer before distant spread yields >99% five-year survival vs. much lower rates for advanced-stage disease
- **[[Cancer Screening Guidelines]]** — [[American Cancer Society]] risk-stratified age thresholds (50, 45, 40) based on individual risk factors
- **[[Health Disparities]]** — Black men face ~70% higher prostate cancer incidence than white men

## Key Takeaways

- **2026 Projections**: ACS projects 333,830 new cases and 36,320 deaths from prostate cancer.
- **Rising Incidence**: Cases rose ~3% annually from 2014–2022; advanced-stage diagnoses rising fastest.
- **Survival Rates**: >99% five-year survival when caught before distant spread; ~100% for localized disease.
- **Racial Disparity**: Black men have nearly 70% higher incidence than white men.
- **Screening Age**: Average-risk at 50; high-risk at 45; highest-risk (multiple affected relatives) at 40.
- **Screening Cascade**: Starts with PSA blood test; abnormal results prompt DRE, MRI, or biopsy.
- **Treatment Benefit**: Early detection yields more options, higher cure rates, and less aggressive interventions.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Prevention vs. Treatment]]**: The article illustrates the asymmetry of intervention — catching disease early is orders of magnitude more effective and less costly than treating advanced-stage cancer, a first-principles argument for investing in screening infrastructure.
