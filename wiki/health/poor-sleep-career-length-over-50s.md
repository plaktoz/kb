## Summary

A University of Turku study of Finnish workers found that severe sleep disturbance after age 50 is associated with up to nine fewer months of working life expectancy through age 68, with long sleep duration (9+ hours) also linked to a shorter career than the 7-9 hour "optimal" range — while short sleep alone showed no measurable difference.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Working Life Expectancy]]**: A measure of remaining years in the workforce from a given age, used here to quantify sleep's economic impact rather than just health outcomes.
- **[[Sleep Disturbance Gradient]]**: Moderate disturbance cost ~5 fewer months of working life, severe disturbance ~8 fewer months, relative to no disturbance — a dose-response relationship independent of gender or job type.
- **[[U-Shaped Sleep Duration Risk]]**: Both short and long sleep carry risks, but this study found only long sleep duration (9+ hours) was associated with reduced working life expectancy, not short sleep (under 7 hours) — contradicting some prior research linking short sleep to earlier retirement.

## Key Takeaways

- **Nine-month cost**: Severely disrupted sleep after 50 was linked to a working-life loss of up to nine months compared to undisturbed sleep.
- **Gender gap**: Professional women with no sleep disturbances had the longest working life expectancy (~14 years from 50-68); men in routine jobs with severe disturbances had the shortest (~12.5 years).
- **Surprising null result**: No measurable difference between short (under 7 hrs) and mid-range (7-8.5 hrs) sleep duration on working life expectancy — only long sleep (9+ hrs) showed a negative association.
- **Socioeconomic angle**: Researchers emphasize sleep-health interventions (CBT for insomnia, workplace sleep education) may be especially valuable for lower socioeconomic status workers extending their working lives.
- **Data source**: Finnish Public Sector study and Health and Social Support study, surveying sleep habits after age 50.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Severely disrupted or excessively long sleep after age 50 is associated with a meaningfully shorter working life — up to nine fewer months compared to workers with undisturbed sleep — independent of gender or job type.

**Q2**: What specific finding challenges the common assumption that short sleep is the primary risk?
**A**: The study found no measurable difference in working life expectancy between short (under 7 hours) and mid-range (7-8.5 hours, considered optimal) sleep duration — only long sleep duration (9+ hours) was associated with reduced working life expectancy.

**Q3**: What's the practical policy implication highlighted by the researchers?
**A**: Sleep-health interventions like cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia or workplace sleep education, delivered through occupational health services, could meaningfully extend working lives — particularly for lower socioeconomic status workers who show the shortest working-life expectancy when sleep is severely disturbed.
