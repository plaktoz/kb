## Summary
A Stanford Medicine-led study of nearly 21,000 women found that the link between saturated fat intake and LDL cholesterol is amplified in those with a genetic predisposition to high LDL and diminished in those without. Cardiovascular event risk from saturated fat was likewise elevated mainly in the top 25% by genetic risk. The authors see this as a step toward "precision prevention" but say there is not yet evidence to guide diet advice by polygenic scores.

## Core Concepts
- [[Polygenic Risk Score]]: tool predicting genetic risk of high cholesterol, developed in a 2021 Nature study with over 1.6 million individuals.
- [[Gene-Environment Interaction]]: genetic predisposition changes how strongly diet affects LDL.
- [[LDL Cholesterol]] and [[Saturated Fat]]: the known association was reproduced, then shown to vary by genetics.
- [[Cardiovascular Disease]]: heart attacks and strokes, the No. 1 cause of death in the US and worldwide.
- [[Ketogenic Diet]]: high-fat diets may suit some people, yet no cardiovascular benefit from more saturated fat was found.
- [[Precision Prevention]]: tailoring diet and exercise recommendations to a patient's genetics.
- [[Women's Health Initiative]]: the cohort (median age 65), with 122 foods and beverages reported.
- [[Shoa Clarke]]: senior author, Stanford Medicine preventive cardiologist.

## Key Takeaways
- **Sample**: Nearly 21,000 sequenced women from the Women's Health Initiative.
- **Amplified Effect**: High genetic LDL propensity exaggerated saturated fat's effect on LDL.
- **Diminished Effect**: Women without the predisposition showed weaker effects.
- **Event Risk**: Only the top 25% by genetic risk saw significantly higher cardiovascular events.
- **Predisposition Is Not Destiny**: Genetically prone people are most sensitive to diet, for better or worse.
- **No Protective Effect**: Even low-risk participants gained no benefit from more saturated fat.
- **Early Follow-Up**: Preliminary data show high polygenic scores raise cholesterol risk after low-carb diets.
- **Not Yet Guidance**: No evidence yet supports using polygenic scores for diet recommendations.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about saturated fat and cholesterol?
**A**: The association between saturated fat intake and LDL cholesterol differs by genetics: it is exaggerated in women genetically prone to high LDL and diminished in those without the predisposition.

**Q2**: What data and method supported the finding?
**A**: Researchers applied a polygenic risk score to nearly 21,000 Women's Health Initiative participants who had sequenced genes, reported their diets, and had fasting cholesterol measured. The risk of cardiovascular events was amplified for the top 25% by genetic risk.

**Q3**: How might this change future prevention, and what is the caveat?
**A**: It points toward "precision prevention," with diet advice tailored to genetics. Currently there is no evidence that polygenic scores should guide diet recommendations.
