## Summary
About 4.3% of pregnant women and more than 9% of new mothers experience [[Eating Disorders]], yet screening, clinician training and treatment access are inadequate. [[ACOG]] guidance does not cover eating disorders, existing tools like the [[SCOFF Questionnaire]] may not fit pregnancy, and insurance and Medicaid coverage are thin. Research on prevention is limited, though early results for peer-support programs are promising.

## Core Concepts
- [[Perinatal Eating Disorders]]: Anorexia nervosa, bulimia nervosa and binge eating disorder during pregnancy and postpartum.
- [[ACOG]]: Recommends general eating disorder assessment but offers no screening or management guidance.
- [[SCOFF Questionnaire]]: Most common screening tool, with unvalidated fit for pregnancy.
- [[SWAG Stereotype]]: "Skinny, white, affluent girl" image that causes clinicians to miss women of color and trans people.
- [[Medicaid Coverage]]: Only 22 states let dietitians bill independently; about 25% of 384 treatment centers accept Medicaid.
- [[Postpartum Depression]]: More likely in women with eating disorder histories.
- [[The Body Project]]: Model for peer support and cognitive dissonance trials in Massachusetts.

## Key Takeaways
- **Prevalence**: About 4.3% of pregnant women and over 9% of new mothers.
- **Risks**: Miscarriage, anemia, low birthweight and postpartum depression, though causation is unproven.
- **Screening Gap**: Few validated tools exist and obstetricians get little training.
- **Policy Gap**: Massachusetts excluded eating disorders because ACOG guidance omits them.
- **Equity Gap**: Black women have similar rates but are less likely to be diagnosed.
- **Access Gap**: Five states have no intensive treatment centers.
- **Patient Actions**: Blind weigh-ins and withholding non-problematic blood sugar results.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Conditions go undetected when no one is looking for them** — Detection depends on clinicians having a prompt, a validated tool and training; without these, symptoms are overlooked or attributed to pregnancy itself. · *model:* [[Streetlight Effect]]
→ explains: Screening Gap, Policy Gap

**T2. Screening only pays off when treatment capacity exists** — Identifying patients adds little value if referrals, insurance coverage and specialists are unavailable.
→ explains: Access Gap, Screening Gap

**T3. Stereotyped prototypes of who is affected cause misdiagnosis** — Clinicians who match patients to a narrow image of the condition miss those who don't fit it. · *model:* [[Representativeness Heuristic]]
→ explains: Equity Gap

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why do doctors keep missing eating disorders in pregnancy?
**A**: There is no widely used validated screening tool, obstetricians receive little training, and ACOG's perinatal mental health guidance does not cover eating disorders. As one specialist put it, providers don't see it because they aren't looking for it.

**Q2**: What data show the access problem in treatment?
**A**: Only 22 state Medicaid programs let dietitians bill independently, and only about 25% of 384 intensive treatment centers accept Medicaid. Five states have none.

**Q3**: What can patients and the system do now?
**A**: Patients can ask for blind weigh-ins and to skip routine blood sugar readings unless there is a problem. The system needs clinical standards, screening and treatment capacity, and families should know the risks.
