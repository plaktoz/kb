## Summary

AMA's "What Doctors Want Patients to Know" series covers mosquito bites with dermatologist Donald Lei, MD: bites are an inflammatory immune response to proteins in mosquito saliva, reactions range from a small bump to rare severe allergy (SMBA), and 2026's West Nile virus season started earlier than any year since 2004 per the CDC.

## Core Concepts

Mosquito bite reactions sit on a spectrum of **[[Immune Response]]** severity — from a routine histamine-driven itch/wheal, to **[[Skeeter Syndrome]]** (large local swelling and warmth, sometimes fever), to rare **Severe Mosquito Bite Allergy (SMBA)** with systemic symptoms — making symptom escalation, not the bite itself, the key clinical signal to watch.

## Key Takeaways

- **Only females bite**: Female mosquitoes need blood protein for egg development; males feed on nectar.
- **Standing water breeding**: Even flowerpot-sized water pools sustain mosquito populations in dense cities.
- **Reaction spectrum**: Small bump → Skeeter syndrome (large swelling/warmth, possible fever) → rare SMBA (high fever, black crusting, blistering).
- **Repellent guidance**: DEET, picaridin, or permethrin are most effective; infants under 2 months need physical barriers only.
- **Early 2026 West Nile season**: Highest case count for this point in the year since 2004, per the CDC.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Mosquito bites trigger an immune response ranging from mild and self-resolving to rare severe reactions, and knowing the escalation signs matters more than the bite itself.

**Q2**: What specific symptoms should prompt immediate medical evaluation?
**A**: Spreading redness/warmth after 24-48 hours, pus, duskiness or black crusting, blisters, widespread rash, swollen lymph nodes, fever, confusion, or — most urgently — hives away from the bite site, difficulty breathing/swallowing, or facial/lip/tongue swelling.

**Q3**: What repellent and prevention guidance does the article give?
**A**: Products containing DEET, picaridin, or permethrin are most effective (DEET/picaridin only for those over 2 months old), avoiding standing water, and using physical barriers like long clothing and netting, especially for infants.
