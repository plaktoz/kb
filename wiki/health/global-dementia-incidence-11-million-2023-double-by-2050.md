## Summary

A [[JAMA Neurology]] study led by [[UNSW Sydney]]'s Center for Healthy Brain Aging ([[CHeBA]]) through the [[COSMIC Consortium]] estimates that 11.4 million people developed dementia in 2023, a figure projected to reach 24.7 million new cases a year by 2050. Pooling 38,418 people from 22 population-based studies in 18 countries and territories, it found incidence was higher among women and substantially higher in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). The researchers say this incidence benchmark should guide prevention, diagnosis and care planning, though the study does not explain why the differences exist.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Dementia]] incidence vs. prevalence**: Incidence counts new cases per year; prevalence counts everyone living with the condition at a given time. The study focuses on incidence.
- **[[COSMIC Consortium]]**: Cohort Studies of Memory in an International Consortium, a CHeBA-led network that combines longitudinal ageing and memory studies worldwide.
- **Harmonized methodology**: The same statistical methods and clinically based diagnoses were applied across cohorts, so countries can be compared directly for the first time.
- **[[Low- and Middle-Income Countries]]**: These regions show the highest incidence, are ageing rapidly, and often have the fewest resources for care.
- **[[Population Aging]]**: The main driver of the projected rise; it shifts the response from reacting to cases toward prevention.
- **Key people**: Lead author [[Ashleigh Vella]], co-author [[Henry Brodaty]], senior author [[Perminder Sachdev]].

## Key Takeaways

- **2023 new cases**: An estimated 11.4 million people developed dementia worldwide.
- **2050 projection**: Projected to reach 24.7 million new cases per year.
- **Study scope**: 38,418 people, 22 studies, 18 countries, six continents.
- **Follow-up**: 3,001 dementia cases across more than 287,000 person-years.
- **Global rate**: 9.7 new cases per 1,000 person-years, age- and sex-standardized.
- **Sex gap**: 10.6 cases per 1,000 person-years in women vs. 8.6 in men.
- **Income gap**: 13.6 in LMICs vs. 8.3 in high-income countries.
- **Prevalence context**: Over 57 million people live with dementia; about 150 million projected by 2050.
- **Planning value**: Incidence helps forecast demand for diagnosis, care and support services.
- **Causes unknown**: The study does not establish why women and LMICs have higher incidence.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. New-case rates drive future demand** — Prevalence is a stock and incidence is the inflow that changes it, so incidence is what lets health systems anticipate future demand for diagnosis and care and decide where prevention will matter most. · *model:* [[Stocks and Flows]]

**T2. Group differences do not reveal their causes** — Finding higher incidence in a population does not show what causes it. The gaps may come from a mix of health, social, economic, educational and environmental factors that need separate study. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's headline estimate of global dementia incidence?
**A**: About 11.4 million people developed dementia in 2023, and this is projected to rise to 24.7 million new cases a year by 2050.

**Q2**: How did incidence differ by sex and country income level?
**A**: Standardized incidence was 10.6 per 1,000 person-years in women vs. 8.6 in men, and 13.6 in LMICs vs. 8.3 in high-income countries.

**Q3**: Why does an incidence benchmark matter for policymakers?
**A**: It helps forecast future demand for diagnosis, care and support and shows where prevention could have the greatest impact, which is especially urgent in fast-ageing LMICs with limited resources.
