## Summary

The National Sleep Foundation reviewed more than 3,200 sleep studies published since its 2015 sleep-duration guidelines and found "a decade of new science continues to support the original recommendations" — the nine age-group sleep ranges remain unchanged and evidence-backed.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Guideline Durability]]**: A rare case where a decade of accumulating research reaffirmed rather than revised existing public-health recommendations, underscoring the guidelines' original evidentiary strength.
- **[[Age-Stratified Sleep Need]]**: Recommended sleep duration decreases steadily across the lifespan, from 14-17 hours for newborns to 7-8 hours for older adults (65+).

## Key Takeaways

- **Review scope**: 3,200+ studies published since the NSF's 2015 recommendations were examined.
- **Recommended ranges**: Newborns (0-3mo) 14-17 hrs; infants (4-11mo) 12-15 hrs; toddlers (1-2yr) 11-14 hrs; preschoolers (3-5) 10-13 hrs; school-age (6-13) 9-11 hrs; teens (14-17) 8-10 hrs; young adults (18-25) 7-9 hrs; adults (26-64) 7-9 hrs; older adults (65+) 7-8 hrs.
- **Conclusion**: "This review shows the core message remains unchanged: Getting the right amount of sleep is essential for health" — Joseph Dzierzewski, NSF senior VP of research.
- **Publication**: Study published in the journal Sleep Health.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: A decade-long review of sleep research confirms that the National Sleep Foundation's 2015 age-based sleep-duration recommendations remain accurate and don't need revision.

**Q2**: How many studies did the review examine, and what age-based standard did it reaffirm?
**A**: More than 3,200 studies were reviewed, reaffirming nine age-group sleep ranges from 14-17 hours for newborns down to 7-8 hours for adults 65 and older.

**Q3**: Why is this finding practically useful for someone trying to evaluate sleep advice?
**A**: It confirms these specific age-based ranges are a stable, well-evidenced reference point rather than guidance likely to change soon — useful as a durable baseline when evaluating other, more novel sleep claims.
