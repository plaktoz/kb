## Summary

A 2026 study in *Human Reproduction* found that body fat percentage is a stronger predictor of male reproductive health than BMI, with high body fat linked to 23% lower sperm count and reduced testosterone. Even men with normal BMI but elevated body fat showed reduced sperm counts and poorer morphology. Excess fat tissue converts testosterone to estrogen, directly impairing sperm production.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Body Fat Percentage]]** as a more precise fertility risk marker than [[BMI]]
- **[[Male Infertility]]**: affects ~15% of U.S. couples; men solely responsible in ~20% of cases
- **[[Testosterone]]** and [[SHBG]] disruption from adipose tissue aromatization
- **[[Sperm Morphology]]** and count as reproductive health indicators
- Researcher [[Nis Brix]] and the *Human Reproduction* journal findings
- **[[Bioimpedance Analysis]]** used to measure body fat in 1,058 Danish men

## Key Takeaways

- Men with 20–24% body fat had on average **23% lower sperm count** than normal-fat peers.
- Men with 25%+ body fat showed **15% lower sperm count**.
- Body fat triggers testosterone-to-estrogen conversion, impairing sperm production.
- Normal BMI does not rule out fertility risk if body fat is elevated.
- Low sperm count may signal broader systemic health issues beyond fertility.
- Lifestyle interventions: exercise, diet, antioxidants, sleep, reduced alcohol/smoking.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Surrogate Metric Failure]]**: BMI measures weight-to-height ratio but not fat distribution — using it as a fertility proxy misses men with normal weight but high fat mass, a classic case of relying on a convenient metric that poorly tracks the underlying variable.
- **[[Circle of Competence]]**: Urologist commentary highlights that fertility screening requires metabolic expertise beyond standard weight assessment; recognizing the limits of BMI's diagnostic scope leads to better risk identification.
