## Summary
Indiana's Mental Health Roundtable Summit (Aug 7, 2026, Indianapolis) brings together mental health professionals, lawmakers, veterans, and first responders to address systemic mental health gaps for emergency and military personnel. The summit is part of a growing national movement recognizing occupational trauma in these populations as distinct from general-population mental health needs.

## Core Concepts

- [[Occupational Trauma]] — cumulative psychological toll of routine exposure to life-threatening emergencies; distinct from single-incident PTSD
- [[First Responder Mental Health]] — police, firefighters, and EMTs face chronic stress exposure that conventional mental health services are not designed for
- [[Military Veterans Mental Health]] — veterans face unique challenges linking service trauma to civilian reintegration
- [[Sagamore Institute]] — Indiana policy organization hosting the 2026 Mental Health Roundtable Summit
- [[Mental Health Roundtable Summit 2026]] — Aug 7, JW Marriott Indianapolis; focus on first responders and military

## Key Takeaways

- **Routine emergency calls are traumatic** — the cumulative nature of occupational trauma is different from a single traumatic event; it's less visible and harder to treat
- **Systemic gap**: mental health infrastructure is designed for the general population, not for professionals trained to suppress distress as a job requirement
- **Summit objectives**: educate professionals, equip participants with resources, promote healing for frontline workers
- **Legislative context**: 2026 AI therapy chatbot bans specifically excluded licensed professionals from being replaced by AI — recognizing that complex trauma requires human-to-human therapeutic relationships

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Identity-Performance Gap]]**: First responders and military personnel are trained to perform under stress — this same training suppresses the signals that would normally prompt them to seek help. Mental health systems designed around self-reporting fail these populations structurally, not individually.
