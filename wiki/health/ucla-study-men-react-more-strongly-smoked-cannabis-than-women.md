## Summary
A UCLA Health randomized trial found that men who use cannabis occasionally reacted more strongly to smoked cannabis than women with similar use habits, despite similar blood THC levels. This contradicts earlier research suggesting women are more sensitive. The researchers say the sample was modest and several findings were exploratory.

## Core Concepts
- [[Sex Differences in Cannabis Effects]]: earlier beliefs came largely from animal research and pooled human trials.
- [[THC]]: blood levels were about equal between men and women, yet men reacted more strongly.
- [[Frequency of Use]]: the study split participants evenly by weekly or daily use to test sex and frequency together.
- [[Randomized Controlled Trial]]: each participant smoked placebo, lower-THC and higher-THC cannabis, blinded, in three sessions.
- [[Hormonal Control]]: women were not on hormonal birth control and were tested in the same menstrual cycle phase.
- [[Cannabis Use Disorder]]: women tend to progress faster from first use to disorder.
- [[Ziva Cooper]] and [[Stephanie Lake]]: principal investigator and lead author at the UCLA Center for Cannabis and Cannabinoids.

## Key Takeaways
- **Core Finding**: Occasional-use men reacted more strongly to smoked cannabis at similar blood THC.
- **Design**: First experiment testing sex and use frequency together.
- **Controls**: Women excluded for hormonal birth control and tested in one cycle phase.
- **Tolerance Hypothesis**: Women may build tolerance with less use than men; untested.
- **Risk Context**: Women move faster from first use to cannabis use disorder.
- **Limits**: Modest sample, exploratory findings, THC strengths below many current products.
- **Next Steps**: Team is studying high-potency products and adults 55 and older.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Uncontrolled variables can reverse an apparent effect** — When two factors that influence an outcome are pooled or left uncontrolled, the apparent effect of one can mislead; isolating each changes the picture. · *model:* [[Confounding Variable]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the UCLA study find about sex and cannabis sensitivity?
**A**: Men who use cannabis occasionally reacted more strongly to smoked cannabis than women with similar use habits, though THC blood levels were about the same. This runs counter to earlier research suggesting women are more sensitive.

**Q2**: Why does the researchers' design change the picture compared with earlier work?
**A**: Earlier beliefs came from animal research and pooled human trials. This study looked at sex and use frequency together, blinded participants across placebo, lower-THC and higher-THC sessions, and controlled for hormones.

**Q3**: What are the implications and caveats of the findings?
**A**: Sex and use history both shape the experience of cannabis, which matters for identifying who is at risk of unpleasant or higher-risk experiences. The sample was modest, several findings exploratory, and the THC strengths were lower than many products sold today.
