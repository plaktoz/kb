## Summary

An [[ISPOR]] Special Interest Group systematic review in [[Value in Health]] of 12 economic studies (2014-2024) found that [[Oral Nutrition Supplements]] in older adults, often at risk of [[Malnutrition]], gave modest short-term health gains and were generally cost-effective or cost-saving, mainly through fewer hospitalizations. Long-term value is uncertain because most studies ran 6 months or less, bundled supplements with other interventions, and were industry-funded.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Oral Nutrition Supplements]]**: Drinks, powders and other formats used under medical supervision when someone can eat but cannot meet needs through diet alone.
- **[[Health Economic Evaluation]]**: Review combined several evaluation types across care settings, in populations with mean ages 63 to 88.
- **Cost offsets**: Seven studies reported per-patient savings of $127 to $1,988 (2024 USD); five reported cost increases of $356 to $672.
- **Utilization-driven value**: Favorable economics were largely driven by reduced healthcare use such as hospital admissions.
- **Targeting**: Greatest value appeared when targeted to higher-risk patients and implemented across care transitions.
- **Evidence limits**: Eight studies bundled supplements with other components such as dietary counseling; most were industry-funded and from high-income countries.

## Key Takeaways

- **Scope**: 12 economic studies, 11 of 12 in high-income countries.
- **Outcomes**: 9 studies favorable on main outcomes; 3 mixed.
- **Savings**: Seven studies found per-patient savings of $127 to $1,988.
- **Costs**: Five studies found added per-patient costs of $356 to $672.
- **Mechanism**: Modest clinical gains translate into fewer hospital admissions.
- **Targeting**: Higher-risk patients and care transitions offer the greatest value.
- **Caveat**: Most studies covered 6 months or less; long-term value uncertain.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Net cost depends on downstream offsets** — An intervention's economic value is its direct cost minus the costs it avoids elsewhere, so it pays off only where the avoided costs exceed what it costs to deliver.
→ explains: Savings, Costs, Mechanism, Targeting

**T2. Bundled interventions cannot be attributed to a single component** — When several elements are delivered together, outcomes cannot be assigned to one of them, and funding source and short follow-up further weaken confidence. · *model:* [[Confounding Variable]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the ISPOR review conclude about oral nutrition supplements in older adults?
**A**: They were generally associated with modest short-term health improvements and were cost-effective or cost-saving. Long-term value remains uncertain.

**Q2**: Where did the economic benefit come from, and how large was it?
**A**: Benefits were driven mainly by reduced healthcare use such as fewer hospitalizations. Seven studies reported per-patient savings of $127 to $1,988, while five reported cost increases of $356 to $672.

**Q3**: How should decision makers apply these findings?
**A**: Consider supplements in a targeted way for higher-risk patients and across care transitions. Longer-term, transparently reported studies are needed first for firm conclusions.
