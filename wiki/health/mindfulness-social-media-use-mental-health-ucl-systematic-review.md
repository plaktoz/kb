## Summary

A UCL-led systematic review in [[PLOS One]] of 43 quantitative studies (28,000+ participants) found that higher [[Mindfulness]] is associated with healthier [[Social Media]] use and lower depression, anxiety, stress, loneliness and [[Fear of Missing Out]]. Because most studies were cross-sectional, the authors say causation cannot be concluded, but mindfulness as a trainable skill is a promising intervention target. They also stress that platform design and social context matter, not just individual choice.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Mindfulness]]**: Non-judgemental attention to present thoughts, feelings and events; measured mostly by the [[Mindful Attention Awareness Scale]] (24 of 43 studies).
- **Protective/buffering role**: Mindfulness appeared to weaken links between online experiences and poorer mental health, and to lower problematic social media use.
- **Evidence base**: 39 of 43 studies supported a protective role; participants aged 10 to 92, with university students the largest group (46.5%).
- **Intervention studies**: One study combining mindfulness exercises, usage self-monitoring and mood tracking improved stress, anxiety, FoMO and problematic use. An eight-day [[Digital Detox]] improved mindful attention but increased negative emotions.
- **Mechanism per [[Marc Tibber]]**: Noticing thoughts and impulses without acting on them makes it easier to step back from habitual reactions and use platforms intentionally.
- **Platform design**: Healthier use is not only individual responsibility; platforms are designed to attract and retain attention ([[Attention Economy]]).

## Key Takeaways

- **Scale**: 43 studies, 28,000+ participants; 39 supported a protective role.
- **Outcomes**: Higher mindfulness linked to less depression, anxiety, stress, loneliness, FoMO.
- **Causation Gap**: Most studies were cross-sectional; causal claims are not supported yet.
- **Trainable Skill**: Mindfulness is a potential target for clinical, educational and public health interventions.
- **Simple Practice**: Pausing before engaging on social media supports more conscious choices.
- **Bias**: Research skews toward younger, predominantly Western populations.
- **Detox Caveat**: Eight days of restriction raised negative emotions versus unrestricted controls.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Association is not causation** — A correlation observed at one point in time cannot show which variable drives the other, so cross-sectional findings only justify hypotheses and further longitudinal or experimental tests. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Causation Gap, Bias

**T2. Outcomes depend on environment as well as individual skill** — Individual capacity (here, mindfulness) interacts with how the surrounding system is designed, so responsibility cannot rest on the individual alone.
→ explains: Trainable Skill, Simple Practice

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the review's central finding about mindfulness and social media?
**A**: People with higher mindfulness reported healthier social media use and lower depression, anxiety, stress, loneliness and FoMO. 39 of 43 studies supported a protective role.

**Q2**: Why can't the review conclude that mindfulness causes better mental health?
**A**: Most of the studies were cross-sectional, so they show association only. All studies also relied on self-reported mindfulness, and the samples skewed young and Western.

**Q3**: How might this finding be applied?
**A**: Because mindfulness is trainable, it is a possible target for interventions, and even a simple pause before engaging on social media can encourage more conscious choices. Platform design and social context still matter.
