## Summary
UC San Diego researchers report that adding ketamine-assisted psychotherapy to a mindfulness-based treatment for opioid use disorder may strengthen body awareness, which may help reduce opioid cravings. The follow-up analysis explains why the combined treatment in a March 2026 trial reduced drug use. The authors call the findings preliminary and rely on self-reported measures.

## Core Concepts
- [[Mindfulness-Oriented Recovery Enhancement]] (MORE): eight-week mind-body therapy developed by [[Eric Garland]] combining mindfulness, reframing of stress, and savoring of positive experiences.
- [[Ketamine-Assisted Psychotherapy]]: two psychedelic-dose ketamine sessions added to MORE in a 68-person randomized trial.
- [[Interoception]]: ability to sense, interpret, and respond to internal body signals; may be disrupted in [[Opioid Use Disorder]].
- [[Buprenorphine]]: medication all trial participants were taking.
- [[Craving Regulation]]: treating bodily craving sensations as information rather than triggers.

## Key Takeaways
- **Trial Result**: MORE plus ketamine produced 44% fewer instances of drug use than MORE alone.
- **Body Awareness**: Gains emerged around the first ketamine session and persisted at one-month follow-up.
- **Skills Over Noticing**: Listening to the body and regulating distress predicted lower cravings three months later.
- **Measurement**: Smartphone prompts three times daily tracked momentary body awareness during and after treatment.
- **Baseline Evidence**: MORE alone previously reduced opioid misuse 45% and relapse 42% on medication.
- **Limitations**: Self-reported measures and unblinded participants; larger objective studies are needed.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What does the study suggest about how ketamine enhances mindfulness therapy for opioid use disorder?
**A**: It may strengthen interoception, helping people notice and skillfully respond to bodily signals rather than reacting automatically to cravings. That improved body awareness is linked to lower opioid cravings.

**Q2**: What evidence connected body awareness to reduced cravings?
**A**: Body awareness rose faster in the MORE-plus-ketamine group, starting around the first ketamine session. Greater gains in listening to the body and using sensations to regulate distress were associated with lower cravings three months later.

**Q3**: What caveats limit how this finding should be applied?
**A**: Body awareness was self-reported and participants knew they received ketamine, so the findings are preliminary. Larger studies with objective measures are needed before drawing firm conclusions.
