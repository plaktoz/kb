## Summary

The FDA approved Freenome's SimpleScreen CRC blood test in July 2026, making it the second blood-based colorectal cancer screening test approved after the Shield test in 2024. In the PREEMPT CRC trial (48,000+ participants), the test identified approximately 8 in 10 colorectal cancers with negative results for roughly 9 in 10 people without cancer. Blood-based tests primarily serve patients unwilling to undergo preferred screening methods such as colonoscopy, as they are less effective but dramatically lower participation barriers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[SimpleScreen CRC]]**: Freenome's FDA-approved blood test for [[Colorectal Cancer]] screening, detecting [[CpG Methylation]] patterns that emerge early in cancer development.
- **[[CpG Methylation]]**: DNA changes detectable in the bloodstream that signal early-stage cancer development; the biomarker basis for SimpleScreen CRC.
- **[[Colorectal Cancer]] Screening Gap**: ~60 million eligible Americans (45+) remain unscreened despite guidelines recommending regular screening; colorectal cancer is the second leading cause of cancer-related death worldwide and the top killer under 50.
- **[[Liquid Biopsy]]**: Blood-based cancer detection approach that lowers logistical and psychological barriers compared to colonoscopy or stool-based tests.
- **PREEMPT CRC Trial**: Large clinical study with 48,000+ participants that validated SimpleScreen CRC's performance metrics.
- **[[Shield Test]]**: The first FDA-approved blood-based colorectal cancer screening test (2024), now joined by SimpleScreen CRC as a second option.

## Key Takeaways

- **FDA approval**: SimpleScreen CRC approved July 2026; second blood-based CRC screening test after Shield (2024).
- **Sensitivity**: Correctly identifies ~80% of colorectal cancers in clinical testing.
- **Specificity**: Returns negative results for ~90% of people without cancer or advanced precancerous lesions.
- **Mechanism**: Detects CpG methylation — early DNA changes in cancer development.
- **Turnaround**: Results available in approximately two weeks; positive result triggers follow-up colonoscopy.
- **Real-world adherence**: Blood-based screening could reach 95% adherence in real-world settings.
- **Clinical standing**: Recommended only when patients decline colonoscopy or stool-based tests — "better than no screening."
- **Guideline update**: American Cancer Society now includes blood tests as an option for patients declining preferred methods.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Availability Heuristic]] → Barrier Reduction**: The core premise mirrors the insight that behavior change follows friction reduction — when the easiest available action is "do nothing," adding a low-friction alternative (a blood draw) shifts the default outcome from unscreened to screened.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: If health systems optimize for test completion rates rather than cancer-detection accuracy, adoption of lower-sensitivity blood tests could inflate screening metrics while leaving more cancers undetected compared to colonoscopy-first approaches.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core argument for adopting blood-based colorectal cancer screening tests despite their lower accuracy than colonoscopy?
**A**: Blood tests lower participation barriers — embarrassment, preparation burden, fear, and inconvenience — and real-world adherence could reach 95%, making them more effective in practice than a more accurate test most people avoid.

**Q2**: How does SimpleScreen CRC detect colorectal cancer, and what performance metrics did it achieve in the PREEMPT CRC trial?
**A**: The test detects CpG methylation patterns in the blood that emerge early in cancer development; in the 48,000+ participant PREEMPT CRC trial it identified ~80% of colorectal cancers and correctly returned negative results for ~90% of cancer-free individuals.

**Q3**: For whom do professional gastroenterology societies recommend blood-based colorectal cancer screening tests?
**A**: They are recommended primarily for individuals who are unwilling to complete preferred screening methods (colonoscopy or stool-based tests), and are characterized as "better than no screening" rather than a first-line option.
