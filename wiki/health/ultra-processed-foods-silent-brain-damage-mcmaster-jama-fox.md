## Summary

A McMaster/PHRI study in JAMA Network Open scanned the brains of about 9,000 adults aged 35 to 69 and found those with the highest [[Ultra-Processed Foods]] intake were 40% more likely to show small areas of silent brain damage. The link was stronger in women and accompanied by more body and visceral fat and less whole-food intake. Because participants were assessed only once, causation cannot be established, and follow-up research is planned.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Ultra-Processed Foods]]** — Soft drinks, packaged snacks and sugary cereals; low in fiber, vitamins and minerals, high in sodium, saturated fat and added sugar (CDC).
- **[[Silent Brain Damage]]** — Small MRI-detected lesions without obvious symptoms; possible early warning of stroke or memory problems.
- **[[Visceral Fat]]** — Higher in heavy UPF consumers; linked to heart disease.
- **[[Russell de Souza]] and [[Sonia Anand]]** — First and senior authors, Population Health Research Institute.
- **[[Dietary Displacement]]** — Whole foods were 41% of intake for top UPF consumers versus 68% for the lowest.
- **[[Correlation vs Causation]]** — Single-time evaluation prevents a causal conclusion.
- **US consumption** — UPFs made up about 55% of Americans' daily calories (CDC, 2021 to 2023 data).

## Key Takeaways

- **Risk**: Highest UPF intake linked to 40% greater likelihood of brain changes.
- **Sex difference**: Women in the top group had nearly twice the likelihood.
- **Unexpected**: Damage typically seen at older ages appeared in middle-aged adults without health concerns.
- **Intake gap**: UPFs averaged about 25% of daily food for participants.
- **Displacement**: Whole foods were 41% of diet for top UPF consumers, 68% for the lowest.
- **Body fat**: Highest consumers carried more body fat and visceral fat.
- **Causation limit**: One-time evaluation means UPFs cannot be proven the cause.
- **Next step**: Longer-term studies with repeated MRI scans are in the works.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the study find about ultra-processed foods and the brain?
**A**: Participants with the highest UPF intake were 40% more likely to show small areas of silent brain damage on MRI, with nearly twice the likelihood in women.

**Q2**: Which data points show how heavy UPF consumers' diets differed?
**A**: Whole foods made up 41% of daily intake for the highest UPF consumers versus 68% for the lowest, and the highest consumers had more body fat and visceral fat.

**Q3**: Why can't the study tell people that cutting UPFs will protect the brain, and what is needed?
**A**: Participants were evaluated only once, so causation can't be determined. Longer-term studies with repeated MRI scans are needed to test whether reducing UPF intake slows brain changes.
