## Summary
A University of Exeter study using ALSPAC data found that children taken to parks at least weekly from birth to age 11 scored 16.5 percent better on mental health assessments than those who visited less. Children also showed better social-emotional development during periods of more frequent park visits. The researchers caution that benefits depend on good-quality, safe, accessible parks and that findings come from one UK cohort.

## Core Concepts
- [[University of Exeter]] study led by Mark Ferguson, published in the Journal of Epidemiology and Community Health.
- [[ALSPAC]] ([[Children of the 90s]]): a long-running UK cohort; 4,219 participants aged 4 to 11.
- [[Social-Emotional Development]]: understanding feelings, getting along with others, and coping.
- [[Green Space and Child Mental Health]]: parks as places for physical activity and adventurous, imaginative play.
- [[Confounding Variable]] control for socioeconomic status and family adversity.
- [[Health Inequality]]: free or low-cost parks could narrow gaps if they are good quality and accessible.

## Key Takeaways
- **Headline Effect**: Weekly park visitors scored 16.5% better on mental health assessments.
- **Within-Child Pattern**: Children showed better development in periods when they visited parks more.
- **Controls**: The analysis accounted for socioeconomic status and family adversity.
- **Visit Patterns**: Advantaged families visited more when children were young; this shifted as children aged.
- **Equity Potential**: Parks are often free, but quality, safety, and welcome determine the benefit.
- **Limitation**: Data come from one UK study; generalizability to diverse communities is untested.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Exeter study find about park visits and children's mental health?
**A**: Children taken to a park at least once a week from birth to age 11 scored on average 16.5% better on mental health assessments than those who visited less often.

**Q2**: What evidence supports a link between park visits and social-emotional development?
**A**: Using data on 4,219 ALSPAC participants, children showed better social-emotional development during periods of more frequent park visits, and frequent visitors overall fared better. The analysis controlled for socioeconomic status and family adversity.

**Q3**: What does the study imply for communities and policy?
**A**: Encouraging weekly park visits could be a practical way to support child wellbeing and reduce inequalities. This only works if parks are good quality, safe, accessible, and welcoming to all families.
