## Summary

A University of Colorado Anschutz phase 2 trial (N=50, published in the American Journal of Psychiatry) found that oral [[Semaglutide]] significantly reduced heavy drinking days, drinking intensity, craving, and alcohol-related consequences in adults with moderate-to-severe alcohol use disorder, marking a potential first new treatment avenue since 2006 — though the drug missed its primary lab-based craving endpoint and one of two key secondary endpoints. Only three medications are currently FDA-approved for alcohol use disorder, and researchers chose the oral pill form specifically because it may be more acceptable to patients than the injectable formulations tested previously.

## Core Concepts

- [[Semaglutide]] — GLP-1 drug behind Ozempic and Wegovy, tested here for alcohol use disorder
- [[Alcohol Use Disorder]] — chronic, undertreated condition affecting an estimated 27.9 million Americans annually
- [[University of Colorado Anschutz]] — institution running the trial
- [[GLP-1 Receptor Agonist]] — drug class mechanism previously associated with appetite suppression
- [[Primary vs. Secondary Endpoints]] — the trial missed its primary endpoint (cue-elicited craving VAS) and one of two key secondary endpoints (drinks per calendar day), but showed significant benefit on heavy drinking days and several other secondary/exploratory measures — a case study in how endpoint selection shapes an apparent "positive" or "negative" trial result
- [[Functional Unblinding]] — placebo participants were more likely than semaglutide participants to correctly guess their group assignment, likely because of GLP-1 drugs' well-publicized side-effect and weight-loss profile, complicating interpretation of the blinded results

## Key Takeaways

- Oral semaglutide reduced heavy drinking days and cravings in a 50-person, 8-week, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial (94% completion, high verified adherence).
- The trial **missed its primary endpoint** (a lab-based cue-elicited craving measure) and did not significantly reduce drinks-per-calendar-day, but did significantly cut heavy drinking days per week.
- Secondary/exploratory wins: fewer drinks per drinking day, lower Penn Alcohol Craving Scale scores, improved PROMIS alcohol-consequences scores, higher odds of dropping a full WHO risk-drinking-level category, and reduced cannabis use days among baseline cannabis users.
- Effect sizes for heavy drinking days and drinks-per-drinking-day were medium to large — potentially exceeding effects reported for currently approved AUD medications.
- No new alcohol use disorder medication has been approved since 2006; only 3 FDA-approved medications currently exist for AUD.
- Limitations: single site, small sample (94% White, all overweight/obese by design), short 8-week duration, no biomarker (e.g., phosphatidylethanol) to corroborate self-reported drinking, and evidence of functional unblinding. Larger, longer, multi-site confirmatory trials are planned.
- Side effects were mostly mild and similar across groups, though "definitely" drug-related events were more common with semaglutide; one participant discontinued due to a rash. Weight change did not differ significantly between groups over the 8 weeks.
