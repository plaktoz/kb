## Summary

An Oregon State University study of 159,391 postmenopausal women found that the absolute risk of malignant central nervous system (CNS) cancers rose as residential [[Radon]] concentration increased. The cohort is more than double the size of the only prior study of radon and CNS cancers, a 2013 Danish study with similar findings. The authors say residential radon may contribute meaningfully to the overall burden of these rare but deadly cancers.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Radon]]**: Odorless, colorless radioactive gas produced by [[Uranium]] decay in rocks and soils; a known [[Lung Cancer]] carcinogen.
- **Mechanism**: Radon decays and emits radioactive particles that collect in tissues and cause [[DNA Damage]], which can lead to cancer.
- **Indoor concentration**: Radon dilutes quickly outdoors but builds up indoors or where air exchange is poor.
- **[[Central Nervous System Cancer]]**: Rare, highly deadly cancers with poorly understood risk factors.
- **[[Women's Health Initiative]]**: Postmenopausal cohort followed for about 18 years; 451 CNS cancer cases identified.
- **Researchers**: [[Matthew Bozigar]] (OSU College of Health epidemiologist) and [[Carson Mowrer]] (now at UNC), published in *[[Neuroepidemiology]]*.
- **Exposure estimation**: Radon levels were estimated using data from the [[U.S. Geological Survey]], the [[EPA]], and other sources.
- **[[EPA Radon Guidelines]]**: No level is safe; mitigate at 4 pCi/L, consider mitigating at 2 pCi/L.

## Key Takeaways

- **Dose-Response Finding**: CNS cancer absolute risk rose with radon concentration across all metrics.
- **Cohort Scale**: 159,391 participants, more than double the 2013 Danish study.
- **Follow-Up**: About 18 years on average; 451 CNS cancer cases identified.
- **Disease Burden**: NIH predicts about 25,000 new cases and 18,000 deaths in 2026.
- **Survival**: Five-year survival is 33%; incidence is 6.1 per 100,000 people.
- **Cheap Detection**: Small passive detectors measure radon at low cost.
- **Mitigation**: Passive or active ventilation in basements and crawl spaces reduces exposure.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Carcinogen risk scales with exposure** — Cancer risk from a radioactive carcinogen rises as exposure concentration rises, with no threshold below which exposure is safe. · *model:* [[Dose-Response Relationship]]

**T2. Radiation damage is not organ-specific** — Radioactive decay particles that enter the body collect in tissues and damage DNA, so a carcinogen known for one organ can raise cancer risk in others.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Oregon State study find about radon and brain cancer?
**A**: Absolute risk of malignant central nervous system cancers rose with higher residential radon concentration across all metrics in a cohort of 159,391 postmenopausal women.

**Q2**: How does radon cause cancer, and why is it dangerous indoors?
**A**: Radon decays and emits radioactive particles that collect in tissues and damage DNA. It dilutes quickly outdoors but concentrates indoors or where air exchange is poor.

**Q3**: What practical steps does the article suggest for reducing radon risk?
**A**: Measure radon with low-cost passive detectors, and mitigate with passive or active ventilation in basements and crawl spaces. The EPA advises mitigation at 4 pCi/L and considering it at 2 pCi/L.
