## Summary
A scoping review of 21 studies (2023–2025) in npj Digital Medicine finds that GenAI mental health chatbots score well on user experience and perceived empathy but leave critical safety, sustained use, and clinical value questions unanswered. Attrition rises over time despite high initial uptake. Some users reported over-reliance, privacy concerns, and crisis-response limitations — gaps that regulation (e.g., 2026 state therapy chatbot bans) is beginning to address.

## Core Concepts

- [[GenAI Mental Health Chatbots]] — LLM-based chatbots that simulate therapeutic encounters with personalized, empathetic responses
- [[User Experience (UX) in Digital Health]] — key outcomes: satisfaction, acceptability, usability, engagement, perceived impact
- [[Attrition Paradox]] — high initial uptake declining over multi-week interventions despite positive user satisfaction ratings
- [[Safety Gaps in Chatbots]] — inability to respond appropriately during crises; misaligned or repetitive outputs erode trust
- [[CBT-Based Digital Interventions]] — most chatbots use cognitive-behavioral therapy principles including behavioral activation, mindfulness, psychoeducation
- [[State Therapy Chatbot Bans]] — complementary policy response closing the gap between promising UX and unproven clinical safety

## Key Takeaways

- **21 studies, 11 countries** — moderate evidence base; most are early-stage prototypes, not validated clinical tools
- **Moderate-to-high satisfaction** but users prefer free-flowing chat over predefined menus; customization matters
- **Empathy does not equal efficacy**: personalized, empathetic responses are not consistently linked to better clinical outcomes
- **Crisis response is a critical failure mode**: chatbots cannot reliably distinguish between routine emotional distress and genuine crisis situations
- **Privacy and over-reliance** are user-reported concerns alongside data security
- **Policy implication**: state bans on standalone AI therapy chatbots reflect this exact evidence gap — empathy at scale ≠ safe clinical practice

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: If user satisfaction is used as a proxy for clinical effectiveness, chatbot designers optimize for empathetic language and engagement — but a chatbot that feels good to use is not necessarily a chatbot that improves mental health outcomes.
