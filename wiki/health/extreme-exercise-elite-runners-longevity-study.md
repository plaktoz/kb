## Summary

A British Journal of Sports Medicine study of the first 200 men to run a sub-4-minute mile found they lived, on average, five years longer than the general population's predicted life expectancy, challenging the view that extreme exercise shortens lifespan. Researchers caution that genetic predisposition and lifestyle factors likely also contribute, and that further research is needed across other exercise types and durations.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Sub-4-Minute Mile]]** cohort — the first 200 male runners (1928-1955 birth years, from 28 countries) to achieve the feat, tracked for longevity outcomes.
- **[[Cardiovascular Adaptation]]** in elite athletes — sustained aerobic output produces enlarged hearts, previously suspected but not confirmed to harm long-term health.
- **Longevity by decade cohort** — runners from the 1950s lived ~9 years longer than average; 1960s cohort ~5.5 years longer; 1970s cohort ~3 years longer, suggesting a narrowing but persistent longevity edge over time.
- **Convergent evidence from cycling** — similar longevity benefits observed in elite Tour de France cyclists, reinforcing the pattern beyond running alone.

## Key Takeaways

- **Average age of death was 73** for all 200 study participants, versus **77** for the 140 still alive at study time — both above general-population predictions.
- **Elite milers lived ~5 years longer on average** than predicted life expectancy based on age, sex, birth year, and nationality.
- **30% of the cohort (60 of 200) had died** at the time of the study.
- **Findings contradict prior assumptions** that extreme exercise increases cardiac events or harmful structural heart changes.
- **Study is observational and runner-specific** — genetic predisposition to speed may confound results, and findings don't extend to other athlete types without further study.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this study?
**A**: Elite athletes who achieved an extreme exercise feat (running a sub-4-minute mile) lived about five years longer on average than the general population's predicted life expectancy, contradicting the belief that extreme exercise shortens lifespan.

**Q2**: What specific longevity data supports this claim?
**A**: Of 200 sub-4-minute milers tracked, those who died averaged age 73 and survivors averaged age 77, both above population predictions; the longevity gap ranged from about 9 years (1950s cohort) to 3 years (1970s cohort).

**Q3**: What is the practical, cautious takeaway for non-elite individuals?
**A**: Researchers suggest the finding is reassuring about the risks of "overdoing" exercise, but recommend most people aim for regular, moderate-to-intense exercise alongside good diet rather than attempting to replicate elite-level training, since genetic factors likely also contribute to the observed longevity.
