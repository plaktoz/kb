## Summary
A 13.5-year study of 87,340 Norwegian adults found that even low levels of physical activity were associated with meaningfully lower stroke and mortality risk, with similar benefits regardless of atrial fibrillation (AFib) status — challenging the assumption that AFib patients should avoid exercise.

## Core Concepts
- [[Atrial Fibrillation]] (AFib) and its relationship to stroke risk.
- Dose-response benefit of [[Physical Activity]] across inactive, low, moderate, and high activity tiers.
- The "[[Talk Test]]" as a practical intensity gauge for starting exercise safely.

## Key Takeaways
- **Large Cohort**: 87,340 adults tracked a median 13.5 years; 6,539 had or developed AFib.
- **Any Activity Helps**: Low activity alone cut stroke risk 9% and mortality risk 11% versus inactivity.
- **Life Expectancy Gain**: High activity was linked to +1.15 years of life expectancy in AFib patients.
- **Fears Overblown**: AFib-related exercise fears are largely unfounded for non-elite athletes; exercise should complement, not replace, standard treatment.
- **Start Small**: Begin with 5-10 minute walks once or twice daily; prioritize consistency over intensity.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's core finding?
**A**: Physical activity, even at low levels, was associated with lower stroke and death risk in a large Norwegian cohort, with similar benefit for people with and without AFib.

**Q2**: What specific numbers support this?
**A**: Low/moderate/high activity levels were linked to 9%/19%/18% lower stroke risk and 11%/18%/22% lower all-cause mortality versus inactivity.

**Q3**: How should someone with AFib apply this?
**A**: Start with short, low-intensity walks, use the talk test to gauge effort, and treat exercise as a complement to (not replacement for) standard AFib medications, consulting a doctor if symptoms are significant.
