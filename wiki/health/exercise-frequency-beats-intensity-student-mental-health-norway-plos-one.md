## Summary
A PLOS One study of 10,460 Norwegian higher-education students found that lower physical activity predicted anxiety or depression one year later. Exercise frequency was the most consistent predictor, more so than intensity or duration. The study is observational, so it cannot prove exercise prevented these conditions.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Exercise Frequency]]**: How often students worked out was the factor most reliably tied to later mental health.
- **[[Depression]] and [[Anxiety]]**: Assessed one year after baseline with a self-administered diagnostic tool.
- **[[Prospective Cohort Study]]**: 10,460 students aged 18-35 tracked from questionnaire to follow-up (SHoT study, [[Michael Grasdalsmoen]] and colleagues).
- **[[Sex Differences in Exercise Effects]]**: Lower intensity mattered more for women; fewer total weekly hours mattered more for men.
- **[[WHO Physical Activity Guidelines]]**: Results support at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity weekly.

## Key Takeaways
- **Frequency over intensity**: Exercise frequency predicted later mental health more reliably than intensity or duration.
- **Dose-response**: Disorder likelihood fell as weekly exercise rose, up to about 10 hours.
- **Sex differences**: Women showed an intensity link; men showed a total-hours link.
- **Guideline support**: Findings back the WHO's 150-minute weekly recommendation.
- **Causation caveat**: Observational design cannot prove exercise prevented depression or anxiety.
- **Policy angle**: Make physical activity accessible and natural in student life.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Norwegian student study conclude about exercise and mental health?
**A**: How often students exercised was more reliably linked to later depression and anxiety than workout intensity or duration. Lower activity predicted more disorders one year later.

**Q2**: What evidence shows a dose-response pattern, and where does it stop?
**A**: Among 10,460 students, the likelihood of common mental disorders decreased as weekly exercise increased, continuing up to about 10 hours per week.

**Q3**: How should the findings be applied, and with what caution?
**A**: Universities should make physical activity accessible and a natural part of student life. The authors caution that the observational design cannot prove exercise prevented depression or anxiety.
