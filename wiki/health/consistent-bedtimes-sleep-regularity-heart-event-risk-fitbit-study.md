## Summary
A Circulation: Population Health and Outcomes study of over 10,000 Fitbit wearers found that night-to-night variability in sleep timing was linked to a 20-23 percent higher risk of serious heart events. Sleep duration showed no association with major heart-related risks. The study cannot prove cause and effect, but regularity is a controllable factor.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Sleep Regularity]]**: Consistency of bedtime and wake time from night to night, the factor that dominated in the findings.
- **[[Sleep Duration]]**: Hours slept, which was not associated with any major heart-related risks.
- **[[Wearable Sleep Tracking]]**: Over 10,000 participants wore a Fitbit for six months, yielding data on quality, quantity, regularity, timing and sleep cycles.
- **[[Cardiovascular Events]]**: Outcomes included heart failure, acute myocardial infarction, cardiomyopathy and ischemic strokes.
- **[[Nguyen Minh Tuan Viet]]**: Lead researcher who called the dominance of regularity over quantity surprising.

## Key Takeaways
- **Timing Variability**: Night-to-night shifts in sleep timing linked to 20-23% higher serious heart event risk.
- **Length Swings**: Each 29-minute night-to-night change in sleep length linked to 24% higher risk.
- **Specific Risks**: 20% higher heart failure risk and 29% higher cardiomyopathy risk.
- **Duration Null**: Total sleep duration was not associated with any major heart-related risks.
- **Controllable Factor**: Consistent bedtimes and wake times are something people can actively control.
- **Causation Caveat**: Study did not establish cause and effect; intervention trials still needed.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Association in observational data does not establish cause** — The study links sleep irregularity to heart events but cannot show that regularizing sleep lowers risk; intervention research is still needed. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about sleep and heart health?
**A**: Consistency of sleep timing mattered more than hours slept. Total sleep duration was not associated with any major heart-related risks.

**Q2**: What data supports the finding?
**A**: Over 10,000 Fitbit wearers tracked for six months showed 20-23% higher risk of serious heart events with night-to-night changes in sleep timing. Each 29-minute shift in sleep length was linked to 24% higher risk.

**Q3**: How should the result be applied, and with what caution?
**A**: Keep consistent bedtimes and wake times, since regularity is controllable even when total sleep is not. The study did not establish cause and effect, so more research is needed on whether intervening lowers risk.
