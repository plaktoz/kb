## Summary
A new study following 112,000+ people over decades found that drinking at least one sugar-sweetened beverage daily is associated with a 2.45x higher risk of stomach cancer, while artificially sweetened beverages showed no such association — adding stomach cancer to the list of cancers already linked to sugary-drink consumption.

## Core Concepts

- [[Sugar-Sweetened Beverage Cancer Link]] — extends prior evidence linking sugary drinks to colorectal, breast, and liver cancer risk to stomach (gastric) cancer specifically, based on a large long-term cohort
- [[Stomach Cancer Screening Gap]] — unlike colorectal cancer, stomach cancer has no standard U.S. screening test and is often diagnosed late, making dietary prevention comparatively more important
- [[Metabolic vs. Inflammatory Mechanism Uncertainty]] — researchers note the causal mechanism is unclear: candidates include fructose's metabolic effects, obesity/insulin resistance from calorie load, and general inflammation, but none is confirmed

## Key Takeaways

- **Risk magnitude**: ≥1 sugar-sweetened beverage/day (soda, punch, lemonade, sports drinks) associated with 2.45x higher stomach cancer risk vs. <1 serving/month.
- **Artificial sweeteners not implicated**: no elevated risk found for artificially sweetened (diet) beverages, suggesting sugar/calorie content — not sweetness itself — drives the association.
- **Study scale**: data from 112,000+ participants enrolled in long-running cohort studies (1976 and 1986), tracked via diet/lifestyle questionnaires; published in Gastro Hep Advances, Aug 17, 2026.
- **Practical swap advice**: taper rather than quit abruptly — mix sparkling water into soda, shift sweetened/unsweetened tea ratios gradually — and watch for added-sugar terms (high-fructose corn syrup, cane syrup, concentrated fruit juice) on labels, since lemonade and some sports/smoothie drinks also count.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Drinking at least one sugar-sweetened beverage daily is associated with more than double the risk of stomach cancer, based on a large long-term cohort study, while artificially sweetened drinks showed no such link.

**Q2**: What specific risk figure does the study report, and what's the comparison baseline?
**A**: A 2.45x higher stomach cancer risk for people drinking ≥1 sugar-sweetened beverage/day compared to those drinking less than one serving per month.

**Q3**: Why do researchers consider this finding particularly actionable for cancer prevention?
**A**: Stomach cancer has no standard U.S. screening test and is often diagnosed late with limited treatment options, so a modifiable dietary risk factor offers a rare concrete prevention lever where early detection tools don't exist.
