## Summary

A large observational study published in *Neurology* (110,000+ participants) found that transient ischemic attacks (TIAs), or mini-strokes, are associated with a 34% higher long-term dementia risk compared to people with no stroke history. The elevated risk was sharpest in the first year (75% higher) but persisted across a 20-year follow-up. Experts caution that TIAs should never be dismissed just because their symptoms resolve.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Transient Ischemic Attack]] (TIA)**: A temporary blockage of blood flow to the brain causing stroke-like symptoms that resolve without permanent damage; commonly called a mini-stroke.
- **[[Dementia Risk]]**: The probability of developing cognitive decline; TIA patients developed dementia at roughly three times the rate they experienced a subsequent full stroke.
- **[[Vascular Disease]]**: TIAs may serve as a marker of underlying vascular pathology that independently drives long-term [[Cognitive Decline]], rather than directly causing it.
- **[[Raed Joundi]]**: Lead study author; found that elevated dementia risk "did not disappear after that early period but remained elevated over the long term."
- **[[Long-term Follow-up Studies]]**: ~7-year median follow-up, but risk was tracked up to 20 years; after ~5 years, TIA patients' dementia rates converged with those of full-stroke patients.

## Key Takeaways

- **34% higher dementia risk**: TIA patients vs. matched controls over ~7-year follow-up.
- **75% higher in year one**: Risk is most acute immediately following a TIA event.
- **Dementia > stroke recurrence**: Dementia occurred ~3x more often than a second stroke in TIA patients.
- **Risk persists 20 years**: Elevated risk does not resolve after the acute phase.
- **Convergence at ~5 years**: TIA and full-stroke patients' dementia rates align around 5 years post-event.
- **Underlying vascular disease**: TIAs may reflect, not solely cause, the pathology driving dementia.
- **Never dismiss resolved symptoms**: Both study experts stress urgent follow-up even when TIA symptoms clear.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Canary in the Coal Mine]]**: A TIA is not merely a near-miss stroke but a signal of broader cerebrovascular vulnerability — ignoring it because symptoms resolved misses the systemic risk it reveals.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core finding of the study regarding TIAs and dementia?
**A**: TIA patients had a 34% higher long-term dementia risk than matched controls, with the elevated risk persisting across 20 years of follow-up.

**Q2**: How does the first-year risk compare to longer-term risk, and what happens around the 5-year mark?
**A**: Risk is 75% higher in the first year post-TIA; after approximately 5 years, TIA patients' dementia rates converge with those of full-stroke patients.

**Q3**: What is the clinical implication for patients who experience a TIA with fully resolved symptoms?
**A**: A TIA should never be dismissed because symptoms resolve — the elevated cognitive risk persists long-term and warrants ongoing monitoring for cognitive decline.
