## Summary

Some U.S. medical schools are adding hands-on nutrition training — cooking demonstrations, culinary-medicine electives — in response to HHS Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr.'s push for future physicians to be versed in 71 nutrition-related topics. So far only 73 of 211 eligible M.D./D.O. programs have signed on, and participating schools told STAT they plan to selectively adopt only evidence-backed content, avoiding topics nutrition experts warn could cause "wellness creep" in medical curricula.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Advancing Nutrition Education]]**: HHS's initiative outlining 71 nutrition-related topics it wants future physicians proficient in, including nutrient bioavailability (soaking, sprouting, fermenting foods) and food-based medicine for metabolic disease.
- **[[Wellness Creep]]**: A term nutrition experts use for the risk of unproven or non-evidence-based "wellness" content entering formal, credentialed medical curricula under the banner of nutrition education.
- **[[Selective Adoption]]**: The strategy several medical schools described — participating in the broader nutrition-education push while explicitly filtering out MAHA-aligned topics not supported by evidence.
- **[[Culinary Medicine]]**: An emerging medical-education discipline combining cooking instruction with clinical nutrition training, exemplified by McGovern Medical School's new elective and teaching kitchen.

## Key Takeaways

- **Only 73 of 211 eligible M.D./D.O. programs (54 M.D., 19 D.O., across 36 states) have signed on** to HHS's nutrition-education initiative so far — the majority, including several leading institutions, have not joined.
- **Participating schools are explicitly filtering the curriculum**, adopting nutrition content while declining topics they consider scientifically unsupported.
- **McGovern Medical School's response** includes teaching-kitchen cooking demonstrations, a campus "holistic garden," and a culinary-medicine elective.
- **Kennedy has attributed rising chronic disease rates partly to physicians not routinely discussing diet with patients**, framing nutrition education as a chronic-disease prevention lever.
- **The tension**: schools want to respond to a legitimate gap (physician nutrition literacy) without importing politically aligned but evidence-weak content into accredited curricula.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core tension described in the article?
**A**: Medical schools want to respond to HHS's push for more physician nutrition education — a genuine gap in most curricula — while explicitly avoiding non-evidence-based topics associated with the broader MAHA political movement, to prevent "wellness creep."

**Q2**: How many eligible medical schools have joined the nutrition-education initiative, and what does that participation rate suggest?
**A**: 73 of 211 eligible M.D./D.O. programs (about a third) have signed on, suggesting cautious, selective institutional uptake rather than broad enthusiastic adoption.

**Q3**: What concrete example illustrates how a participating school is implementing the initiative?
**A**: McGovern Medical School at UT Health Houston added hands-on nutrition experiences — teaching-kitchen cooking demonstrations, tours of a campus "holistic garden," and a culinary-medicine elective — for incoming students.
