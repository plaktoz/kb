## Summary

Three recent studies converge on the conclusion that both what you eat and when you eat it meaningfully shape dementia risk. A 26-year longitudinal study found that managing just three midlife risk factors — diabetes, hypertension, and smoking — can extend dementia-free life by up to 13 years. Two other trials reinforce the picture through early-life sugar restriction and time-restricted eating.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Modifiable Dementia Risk]]**: The [[WHO]] estimates up to 45% of dementia risks are modifiable through lifestyle changes; over 57 million people worldwide currently live with dementia.
- **[[Cardiovascular-Brain Health Link]]**: Midlife control of [[Diabetes]], [[Hypertension]], and [[Smoking]] strongly predicts dementia outcomes — all three simultaneously increases risk 2.69×.
- **[[First 1,000 Days]]**: Early-life sugar restriction is linked to 46% lower [[Alzheimer's Disease]] risk and 27% lower all-cause dementia risk — see [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]] for full coverage.
- **[[Time-Restricted Eating]]**: Limiting daily meals to a 9-hour window improved memory, learning, and problem-solving in older women over 6 months beyond caloric restriction alone — see [[time-restricted-eating-brain-health-older-adults]] for full coverage.
- **Dr. Dung Trinh** — Commented on both the midlife risk factor study and the time-restricted eating trial; emphasizes the cardiovascular-cognitive health connection.

## Key Takeaways

- **2.69× higher dementia risk** in adults who had all three midlife factors: diabetes, hypertension, and smoking.
- **Up to 13 extra dementia-free years** associated with avoiding all three risk factors at midlife.
- The 26-year study tracked 12,409 participants (published in *Neurology Open Access*).
- **Early sugar restriction**: 46% lower Alzheimer's risk, 27% lower all-cause dementia risk (UK Biobank, *npj Aging*).
- **9-hour eating window**: cognitive improvements in memory, learning, and problem-solving after 6 months (47 postmenopausal women).
- "When people eat may matter for cognitive health, not just how much they eat" — Dr. Trinh.
- All three findings are observational or early-stage; causal conclusions require further research.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Multiplier Effect]]**: Having all three midlife risk factors (diabetes, hypertension, smoking) doesn't add risk linearly — the 2.69× hazard ratio suggests these factors compound each other, making co-occurrence far more dangerous than any single factor alone.
- **[[Critical Window Hypothesis]]**: The first 1,000 days and midlife both emerge as particularly high-leverage periods for brain health intervention — the same protective investments yield different magnitudes of benefit at different life stages, suggesting timing of the intervention matters as much as the intervention itself.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the 26-year midlife risk factor study?
**A**: Adults who had all three risk factors — diabetes, hypertension, and smoking — were 2.69 times more likely to develop dementia; avoiding all three was associated with up to 13 additional dementia-free years.

**Q2**: What specific eating-window length showed cognitive benefits, and in which population?
**A**: A 9-hour eating window (rather than unrestricted eating) improved memory, learning, and problem-solving after 6 months in 47 overweight or obese postmenopausal women aged 50–79.

**Q3**: What is the practical implication for someone in midlife wanting to reduce dementia risk?
**A**: The three most impactful levers appear to be managing diabetes, controlling hypertension, and quitting smoking — controlling all three simultaneously is disproportionately protective, adding up to 13 dementia-free years compared to having all three risk factors present.
