## Summary

A five-year retrospective study at Johns Hopkins Hospital, published in the Annals of the American Thoracic Society, found that the same make of pulse oximeter gradually shifted from overestimating to underestimating blood oxygen in all patients at higher oxygen levels, with worse inaccuracies in patients of color. The drift has no known explanation, and the manufacturer, Masimo, says it knows of no sensor, software, or manufacturing changes that would account for it. Together with the FDA-funded EquiOx and Emory studies, the findings suggest oximeter errors are inconsistent and come from more than skin tone, which makes it hard for clinicians to correct for them.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Pulse Oximetry]]**: Devices shine light through skin at two wavelengths, one absorbed by oxygenated blood and one by deoxygenated blood, then compare absorption to estimate the percent of blood that is oxygenated.
- **[[Pulse Oximeter Racial Bias]]**: Several studies, including a seminal 2020 [[New England Journal of Medicine]] paper, showed oximeters overestimate oxygen on darker skin, making patients of color appear healthier than they are.
- **[[Arterial Blood Gas Test]]**: The gold-standard but more invasive measure of blood oxygen, used as the comparison benchmark.
- **Hopkins retrospective study**: Five years of ICU data at [[Johns Hopkins Hospital]] showed readings drifting from overestimates to underestimates against arterial blood gas results.
- **[[EquiOx Study]]**: An FDA-funded prospective study, published earlier this year, meant to explain overestimation on darker skin. It found underestimation instead, which its authors struggled to explain.
- **Emory prospective study**: Found racial bias that skin tone alone did not fully explain. Conditions like [[Anemia]], cardiovascular disease, or liver disease, more common in patients of color, may also affect readings.
- **[[Masimo]]**: Manufacturer of the oximeters studied. It disputes that the data show a cause and cites company-supported studies that found no skin-tone differences.
- **People**: [[Ashraf Fawzy]] (Hopkins, study author), [[Jack Iwashyna]] (Hopkins, study author), [[Thomas Valley]] (University of Colorado Anschutz, co-author of the 2020 NEJM paper), [[Sivasubramanium Bhavani]] (Emory, led the prospective study).
- **[[Retrospective vs Prospective Studies]]**: Prospective studies were expected to find root causes, but so far they have raised more questions than answers.

## Key Takeaways

- **Drift**: The same oximeter make moved from overestimating to underestimating oxygen over five years.
- **Scope**: Underestimation hit all patients at higher oxygen levels, and was worse in patients of color.
- **Black box**: Fawzy says nobody knows why readings change year over year.
- **Manufacturer response**: Masimo reports no sensor, software, or manufacturing changes during the study period.
- **Other factors**: Sensor type, placement, signal quality, and perfusion can all affect readings.
- **Overestimation risk**: Patients may not get care they need.
- **Underestimation risk**: Patients may get unnecessary care or stay in hospital longer.
- **Beyond skin tone**: Anemia, cardiovascular disease, and liver disease may also bias readings.
- **Calibration limits**: An oximeter fully calibrated for skin tone could still show racial bias.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Systematic vs Random Error]]**: A consistent bias, like a clock that always runs five minutes slow, can be corrected for. Error that changes direction over time cannot be corrected, which is Valley's central point.
- **[[Confounding Variables]]**: The Emory finding that conditions more common in patients of color can distort readings means skin tone may be a proxy for other causes, so fixing skin-tone calibration alone may not remove the bias.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the Johns Hopkins five-year study find about pulse oximeter accuracy?
**A**: The same make of oximeter shifted from overestimating to underestimating blood oxygen in all patients at higher oxygen levels, and the errors were worse in patients of color.

**Q2**: How does a pulse oximeter estimate blood oxygen, and what is it benchmarked against?
**A**: It shines light at two wavelengths, one absorbed by oxygenated blood and one by deoxygenated blood, and compares absorption to estimate the percent oxygenated. Its readings are compared against the more invasive arterial blood gas test.

**Q3**: Why is inconsistent error worse for clinicians than a known, consistent bias?
**A**: A consistent bias, like a clock that is always five minutes slow, can be adjusted for. When readings drift between overestimating and underestimating, clinicians cannot reliably correct them, which risks both missed care and unnecessary care.
