## Summary

A two-year RCT published in *JAMA Network Open* compared a Mediterranean-style diet (Med-South, adapted for the U.S. South) against a calorie-focused program (WeightWatchers) in 360 obese North Carolina adults. Both arms achieved nearly identical weight loss (~3.4–3.5%), but the Mediterranean group showed significantly greater improvements in diet quality and far higher program satisfaction. Dietitians and researchers conclude that diet quality and adherence together outperform calorie restriction alone for durable health outcomes.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Mediterranean Diet]]** — a whole-food dietary pattern emphasizing vegetables, fruit, nuts, whole grains, and olive oil; adapted as Med-South for Southern U.S. food culture in this trial
- **[[Calorie Counting / WeightWatchers]]** — a structured calorie-deficit approach that does not mandate specific food types, only total energy intake
- **[[Diet Quality vs. Calorie Restriction]]** — the central tension: calorie approaches can drive weight loss on any food, but quality-focused approaches may confer additional metabolic and behavioral benefits
- **[[DIETFITS Trial]]** — a 2024 companion study reinforcing that both dietary adherence *and* diet quality together produce the greatest weight loss outcomes

## Key Takeaways

- **Equal short-term weight loss**: both groups lost ~3.4–3.5% body weight over 24 months
- **Diet quality advantage**: Mediterranean group ate significantly more nuts, whole grains, vegetables, and fruit
- **Program satisfaction gap**: 95% of Mediterranean participants would recommend the program vs. 68% for calorie-counting
- **Quality over quantity**: [[Kristin Kirkpatrick]] emphasizes long-term sustainability favors diet quality approaches
- **Calorie caveat**: [[Christopher Gardner]] (Stanford) notes weight loss is achievable on junk food if calories are restricted — but quality supports better long-term health
- **DIETFITS finding**: adherence + quality together outperform either factor alone

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When weight loss becomes the sole target metric, dieters optimize for calorie reduction and may sacrifice food quality — achieving the number while undermining long-term metabolic health.
- **[[Systems Thinking]]**: Diet quality affects multiple downstream outcomes (satiety, micronutrient density, gut microbiome, chronic disease risk) that a single calorie metric cannot capture; optimizing the whole system outperforms optimizing one variable.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What was the core finding of the Med-South vs. WeightWatchers trial regarding weight loss?
**A**: Both groups achieved nearly identical weight loss (~3.4–3.5%) over 24 months, meaning diet quality and calorie-counting approaches were equally effective for short-term weight reduction.

**Q2**: What distinguished the Mediterranean diet group beyond weight loss outcomes?
**A**: The Mediterranean group showed significantly greater improvements in diet quality (more nuts, whole grains, vegetables, fruit) and reported much higher satisfaction — 95% would recommend the program vs. 68% in the calorie-counting group.

**Q3**: How does the DIETFITS trial complement this study's implications for practitioners?
**A**: DIETFITS found that dietary adherence and diet quality together produce the greatest weight loss, suggesting that neither factor alone is sufficient — practitioners should target both food quality and consistent adherence rather than calories exclusively.
