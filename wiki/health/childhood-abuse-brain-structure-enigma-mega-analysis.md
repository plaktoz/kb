## Summary

A UCLA-led MRI mega-analysis of 3,711 people across eight countries found that the severity of childhood abuse and neglect is linked to measurable deviations from normal brain structure, a kind of "neurobiological scar." The effects were not uniform: some regions thinned or shrank while others thickened or grew, and the pattern differed by maltreatment type, age and sex, with the strongest effects in young women. The authors say this challenges the idea that childhood trauma has a single, fixed effect on the brain.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Childhood Maltreatment]]**: Measured with a standard retrospective questionnaire covering physical, emotional and sexual abuse plus physical and emotional neglect. The answers were turned into separate abuse-severity and neglect-severity scores.
- **[[ENIGMA Consortium]]**: An international neuroimaging collective whose MDD and PTSD working groups supplied MRI scans of 1,389 patients and 2,322 healthy controls.
- **[[Neuroplasticity|Heightened Plasticity]]**: Childhood is a sensitive period when major neural networks are still forming, so environmental inputs have an outsized effect on brain development.
- **[[Normative Brain Charts]]**: A "growth chart for the brain" index used to measure how far each person's brain structure deviated from what is expected for their age.
- **Brain metrics**: Cortical thickness, overall surface area and subcortical volume, with the cortex split into about 70 regions.
- **Researchers**: [[Tiffany Ho]] (UCLA psychology professor, corresponding author) and [[Haley Wang]] (UCLA Psychiatry postdoc, lead author). Published in *[[Biological Psychiatry]]* (2026).
- **Linked disorders**: [[Major Depressive Disorder]] and [[Posttraumatic Stress Disorder]], for which childhood maltreatment is a major risk factor.
- Related: [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]], another adversity type that predicts mental health risk.

## Key Takeaways

- **Scale**: Largest study of its kind, with 3,711 people from eight countries.
- **Abuse effects**: Larger thalamus and pallidum, and smaller isthmus cingulate cortex.
- **Orbitofrontal**: Abuse was linked to larger medial orbitofrontal cortex surface area.
- **Sex difference**: Effects were strongest in young women and persisted in older women.
- **Possible reasons**: Girls face more sexual trauma, or their brains may respond more strongly.
- **Neglect effects**: Narrower patterns. Young women showed cingulate surface-area changes; young men showed a smaller right middle temporal cortex.
- **Non-uniform**: Some regions thinned or shrank while others thickened or expanded.
- **Caveat**: The scans say nothing about any individual's cognitive or emotional abilities.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of the UCLA/ENIGMA childhood maltreatment study?
**A**: More severe childhood abuse and neglect are linked to measurable, non-uniform deviations from normal brain structure that vary by maltreatment type, age and sex, a "neurobiological scar."

**Q2**: Which structural differences were linked to more severe childhood abuse?
**A**: Larger thalamus and pallidum volumes, smaller isthmus cingulate cortex regions, and a larger medial orbitofrontal cortex surface area than expected for age. These effects were most pronounced in young women.

**Q3**: How do the findings change how we should think about childhood trauma's effect on the brain?
**A**: Trauma does not have one static effect. Different types of adversity are linked to different, age- and sex-dependent changes. These scars should not be read as measures of an individual's cognitive or emotional ability.
