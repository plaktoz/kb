## Summary

A Mass General Brigham and Rush University study of 1,338 older adults followed for up to 19 years found that longer and more frequent daytime naps, especially in the morning, were associated with higher mortality. Naps were measured objectively with wrist activity monitors. The researchers stress this is correlation, not causation, and that excessive napping likely signals underlying disease.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Daytime Napping]]**: Common in older adults (20% to 60% nap regularly); excessive napping is tied to several health concerns.
- **[[Actigraphy]]**: Wrist activity monitors worn for 10 days gave objective nap duration, frequency, timing, and day-to-day variation.
- **[[Rush Memory and Aging Project]]**: Long-running cohort launched in 1997, mostly white older adults in northern Illinois.
- **[[All-Cause Mortality]]**: The outcome tracked through 2025 against baseline napping habits.
- **[[Wearable Health Monitoring]]**: Researchers propose using wearable nap assessments to catch conditions early.
- **[[Circadian Dysregulation]]**: One possible underlying driver of excessive napping, alongside chronic disease and sleep disturbances.
- **[[Chenlu Gao]]**: Lead author, Mass General Brigham; published in *JAMA Network Open*.

## Key Takeaways

- **Duration**: Each additional hour of daily napping linked to about 13% higher mortality risk.
- **Frequency**: One extra nap per day linked to about a 7% increase in risk.
- **Timing**: Morning nappers had a 30% higher mortality risk than afternoon nappers.
- **Irregularity**: Day-to-day variability in nap timing or length showed no increased mortality risk.
- **Measurement**: Objective monitoring avoids the flaws of self-reported napping in earlier studies.
- **Application**: Wearable nap tracking could flag hidden illness or neurological decline in older adults.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Correlation does not establish causation** — A strong association between napping and death can reflect an underlying condition rather than an effect of napping. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: Duration, Frequency, Timing, Application

**T2. A behavior can serve as a marker of hidden underlying state** — Excessive napping may be a symptom of unrecognized disease, so tracking it can give early warning. · *model:* [[Reverse Causation]]

**T3. Objective measurement is more reliable than self-report** — Recall-based data omit details such as nap timing and regularity and may be inaccurate. · *model:* none

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What did the 19-year study find about napping and mortality?
**A**: Older adults who napped longer or more often, particularly in the morning, had higher mortality risk, though the link is correlational.

**Q2**: What specific figures support this?
**A**: Each extra hour of daily napping was linked to about 13% higher mortality, each extra nap to about 7%, and morning napping to 30% higher risk than afternoon napping.

**Q3**: How might these findings be applied?
**A**: Wearable activity trackers could monitor daytime sleep patterns in older adults to flag underlying illness, disrupted sleep cycles, or neurological decline earlier.
