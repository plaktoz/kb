## Summary

A large South Korean retrospective study of 101,148 adults, published in [[JAMA Internal Medicine]], found that fasting duration made little clinical difference for most of 121 routine laboratory tests. Only a handful — notably blood glucose, triglycerides, liver enzymes and lipase — were meaningfully higher when patients had fasted for less than 8 hours, and fasting beyond 12 hours added nothing. The researchers propose replacing the blanket 8–12 hour [[Pre-Test Fasting]] rule with test-specific requirements, pending validation in more diverse populations.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Pre-Test Fasting]]**: The 8–12 hour no-food rule, originally meant for glucose and lipid testing, that drifted into a default for nearly all routine blood panels.
- **[[Nonfasting Blood Testing]]**: People spend most of the day in a fed state, so nonfasting results better represent habitual physiology.
- **[[Retrospective Cohort Study]]**: Researchers screened nearly 10 million test results from 670 South Korean hospitals, using phlebotomist-documented time since last meal.
- **[[Within-Person Comparison]]**: Paired comparisons of the same patients across visits with different fasting durations ruled out health differences as a confounder.
- **[[Clinical Error Threshold]]**: The benchmark used to decide whether a fasting-related shift in a result was large enough to matter clinically.
- **Fasting-sensitive analytes**: [[Blood Glucose]], [[Triglycerides]], [[Lipase]] and some [[Liver Enzymes]] showed meaningful changes with short fasts.
- **[[Sunghwan Shin]]**: Lead author of "Fasting Duration and Differences Across Routine Laboratory Tests"; an accompanying commentary by [[Liv Tybjærg Nordestgaard]] et al was titled "Time to Stop Fasting Before Blood Sampling."

## Key Takeaways

- **Scale**: 101,148 adults; ~10 million results from 670 hospitals; 121 routine tests.
- **Fasting groups**: Under 8 hours, 8–12 hours, and over 12 hours.
- **Unaffected tests**: Blood counts, kidney markers, electrolytes and most liver enzymes were stable.
- **Only 10 tests** exceeded clinical error thresholds in within-person comparisons.
- **Under-8-hour shifts**: Glucose +10.8%, triglycerides +20.1%, lipase +36.4%.
- **Glucose decay**: Dropped about 4.3 mg/dL per additional hour of fasting.
- **No benefit past 12 hours**: Longer fasts only added patient discomfort.
- **Hidden costs of fasting**: Extra visits, delayed same-day testing, early-morning lab congestion.
- **Proposal**: Test-specific fasting rules instead of one-size-fits-all requirements.
- **Caveat**: Findings need replication beyond the South Korean population.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the study's central finding about fasting before routine blood tests?
**A**: For most of 121 routine lab tests, fasting duration made little clinical difference; only a handful of tests, like glucose and triglycerides, were meaningfully affected by fasting less than 8 hours.

**Q2**: How did the researchers rule out patients' health differences as the cause of result variations, and what did they find for glucose?
**A**: They compared the same people across separate visits with different fasting durations. With under 8 hours of fasting, glucose was 10.8% higher and fell about 4.3 mg/dL per additional fasting hour.

**Q3**: What practical change do the researchers propose, and why?
**A**: Replace the blanket fasting rule with test-specific requirements, since fasting only matters for a few tests and imposes costs like extra visits and morning lab rushes. Fasting beyond 12 hours should be avoided since it adds discomfort without improving accuracy.
