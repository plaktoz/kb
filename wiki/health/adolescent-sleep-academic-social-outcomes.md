## Summary
A UC Davis Health study of adolescents aged 10-13 found that sleep quality is the strongest lifestyle factor — ahead of screen time, exercise, and diet — linking mental health symptoms (anxiety, depression, psychotic-like disturbances) to worse grades and social functioning. The finding suggests sleep may be an underappreciated, actionable lever for supporting struggling youth.

## Core Concepts
Using data from the [[Adolescent Brain Cognitive Development (ABCD) Study]], researchers examined how four [[Lifestyle Factors]] — sleep, screen time, physical activity, diet — mediate the relationship between [[Mental Health]] symptoms and academic/social outcomes in early [[Adolescence]]. [[Sleep Quality]] explained the largest share of the association: 18.5% for depression, 36.3% for anxiety, and 8.3% for psychotic-like disturbances — well above screen time's contribution (5-6.3%).

## Key Takeaways
- **Sleep dominates**: Sleep was the strongest lifestyle factor linking mental health symptoms to lower grades and social problems, well ahead of screen time.
- **Anxiety link strongest**: Sleep explained 36.3% of the association between anxiety and functional outcomes.
- **Global context**: About one in seven adolescents worldwide experience a mental disorder; depression and anxiety are leading causes of youth disability.
- **Actionable target**: Unlike underlying mental health conditions, sleep habits are something families can directly address, per the study's senior author.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this study?
**A**: Sleep quality matters more than screen time, exercise, or diet in explaining why adolescent mental health symptoms translate into worse academic and social outcomes.

**Q2**: What specific statistics support this claim?
**A**: Sleep accounted for 18.5% of the association between depression and functional outcomes, 36.3% for anxiety, and 8.3% for psychotic-like disturbances — larger shares than screen time (5-6.3%).

**Q3**: What is the practical implication for parents and clinicians?
**A**: Since sleep is a modifiable lifestyle factor, prioritizing healthy sleep routines may be a practical way to support struggling adolescents' emotional well-being and daily functioning, alongside (not instead of) professional mental health care.
