## Summary

This HBR case study uses the fictional German luxury automaker **Hohenbruck Motoren** and its car subscription service **Hohenbruck Access** (launched 2021) to probe whether subscription models genuinely deliver value for both companies and customers. The monthly fee exceeds a typical lease cost but bundles flexibility and services in return. The central question is whether such a program is truly working — or merely appears to be — from both financial and strategic standpoints.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Subscription Business Model]]**: A recurring-revenue structure that trades ownership for access, often bundling services and flexibility at a premium price point.
- **[[Customer Lifetime Value]]**: The lens through which subscription viability is ultimately judged — does the bundled offering retain customers long enough to justify higher acquisition and operational costs?
- **[[Business Model Viability]]**: Whether the economics of a model (unit margins, churn, utilization) support sustainable growth.
- **[[Customer Strategy]]**: How a company designs offerings to attract, retain, and grow value from its target customer segment.
- **[[Growth Strategy]]**: The broader strategic rationale for launching or scaling a subscription program beyond incremental revenue — e.g. lock-in, data acquisition, brand differentiation.
- **[[Hohenbruck Motoren]]**: Fictional German luxury automaker used as the case vehicle; its SVP of Customer Strategy, Jon Weber, anchors the narrative.
- **[[Value Proposition]]**: The trade-off between higher monthly cost vs. flexibility and bundled services — the core tension the case studies.

## Key Takeaways

- **Premium Pricing Trade-off**: Subscription fee exceeds lease cost; value must be justified by flexibility and bundled benefits.
- **Dual Stakeholder Test**: A program must prove value to both the company (margins, retention) and customers (utility, convenience).
- **Flexibility as Differentiator**: Subscription models compete on optionality, not just price — a distinct value axis from traditional leasing.
- **Business Model Scrutiny**: Growth metrics alone can mask whether a subscription is structurally sound or masking churn.
- **Strategic Intent Matters**: Launching a subscription program may serve goals beyond revenue — brand positioning, data, or customer lock-in.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Jobs To Be Done]]**: Customers don't buy a subscription for a car — they hire it to solve the job of flexible, low-hassle premium mobility; pricing must reflect that job's value, not the asset's cost.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: If the program is measured solely by subscriber count or revenue, teams will optimize for those metrics even when underlying unit economics or customer satisfaction are deteriorating.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central question the Hohenbruck Access case study asks?
**A**: Whether the car subscription program is genuinely delivering value — for both the company and its customers — despite a monthly fee that exceeds a typical lease.

**Q2**: What trade-off defines the Hohenbruck Access value proposition?
**A**: The subscription costs more per month than a lease, but offers greater flexibility and bundled services in return — the case examines whether that premium is justified.

**Q3**: How can a manager apply the case's framework to evaluate their own subscription program?
**A**: Test the program against a dual stakeholder lens: verify that unit economics and retention are sound for the business, and that customers are receiving measurable utility that justifies the premium over conventional alternatives.
