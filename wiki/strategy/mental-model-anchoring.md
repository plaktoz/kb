## Summary

Anchoring is a [[Cognitive Bias]] where an initial numerical value — even an arbitrary one — pulls subsequent estimates toward it, a pattern first documented by [[Amos Tversky]] and [[Daniel Kahneman]]. The underlying mechanism is selective accessibility: anchors make hypothesis-consistent information more mentally available rather than triggering simple insufficient adjustment. Both experts and novices are susceptible, though greater domain knowledge reduces vulnerability to irrelevant anchors.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Anchoring Bias]]**: The tendency to over-rely on the first piece of numerical information encountered when making estimates or decisions.
- **[[Amos Tversky]] and [[Daniel Kahneman]]**: Psychologists who first identified and experimentally documented anchoring as a cognitive phenomenon.
- **Selective Accessibility**: The mechanism explaining why anchoring works — anchors prime anchor-consistent information to become more mentally available.
- **Insufficient Adjustment**: A secondary mechanism where people start from the anchor and adjust, but not far enough to reach an unbiased estimate.
- **[[Investment Psychology]]**: Anchoring causes investors to under-react to new information when prior valuations serve as reference points (per Russell Fuller).

## Key Takeaways

- **Classic Evidence**: Tversky & Kahneman's UN experiment showed wildly divergent estimates based on different arbitrary anchors.
- **Universal Susceptibility**: Anchoring affects both novices and domain experts, though experts are less swayed by irrelevant anchors.
- **Everyday Applications**: House listing prices, credit card minimums, restaurant menus, and first impressions all exploit anchoring.
- **Investment Risk**: Anchoring to prior price levels causes systematic under-reaction to new fundamental information.
- **Root Cause**: Selective accessibility — anchors bias which information comes to mind — not merely lazy thinking.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Availability Bias]]**: Anchors work partly because they flood working memory with anchor-consistent data, making counter-evidence less retrievable — a direct application of availability heuristic to numerical estimation.
- **[[Status Quo Bias]]**: Once an anchor is set, the cognitive cost of moving away from it mirrors status quo bias — the existing reference point carries disproportionate weight regardless of new evidence.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of anchoring as a cognitive bias?
**A**: An initial numerical value — even an arbitrary one — pulls subsequent estimates toward it through selective accessibility and insufficient adjustment away from the starting point.

**Q2**: What mechanism explains why anchoring works, according to the selective accessibility account?
**A**: Anchors make hypothesis-consistent information more mentally available rather than causing people to simply adjust insufficiently; they prime the mind to retrieve confirming evidence preferentially.

**Q3**: How should investors apply knowledge of anchoring to their decision-making?
**A**: Investors should be alert to over-weighting prior price levels or valuations as reference points, since doing so causes systematic under-reaction to new fundamental information that should shift their estimate.
