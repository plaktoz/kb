## Summary

Conventional tough interview questions tend to elicit rehearsed, polished responses rather than genuine candor. The authors propose "curveball" questions — unexpected yet targeted — that are closely tied to what the interviewer actually wants to learn while bypassing prepared talking points. Professionals across fields such as management, journalism, and negotiation can use this approach to surface information the other party may be reluctant to reveal.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Curveball Questions]]**: Questions that are targeted and purposeful but unexpected enough to prevent canned responses.
- **[[Information Asymmetry]]**: The underlying challenge that curveball questions address — one party holds information the other wants but may not volunteer.
- **[[Candor Elicitation]]**: The goal of bypassing rehearsed answers to get genuine, unfiltered responses.
- **[[Interview Strategy]]**: A framework applicable to managers, journalists, negotiators, and any professional seeking honest information.
- **[[Sameer B. Srivastava]]** (UC Berkeley Haas) and **[[Wei Cai]]** (Columbia Business School) — researchers behind the empirical study.

## Key Takeaways

- **Rehearsed Responses Problem**: Standard tough questions prompt polished, prepared answers — not candor.
- **Targeted Surprise**: Curveball questions must be both relevant to the interviewer's goal AND unexpected by the respondent.
- **Broad Applicability**: The technique applies across interviewing, management, journalism, and negotiation.
- **Insight Goal**: The core aim is surfacing information the respondent is reluctant to reveal voluntarily.
- **Research-Backed**: Findings come from academic researchers at Columbia and UC Berkeley.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When candidates know what "tough questions" look like, they optimize for passing them — rehearsed answers become the proxy for genuine transparency, defeating the original purpose of hard questions.
- **[[Pattern Interruption]]**: Unexpected stimuli break automatic, scripted responses and force genuine, in-the-moment thinking — the cognitive mechanism that makes curveball questions work.
