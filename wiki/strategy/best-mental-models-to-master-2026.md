## Summary

[[Mental Models]] — reusable, simplified frameworks for reasoning, tracing back to psychologist [[Kenneth Craik]] and popularized by [[Charlie Munger]]'s cross-disciplinary "latticework" — matter more in 2026 because AI has made information abundant and cheap while good judgment remains scarce and irreplaceable. The article argues experts outperform novices mainly by holding a richer domain-specific set of models, and warns against both superficial model-collecting and "man-with-a-hammer" overapplication of a single favorite model.

## Core Concepts

- [[Mental Models]] — simplified, reusable representations of how something works, deliberately stripped of unnecessary detail.
- [[Kenneth Craik]] — psychologist who proposed in 1943 that the mind builds small-scale internal models of reality to reason and predict.
- [[Charlie Munger]] — popularized the cross-disciplinary "latticework of mental models" approach to judgment.
- [[Latticework of Mental Models]] — the practice of combining frameworks from multiple disciplines rather than relying on one.
- [[Compounding]] — example of a durable model illustrated against a short-lived isolated fact (a stock's quarterly return).
- [[Man-with-a-Hammer Tendency]] — the risk of overapplying one favorite model to situations it doesn't actually fit.

## Key Takeaways

- **AI Shifts the Bottleneck**: judgment about which information matters replaces access to information as the scarce skill.
- **Craik's Origin**: the mind builds internal simplified models of reality to predict and reason (1943).
- **Munger's Latticework**: cross-disciplinary models outperform any single discipline's framework alone.
- **Expert vs. Novice**: experts recognize patterns via richer domain-specific model sets, skipping step-by-step reasoning.
- **Models Outlast Facts**: facts go stale quickly; models like compounding or opportunity cost keep transferring.
- **Watch for Overuse**: "man-with-a-hammer syndrome" forces one favorite model onto every situation.
- **Decision Overload**: models act as filters that narrow abundant options to a reasoned few.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Latticework of Mental Models]]**: The article's central recommendation — combine cross-disciplinary models rather than relying on a single one, drawn directly from Munger's approach.
- **[[Man-with-a-Hammer Tendency]]**: Named explicitly as a common failure mode — forcing a familiar model onto a situation whose details don't actually match its assumptions.
- **[[Compounding]]**: Used as the article's worked example of a model that stays useful indefinitely, unlike an isolated fact such as one quarter's stock return.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why does the article claim mental models matter more in 2026 than before?
**A**: AI has made information generation cheap and instant, but has not increased the quality of judgment needed to evaluate that information — mental models are what let people ask the right follow-up question or catch a flawed premise.

**Q2**: How does the article distinguish mental models from isolated facts?
**A**: Facts are narrow and go stale quickly, while models like opportunity cost or compounding are reusable frameworks that keep applying across business, relationships, and health even as circumstances change.

**Q3**: What mistake does "man-with-a-hammer syndrome" describe, and how should it be avoided?
**A**: Overapplying one familiar model to situations it doesn't actually fit; avoiding it requires holding multiple models simultaneously and checking whether a situation's details match a given model's assumptions before using it.
