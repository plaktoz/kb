## Summary

Sahil Bloom reflects on a lifelong game-of-catch tradition with his father, tracing it from his first throw at age five through his Stanford baseball years and into fatherhood. A family trip forces a collision of time — his aging father beside his young son — crystallizing the insight that impermanence is not a flaw but the source of beauty. The essay closes with Bloom and his father stepping outside with two dusty gloves, collapsing decades in a single act.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Impermanence]]** — the central theme: things are beautiful *because* they end, not in spite of it
- **[[Sahil Bloom]]** — author and investor-philosopher known for translating life lessons into mental models
- **[[Time Perception]]** — time moves invisibly until a moment of contrast (a 70-year-old father seen next to a young grandchild) makes it visible
- **[[Ritual and Repetition]]** — the daily catch routine as a container for relationship-building across seasons and life stages
- **[[Nostalgia and Memory]]** — the gap between the image we hold of someone and who they have become

## Key Takeaways

- **Core thesis**: "The most beautiful things in life are made beautiful by their impermanence."
- **Time illusion**: We hold outdated mental images of loved ones until contrast forces an update.
- **Ritual as relationship**: Repeated small acts (a daily game of catch) accumulate into identity and connection.
- **Ending is not loss**: The shoulder injury that ended baseball also ended the catch routine — until a choice to restart.
- **Small acts, large meaning**: Retrieving dusty gloves is a trivial act that carries decades of weight.
- **Aging loved ones**: "For his age" — the asterisk that appears on cherished mental images as time passes.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Impermanence]]**: Beauty is a function of finitude — sunsets, laughter, silence after rain are moving precisely because they cannot last, a first principle Bloom uses to reframe grief about endings as appreciation for what existed.
- **[[Availability Bias]]**: We default to the most vivid, cached image of a person (a strong athletic father) and unconsciously treat it as current — only sharp contrast breaks the illusion and forces a real update.
