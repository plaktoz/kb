## Summary

[[Roger Martin]], former dean of Rotman School of Management, argues that "strategic planning" is a misnomer — most planning has nothing to do with strategy. Strategy is an integrative set of choices that positions an organization to win on a chosen playing field, whereas planning is merely a comfortable list of activities the organization controls. Leaders default to planning because it avoids the inherent angst of betting on outcomes you cannot control.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Strategy vs. Planning]]**: Strategy requires a coherent theory of winning; planning is a list of activities with no such internal coherence.
- **[[Roger Martin]]**: Strategy thinker and former Rotman dean; distinguishes strategy as an externally-oriented wager on customer behavior versus internally-controlled cost activities.
- **[[Competitive Positioning]]**: Strategy specifies where to play and how to win — not just what to spend.
- **[[Southwest Airlines]]**: Canonical case study of deliberate strategy (point-to-point routes, single aircraft type, no meals, direct booking) beating incumbents who were merely "playing to participate."
- **[[Playing to Win]]**: Martin's framing for organizations that commit to a theory of competitive advantage, contrasted with "playing to play."
- **[[Strategic Logic Testing]]**: Articulating what must be true — about the company, industry, competition, and customers — for a strategy to succeed, enabling ongoing calibration.

## Key Takeaways

- **Strategy requires a theory**: Coherent, doable choices about where to play and how to win.
- **Planning is comfortable**: Leaders control costs; they cannot control customer decisions.
- **Planning guarantees losing**: While you plan, a competitor may be figuring out how to win.
- **Southwest case**: Won by targeting Greyhound substitutes, not competing head-on with major carriers.
- **Incumbents "played to play"**: No theory of beating rivals — just buying planes and gates.
- **Accept angst**: Strategy requires committing to unproven bets; that's leadership, not bad management.
- **Lay out the logic**: Write what must be true for the strategy to work, then watch and tweak.
- **Keep it simple**: A one-page strategy — where to play, how to win, capabilities, systems, aspiration.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Plans Are Not Strategies]]**: Conflating activity lists with strategic choices creates an illusion of direction while leaving the organization vulnerable to competitors who actually choose to win.
- **[[Inversion]]**: Martin implicitly applies inversion — instead of asking "what should we do?", ask "what must be true for us to win?" This surfaces hidden assumptions and makes the theory falsifiable as reality unfolds.
- **[[Commitment Under Uncertainty]]**: Great strategy is irreducibly a bet on uncontrollable outcomes; the willingness to commit to a falsifiable theory is what separates leaders from administrators.
