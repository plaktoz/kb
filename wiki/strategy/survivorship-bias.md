## Summary

Survivorship bias is the logical error of focusing on visible successes while ignoring the far more numerous failures, distorting our sense of probability and causation. We mistake outliers like Bill Gates or the Beatles for templates, when timing, luck, and context heavily shaped their outcomes. The fix is to actively seek out the unseen failures that never made it into the data.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: Overweighting winners while invisible losers skew our mental model of reality.
- **[[Abraham Wald]]**: WWII statistician who recognized that studying undamaged returning planes obscured the critical information held by planes that were shot down.
- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Ignoring the true frequency of failure when constructing beliefs from high-profile success stories.
- **[[Daniel Kahneman]]**: Noted that "a stupid decision that works out well becomes a brilliant decision in hindsight" — selection bias retroactively validates poor reasoning.
- **[[Fooled by Randomness]]** and **[[The Art of Thinking Clearly]]**: Recommended texts that extend this theme.

## Key Takeaways

- **Outliers are not templates**: Bill Gates and the Beatles are exceptions, not rules.
- **Failures are invisible**: Collapsed companies are excluded from startup statistics, inflating apparent success rates.
- **Timing and luck matter**: Success stories often obscure the role of circumstance.
- **Wald's inversion**: Study what you don't see — missing data often holds the most critical insight.
- **Failure narratives are instructive**: Learning only from winners is a distorted curriculum.
- **Actionable tool**: Survivorship bias isn't a reason for paralysis; it enables more realistic decision-making.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: Visible outcomes are a non-random sample — the absence of failure data systematically inflates our estimate of success rates and causes us to attribute luck to skill.
- **[[Inversion]]**: Wald's approach is pure inversion — instead of optimizing from what returned, he asked what the planes that didn't return would tell us, flipping the question to reveal the true signal.
- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Entrepreneurs and investors routinely ignore the underlying frequency of failure because the vivid, available examples they encounter are all survivors — the mind fills in a rosier base rate from a biased sample.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of survivorship bias?
**A**: Survivorship bias is the error of overweighting success stories while ignoring the far more numerous failures, leading us to mistake lucky outliers for replicable templates.

**Q2**: How did Abraham Wald's WWII analysis illustrate the key fix for survivorship bias?
**A**: Wald recognized that the planes returning with damage were the survivors — the planes that never returned held the most critical information, so he recommended reinforcing the areas with no visible damage on returning aircraft.

**Q3**: How should survivorship bias change how we approach learning from success stories?
**A**: We should actively seek out failure narratives alongside success stories, and treat outliers as data points shaped by luck and timing rather than as causal blueprints to replicate.
