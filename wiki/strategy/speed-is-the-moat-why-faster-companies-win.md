## Summary
In markets disrupted by fast replication, a company's rate of learning and shipping — its [[Velocity Moat]] — is a more durable competitive advantage than owned assets like brand, IP, or data, because speed compounds while static assets depreciate. The article argues [[Time to Value]], not shipping speed itself, is the metric that actually determines whether velocity becomes advantage, and that durable speed comes from structural choices like small reversible batches, [[Composable Systems]], and buying undifferentiated foundations rather than building them.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Velocity Moat]]** — advantage from the rate of learning and shipping, which compounds each cycle, versus an accumulation moat of owned assets that depreciates without reinvestment.
- **[[Time to Value]]** — how long from a customer's first contact until they receive a result worth paying for; the metric that actually determines competitive advantage, not launch date or deploy frequency.
- **[[Decision Latency]]** — the time between recognizing a choice and committing to it; fast organizations push authority down and shrink this gap.
- **[[Composable Systems]]** — modular architectures with clean interfaces that let teams change one part without whole-organization coordination.
- **[[Buy Versus Build]]** — the decision to purchase a pre-tested, undifferentiated foundation (auth, billing, deploy pipeline) rather than build it, freeing velocity for differentiating work.
- **[[Foundation Tax]]** — the months a team spends building unglamorous, undifferentiated infrastructure before it can run its first real market experiment.

## Key Takeaways
- **Compounding vs. depreciation**: Speed compounds each cycle; static assets like brand or data depreciate without reinvestment.
- **Time to value beats speed to market**: Launching starts the customer's clock, it doesn't end it.
- **Small batches, reversible decisions**: Cheap-to-reverse changes remove the deliberation tax that slows organizations.
- **[[Composable Systems]] over monoliths**: Modular architecture lets most changes skip cross-team coordination.
- **[[Buy Versus Build]]**: Buying the undifferentiated foundation returns months of time to the part that compounds.
- **Real trade-offs**: Speed amplifies underlying discipline — quality debt, direction risk, and burnout are named costs.
- **Fast companies bet small and often**, affording asymmetric bets that slow companies must avoid.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models
- **[[OODA Loop]]**: The article's ship-measure-learn cycle mirrors Boyd's observe-orient-decide-act loop — the side that cycles through it faster gains a compounding tempo advantage over rivals.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: Time spent building undifferentiated foundation (auth, billing) is time not spent learning from the market, so "can we build this" is the wrong question — "what does building it cost us elsewhere" is the right one.

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about competitive advantage in 2026?
**A**: Velocity — the rate of learning and shipping — is a more durable competitive advantage than accumulated static assets, because speed compounds while assets depreciate.

**Q2**: What metric does the article argue matters more than shipping speed itself?
**A**: Time to value — how quickly a customer reaches a result worth paying for — matters more than launch date or internal deploy frequency, since it's grounded in the customer's actual experience and harder to fake.

**Q3**: How does the "buy versus build" decision relate to speed advantage?
**A**: Buying a pre-tested, undifferentiated foundation frees scarce velocity to be spent on what actually differentiates and compounds, rather than months of unglamorous plumbing work that every competitor could also build.
