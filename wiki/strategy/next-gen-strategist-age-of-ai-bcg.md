## Summary

BCG surveyed 175 chief strategy officers and found that as AI commoditizes traditional analytical skills, four "untamed" challenges are reshaping what strategists need to do well: navigating complexity, reinventing advantage continuously, balancing short- and long-term priorities, and critically evaluating AI-generated recommendations — each calling for a distinct mode of thinking.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Systems Thinking]]**: Shifts the question from "how do we optimize this part?" to "how does the whole system behave?" — needed because contextual factors (geopolitics, technology, climate) now explain almost half the variation in company profitability, yet only 12% of surveyed strategists feel prepared to manage it.
- **[[Counterfactual Thinking]]**: Asks "what would we build if we started from scratch?" — the mindset that let [[Fujifilm]] reinvent itself around healthcare and materials science while [[Kodak]] optimized within its dying film business and eventually failed.
- **[[Integrative Thinking]]**: Reframes "either/or" trade-offs (like short-term performance vs. long-term investment) as "both/and" syntheses — only 2% of companies actually manage this balance well, per BCG's separate research.
- **[[Critical Thinking About AI Output]]**: The discipline of assessing machine-generated reasoning quality, spotting shared blind spots across AI tools, and knowing when human judgment must override an algorithmic recommendation.

## Key Takeaways

- **Complexity gap**: 80% of strategists see managing complexity as core to their work, but many feel underprepared; contextual/macro factors now drive nearly half of profitability variation.
- **Reinvention gap**: 94% call continuous reinvention critical, yet only 12% feel well prepared — illustrated by the Kodak/Fujifilm divergence when digital photography emerged.
- **Trade-off management**: Over 75% say balancing short-term and long-term priorities is a major part of their job, but only half feel confident in their skill here, and only 2% of companies actually execute it well.
- **AI judgment gap**: ~60% say AI now centrally affects their role, but 75% say they need to build their capability to critically evaluate it.
- **Practical routines**: Cross-unit strategy forums, pre-mortems, dual-horizon budgeting, assumption audits, and "red-team" rotations that argue against a recommended course of action before it's approved.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Munger's Latticework]]**: BCG's call to "challenge the mental models organizations use to interpret the world" before major strategic moves directly echoes Munger's cross-disciplinary latticework approach — entrenched, unexamined models are what separated Kodak's failure from Fujifilm's reinvention.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: As AI commoditizes traditional strategic analysis, the strategist's scarce resource shifts from analytical output to selecting the right mode of thinking — systems, counterfactual, integrative, or critical — for each type of "untamed" strategic challenge.

**Q2**: What historical example illustrates the difference between traditional optimization and counterfactual thinking?
**A**: When digital photography emerged, Kodak optimized within its existing film-based model (protecting margins during the digital transition), while Fujifilm asked what its capabilities would be worth in a world without film and reinvented itself around healthcare, materials science, and cosmetics.

**Q3**: What practical routine does BCG recommend for institutionalizing critical thinking about AI-generated strategy recommendations?
**A**: An "assumption audit" before major AI-informed decisions (stress-testing the model's three most-relied-upon hypotheses) and a "red-team rotation" — a small group given 48 hours to build the case against a recommended course of action before it's approved.
