## Summary

The hardest and most valuable product decisions are refusals — choosing not to build something — rather than shipping more features. Two financial-services case studies (a "document hub" and a "notifications centre") illustrate how simple requests balloon into costly internal platforms that serve the organisation more than users. The argument closes with evidence from cognitive science, Gerry McGovern's content-deletion experiment, and Jobs's 1997 product-matrix reset to show that subtraction is systematically undervalued.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Subtraction Bias]]**: A [[Nature]] study shows people are cognitively wired to add rather than remove — explaining why teams default to feature requests over deletion or refusal.
- **[[Platform Creep]]**: Incremental stakeholder requests for "simple" features (document hubs, notifications centres) compound into full platform commitments that serve internal coordination more than end users.
- **[[Total Cost of Ownership]]**: Showing stakeholders multi-year running costs — not just build costs — reframes platform requests by surfacing the true burden of saying yes.
- **[[Jobs 1997 Product Matrix]]**: [[Steve Jobs]] cancelled all but four products on return to Apple not as an endpoint, but as a focusing mechanism to concentrate resources and eliminate distraction.
- **[[Gerry McGovern]]**: Content strategist whose research found removing 80–90% of website content improved sales and reduced support load — a direct empirical case for aggressive pruning.
- **[[AI Agents as Pruning Tools]]**: Rather than deploying agents to generate yet more features, the author argues they are better suited to pruning and maintenance — inverting the dominant "endless execution" narrative.

## Key Takeaways

- **Refusing to build** is often the highest-leverage product decision.
- **Ballpark running costs over years**, not just build cost, to reframe stakeholder requests.
- **Address communication needs individually**, not with one-size-fits-all platforms.
- **Ask "does it make the boat go faster?"** before committing to any new capability.
- **Remove 80–90% of content** can improve sales and reduce support (McGovern finding).
- **Cognitive bias toward addition** means subtraction requires deliberate, active effort.
- **AI agents** should prune and maintain, not endlessly generate new features.
- **Jobs's product matrix**: fewer products, deeper focus — use as a periodic reset, not a permanent constraint.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: Every feature you say yes to consumes capacity that cannot be used for higher-value work — the real cost of building something is the better thing you didn't build.
- **[[Subtraction Bias]]**: Humans systematically overlook removal as a solution; naming this bias is the first step to counteracting it in product reviews.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When "shipping features" becomes the proxy for product progress, teams optimise for output volume rather than user outcomes — the document-hub and notifications-centre cases illustrate this exactly.
