## Summary

The [[Circle of Competence]] is a mental model describing the domain of earned, deep knowledge where you hold a genuine advantage over others. The critical insight is that the boundary of your circle matters far more than its size — operating inside it yields an edge, while straying beyond it transfers that edge to better-informed competitors. [[Warren Buffett]] and [[Charlie Munger]] used this framework to drive Berkshire Hathaway's disciplined avoidance of industries where they lacked a real understanding.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Circle of Competence]]**: The bounded area of knowledge where your depth gives you a decision-making advantage.
- **Confidence vs. Competence gap**: Confidence erodes more slowly than competence when moving outside your circle, creating a dangerous blind spot.
- **[[Charlie Munger]]**: Applied the circle explicitly to Berkshire's investment discipline — avoided pharmaceuticals because "we've got no edge" in biology or chemistry.
- **[[Warren Buffett]]**: Cited [[Rose Blumkin]] as an exemplar — she built Nebraska's largest furniture store by staying tightly within her domain of furniture, cash, and real estate.
- **Known unknowns vs. Unknown unknowns**: The biggest risk is not what you know you don't know, but what is invisible to you entirely.

## Key Takeaways

- **Boundary awareness**: Knowing the edge of your circle is more valuable than expanding it.
- **Advantage transfer**: Cross the perimeter and your edge moves to better-informed rivals.
- **Confidence lag**: Confidence doesn't shrink as fast as competence outside your circle.
- **Narrow can be a feature**: Rose Blumkin's refusal to stray was a deliberate strength.
- **Say "I don't know"**: Intellectual honesty at the boundary prevents costly overreach.
- **Gradual expansion**: Grow the circle honestly and incrementally, not by wishful thinking.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Circle of Competence]]**: Operating only where you have earned knowledge prevents the confidence-competence gap from becoming a fatal blind spot — the model forces explicit acknowledgment of what you don't know.
- **[[Dunning-Kruger Effect]]**: The article's warning that confidence erodes slower than competence is precisely the mechanism Dunning-Kruger describes — incompetence in a new domain is invisible to the person entering it.
- **[[Margin of Safety]]**: Staying well inside your circle (rather than right at the edge) creates a cognitive margin of safety, reducing the probability of an unseen mistake.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the Circle of Competence mental model?
**A**: Your advantage as a thinker or investor comes from operating within your domain of earned, deep knowledge — and the boundary of that circle matters more than its size.

**Q2**: Why is the confidence-competence gap especially dangerous near the edge of your circle?
**A**: Confidence does not shrink as quickly as actual competence when you move outside your circle, meaning you can feel equally capable while your real edge has already disappeared.

**Q3**: How can you apply the Circle of Competence to improve decision-making in practice?
**A**: Identify the areas where you have genuine earned knowledge, operate well inside them, expand the circle gradually and honestly, and freely say "I don't know" when approaching the boundary.
