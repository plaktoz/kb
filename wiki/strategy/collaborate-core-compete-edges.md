## Summary

Frank Nagle argues that rival companies should collaborate on shared foundational infrastructure ("the core") while competing independently on differentiated offerings ("the edges"). Using the Tour de France peloton as a metaphor, he shows that rivals who share effort on common ground move faster collectively than any could alone. Nagle developed this framework advising the [[Linux Foundation]], where rival tech firms routinely co-invest in open-source infrastructure while fiercely competing on products built atop it.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Coopetition]]** — strategic cooperation between competitors on shared foundational layers
- **Core vs. Edges** — the distinction between commoditized shared infrastructure (core) and proprietary differentiated offerings (edges)
- **[[Open Source]] as Shared Core** — open-source projects as the canonical example of core collaboration among rivals
- **[[Linux Foundation]]** — institutional vehicle enabling rivals to co-invest in shared infrastructure
- **Peloton Dynamic** — rival teams in cycling temporarily cooperate on wind resistance while each pursues individual competitive goals; a metaphor for strategic co-investment

## Key Takeaways

- **Core collaboration**: rivals co-invest in shared infrastructure that no single firm profits from uniquely.
- **Edge competition**: each firm competes fiercely on proprietary layers built atop the shared core.
- **Peloton metaphor**: the group moves faster than any solo rider — collective efficiency enables individual wins.
- **Linux Foundation precedent**: rival tech companies routinely fund shared open-source infrastructure together.
- **Strategic error — isolation**: companies that refuse to collaborate on the core sacrifice shared efficiency gains.
- **Strategic error — over-collaboration**: companies that collaborate too broadly give up proprietary advantage.
- **Key skill**: knowing *what* belongs in the core vs. *what* belongs at the edges.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Comparative Advantage]]**: each firm contributes to shared infrastructure where the marginal cost of exclusion is high, then redirects freed capacity to differentiated strengths — the same logic that drives trade between nations.
- **[[Game Theory]] — Coordination Game]]**: the peloton only forms when rivals trust that others will rotate effort; breakdown of that trust collapses the collective gain back to zero-sum competition across every layer.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central strategic prescription of Nagle's "collaborate on the core, compete on the edges" framework?
**A**: Rival companies should jointly invest in shared foundational infrastructure where no single firm gains unique advantage, while competing independently on the differentiated products and services built atop that infrastructure.

**Q2**: What real-world institution does Nagle cite as evidence this framework works in practice?
**A**: The Linux Foundation, where rival tech companies routinely co-invest in shared open-source infrastructure while fiercely competing on the proprietary products they build on top of it.

**Q3**: How does the Tour de France peloton illustrate the strategic logic of core collaboration?
**A**: Rival cycling teams temporarily ride together to share wind resistance benefits, allowing the collective to move faster than any individual could alone — the same principle applies when competing firms share the effort of building common infrastructure rather than each building it separately.
