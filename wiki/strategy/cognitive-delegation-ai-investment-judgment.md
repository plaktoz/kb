## Summary

CFA Institute researchers warn that AI adoption in investment management risks "cognitive delegation" — professionals relying on AI-generated outputs before building their own robust understanding of the underlying analysis — which erodes the tacit judgment historically built through direct experience with uncertainty, and complicates accountability when AI and human contributions blur together.

## Core Concepts

**[[Cognitive Delegation]]** describes outsourcing analytical thinking to AI before internalizing the reasoning — distinct from simple efficiency gains, because it compresses or bypasses the friction through which professionals traditionally build **[[Tacit Knowledge]]** and defensible judgment. This connects to **[[Accountability Gap|Accountability]]** concerns: legal and regulatory frameworks hold humans and firms responsible for outcomes, but as AI's role grows, disentangling human versus AI contribution complicates both governance and performance attribution.

## Key Takeaways

- **Not limited to juniors**: Even experienced professionals' internal mental models can erode as AI-assisted output becomes normalized.
- **Live-discussion failure mode**: The gap in understanding shows up most clearly when professionals must defend assumptions or revise conclusions in real time.
- **Governance requirement**: Firms need clear decision rights, documented analytical processes, and audit mechanisms to avoid "black-box dependency."
- **Attribution erosion**: As human/AI contributions blur, compensation, promotion, and talent evaluation become harder to ground in demonstrated skill.
- **Competitive reframe**: As AI commoditizes information access, competitive advantage shifts to preserved judgment and accountability, not data or compute access.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Skin in the Game]]**: The accountability problem the authors describe is essentially a misalignment between who bears the consequences of a decision (the human/firm) and who did the cognitive work behind it (increasingly, the AI) — a classic setup for principal-agent-style failure if not governed deliberately.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Growing reliance on AI in investment analysis risks eroding investors' own judgment and mental models, while also blurring accountability for the decisions that result.

**Q2**: What specific failure mode illustrates the risk of cognitive delegation for junior analysts?
**A**: Entry-level analysts can now produce sophisticated financial models and theses using AI without internalizing the underlying frameworks, which becomes visible when they can't defend assumptions or revise conclusions in live discussions.

**Q3**: What governance response do the authors recommend for investment firms?
**A**: Treating AI as a tool operating within a well-defined architecture of human oversight — with clear decision rights, transparent documentation of analytical processes, and robust audit mechanisms — rather than as an autonomous decision-maker.
