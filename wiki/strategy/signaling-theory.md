## Summary

Signaling is how organisms and humans communicate qualities about themselves through costly actions rather than words — because talk is cheap, believable signals require real expenditure of time, energy, or resources. The concept spans biology (peacock tails, gazelle stotting) through economics ([[Information Asymmetry]], [[Veblen Goods]]) to workplace behavior. Honest signals are hard to fake; easy-to-fake signals quickly lose credibility.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Signaling Theory]]**: Costly actions serve as credible proxies for underlying qualities that cannot be directly observed.
- **[[Countersignaling]]**: Signaling by *not* signaling — established high-status individuals (billionaire eating fast food, CEO in a hoodie) can skip conventional status displays because their position is already known.
- **[[Honest vs Dishonest Signaling]]**: Genuine signals are difficult or expensive to counterfeit; experience is cited as one of the hardest qualities to fake.
- **[[Biological Signaling]]**: Peacock tails (costly handicap demonstrating fitness), gazelle stotting (signaling strength to predators), and aposematism (bright warning colors signaling danger) are natural analogs.
- **[[Akerlof Market for Lemons]]**: Poor signaling degrades entire markets — when buyers cannot distinguish quality, good products are driven out alongside bad ones.
- **[[Screening]]**: The mechanism by which uninformed parties prompt informed ones to reveal hidden information; the counterpart to signaling.
- **[[Conspicuous Consumption]]**: Identified by [[Thorstein Veblen]] — purchases signal status beyond utility. [[Veblen Goods]] perversely gain desirability as price rises.
- **[[Workplace Signaling]]**: Open offices, packed calendars, and physical presence signal productivity while often undermining actual output.

## Key Takeaways

- **Costly signals are credible**: Only actions requiring real sacrifice reliably communicate underlying quality.
- **Countersignaling**: Overeager status displays can backfire once reputation is established.
- **Tyler Cowen's insight**: Sharing good news too eagerly can make a person look bad.
- **Market degradation**: Akerlof showed weak signaling collapses entire markets, not just individual deals.
- **Veblen Goods**: Higher price increases demand — status signal dominates utility.
- **Workplace theater**: Visible busyness signals productivity but often destroys it.
- **Experience as signal**: Hard to counterfeit, making it one of the most durable signals.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Handicap Principle]]**: Zahavi's insight — only costly, hard-to-fake signals are evolutionarily stable, because cheap signals invite cheating and erode trust across the system.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When a signal becomes a target (e.g., packed calendar = productivity), people optimize for the signal rather than the underlying quality it was meant to proxy.
- **[[Information Asymmetry]]**: The root cause of all signaling problems — when one party knows more than the other, costly signals emerge as equilibrium mechanisms to bridge the gap.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of signaling theory?
**A**: Honest communication of unobservable qualities requires costly signals that are too expensive for those without the underlying quality to fake.

**Q2**: What is countersignaling and why does it work?
**A**: Countersignaling is forgoing conventional status displays; it works because only those whose status is already well-established can afford to skip the performance without being misread.

**Q3**: How does poor signaling degrade markets, and what is the implication for institutions?
**A**: Akerlof's "Market for Lemons" shows that when buyers cannot distinguish quality, adverse selection drives good products out — implying that any institution or market needs credible screening or signaling mechanisms to remain functional.
