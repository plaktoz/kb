## Summary

Organizations reflexively punish repeated mistakes, but this habit misunderstands failure — conditions are never truly identical, so no mistake is ever truly repeated. Drawing on [[Heraclitus]]'s insight that you cannot step in the same river twice, the author argues that true learning requires reconstructing the decision context at the time, not just cataloguing outcomes. Evolution's "blind" strategy of repeating mutations without memory of failure is actually adaptive, because a trait that failed before may succeed in a changed environment.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Evolution]] as a model for failure tolerance** — natural selection works *because* evolution has no memory; it blindly retries mutations that may now succeed in altered conditions.
- **Two types of failure** — [[Gary Klein]] (*Seeing What Others Don't*) and the author distinguish between ignorance-based failure (knowledge doesn't exist yet) and ineptitude-based failure (knowledge exists but isn't applied).
- **[[Heraclitus]] and changing conditions** — both actor and environment are always in flux, making true repetition of a mistake impossible.
- **Organizational learning traps** — managers over-index on error reduction vs. insight generation, punishing repeated mistakes without accounting for changed variables.
- **Contextual decision reconstruction** — understanding a failure requires knowing the key variables, decision process, and available knowledge *at the time*, not just the outcome.

## Key Takeaways

- **Blind repetition wins**: Evolution retries failed mutations because environments change — blindness is an adaptive feature.
- **Two failure types**: Ignorance-based (forgivable) vs. ineptitude-based (harder to excuse).
- **No true repeats**: Circumstances always differ; "same mistake twice" is a category error.
- **Context over outcome**: Judging decisions requires reconstructing the knowledge available at decision time.
- **Error-reduction bias**: Organizations spend more energy preventing errors than generating insights ([[Gary Klein]]).

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Heraclitus's River]]**: Conditions never repeat exactly — the same action in a new environment is a new experiment, not a recurrence. This explains why punishing "repeated" mistakes often misfires.
- **[[Hindsight Bias]]**: Evaluating past decisions with knowledge gained afterward makes failures look more avoidable than they were, distorting organizational learning.
- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: Asking not just "what went wrong?" but "what did we know at the time, and what has changed since?" turns failure analysis into durable insight rather than blame.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument of the article regarding repeated mistakes?
**A**: Because circumstances constantly change, no mistake is ever truly repeated — punishing "repeated" failures misunderstands how conditions and actors both evolve.

**Q2**: How does evolution's blindness serve as an adaptive advantage?
**A**: Evolution retries mutations without memory of prior failures; a trait that failed in one environment may succeed in a changed one, so the lack of memory is a feature, not a bug.

**Q3**: How should organizations apply this insight when analyzing failures?
**A**: Rather than cataloguing what went wrong, they should reconstruct the key variables, decision process, and knowledge available at the time of the failure to extract genuine learning.
