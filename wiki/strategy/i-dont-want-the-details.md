## Summary

After an incident, an SVP cut off a detailed explanation saying "I don't want the details — I want to know what we're changing," which initially seemed dismissive but was actually a statement of trust in competence and a redirect toward systemic improvement. The article argues that compelling explanations of *why* things went wrong can paradoxically reduce urgency to fix anything, because once behavior is deemed reasonable, nothing changes. The better question is not what caused the failure, but what structural change would prevent it if the same people weren't around.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Post-Incident Review]]** — organizational practice of analyzing failures; the author argues focus should shift from explanation to corrective action
- **[[Systems Thinking]]** — treating failures as structural outputs rather than individual mistakes
- **[[Blameless Culture]]** — assuming reasonable people produced the outcome, then asking what the system needs to change
- **[[Michael Heap]]** — engineering leader writing on incident management and organizational design
- **Explanation vs. Fix** — understanding why something happened is distinct from preventing recurrence
- **Structural problems** — ownership gaps, changing requirements, and alert fatigue as root-cause categories
- **Corrective action test** — "If everyone involved left tomorrow, would the fix still work?"

## Key Takeaways

- **Explanation ≠ Fix**: understanding an incident doesn't prevent recurrence.
- **Risk of closure**: a compelling explanation reduces urgency; agreed-upon reasonableness kills action.
- **Better question**: "Given reasonable people produced this, what needs to change?"
- **Fix systems, not people**: structural issues (ownership gaps, alert fatigue) need structural solutions.
- **Durability test**: would the fix survive full team turnover?
- **Deliberate risk acceptance**: not every failure needs a new process — but inaction must be conscious, not rationalized.
- **Vague commitments fail**: "communicate better" is a hope, not a fix.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When post-mortems optimize for explaining behavior as reasonable, the metric (blamelessness) becomes the goal — at the expense of actual system change.
- **[[Systems Thinking]]**: Failures are emergent outputs of a system; treating them as individual errors ignores the structural inputs that will reproduce the same outcome with different people.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core argument against focusing on detailed explanations after an incident?
**A**: A compelling explanation can reduce urgency to fix anything — once behavior is deemed reasonable, organizations stop changing the system that produced the failure.

**Q2**: What test does the author recommend for evaluating corrective actions?
**A**: "If everyone involved left tomorrow, would the fix still work?" — this checks whether the change is structural rather than person-dependent.

**Q3**: How should organizations apply this principle to recurring failures?
**A**: Ask "If the same situation happened tomorrow, what would cause a different outcome?" — and treat vague commitments like "communicate better" as hopes, not valid fixes.
