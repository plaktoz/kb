## Summary

The authors argue that whether a firm builds, buys, or partners for AI foundation models is mainly a cost-benefit choice shaped by data, speed of innovation, and customization needs, not a bid to avoid competing. Because AI has weaker network effects, fast leapfrogging, and nonrival data, blanket skepticism toward AI acquisitions and partnerships could reduce entry rather than increase it. They still flag foreclosure, lock-in, and concentration risks and suggest open-source models, interoperability, and non-exclusive deals as guardrails.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Build vs. Buy]]**: Choice between in-house development, acquisition, or partnership for AI capabilities.
- **[[AI Value Stack]]**: Foundation models and applications built on them, with hardware, compute, and data as upstream inputs.
- **[[Nonrival Data]]**: Open, public, or synthetic data reusable across models; shows increasing returns to scale.
- **[[Proprietary Data]]**: Financial and clinical data retain value and favor building (e.g. [[JPMorgan Chase]]).
- **[[Vendor Lock-In]]**: Dependence on a provider limits switching and bargaining power.
- **[[Killer Acquisitions]]**: Incumbents buying nascent rivals to stop future competition.
- **[[Hybrid AI Strategy]]**: Build for core use cases, partner for non-core ([[Roche]] and Genentech).
- **[[Multi-Homing]]**: Using several AI vendors at once to test capabilities and limit foreclosure.
- **[[Open-Source AI Models]]**: Closing the performance gap with closed models; a potential guardrail.
- **[[2023 US Merger Guidelines]]**: Say internal growth into concentrated markets benefits competition.

## Key Takeaways

- **Divergent Paths**: Tesla built, Toyota partnered with Waymo, and GM acquired Cruise.
- **Data Decides**: Fast-decaying behavior data favors fresh, in-house models; regulated proprietary data favors building.
- **Build Costs**: Large upfront compute, data, and talent spend with uncertain short-run returns.
- **Buy Benefits**: Faster time to market, shifted safety liability, and multi-homing flexibility.
- **Lock-In Risk**: Rapid leapfrogging may counter it, but integration costs can still bind customers.
- **Replacement Effect Doubt**: Blocking deals may not trigger de novo entry in AI.
- **Weaker Tipping Forces**: Network effects and data feedback loops are weaker than in Web 2.0.
- **Guardrails**: Open-source models, data interoperability, and non-exclusive partnerships.

## 🧠 Core Principles

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim about build versus buy in AI?
**A**: The choice is mainly a cost-benefit decision driven by data, speed, and customization needs, so forgoing in-house development is not necessarily an anticompetitive motive. Stringent policy against AI partnerships may therefore have unintended consequences.

**Q2**: Why does the type of data influence the decision?
**A**: Behavior data on streaming or social platforms decays quickly and can be collected by newcomers, while proprietary financial or clinical data keeps its value and is hard to share, favoring building. Nonrival open data lowers barriers and can be reused across models.

**Q3**: What guardrails do the authors suggest for regulators?
**A**: They recommend monitoring the sector and promoting open-source models, data interoperability, and non-exclusive partnerships, which counter foreclosure and concentration while preserving innovation incentives.
