## Summary
Crypto businesses are rarely regulated as one activity, so boards should map the transaction end to end and test each function against each jurisdiction's regulatory perimeter. A licence is not itself an advantage; the advantage lies in an operating model and entity structure that supports strategy, preserves optionality and satisfies banks and counterparties. Hong Kong is used as a running illustration.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Transaction-First Approach]]**: map the transaction, customer asset, regulated activities, jurisdictions, responsible entities and counterparty controls.
- **[[Activity-Based Regulation]]**: outcomes follow what a business does, not what it calls itself.
- **[[Crypto Custody]]**: custody, debtor-creditor and security arrangements determine who bears loss in [[Insolvency]].
- **[[Own, Partner or Restrict]]**: the choice for each regulated capability.
- **[[Regulatory Optionality]]**: structures should leave room for the next product, market or investor stage.
- **[[Regulatory Debt]]**: launch structures that become costly to unwind.
- **[[Counterparty Risk]] and bankability**: banks, custodians and card schemes may demand more than legal minimums.
- **[[Stablecoins]]**, [[Reverse Solicitation]] and [[Hong Kong Virtual Asset Regulation]].

## Key Takeaways
- **Substance Over Label**: Classification turns on activities performed, not product names like "wallet".
- **Licence Is Not Advantage**: Advantage comes from the operating model a licence enables.
- **Ownership Matters**: Custody versus debtor-creditor versus security decides loss allocation on failure.
- **Footprint Control**: A global website does not create a global licence; reverse solicitation is narrow.
- **Own, Partner, Restrict**: Sequence licensing as a roadmap, not a one-off exercise.
- **Outsourcing Limits**: A licensed partner does not resolve your own regulatory position.
- **Three Tests**: Legally permissible, regulator-acceptable, counterparty-supportable.
- **Avoid Arbitrage**: Regulatory credibility outweighs short-term lightest-touch jurisdictions.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Regulatory consequences follow function, not label** — Classification depends on what activities are actually performed and who controls assets, not on commercial names or technology choices such as MPC.
→ explains: Substance Over Label, Ownership Matters, Outsourcing Limits

**T2. Structure should preserve optionality** — Because strategy changes faster than licence architecture, a regulatory structure should fit current needs while leaving credible routes to future products and markets, avoiding accumulated regulatory debt. · *model:* [[Optionality]]
→ explains: Licence Is Not Advantage, Own, Partner, Restrict, Avoid Arbitrage

**T3. Dependence on few critical counterparties is fragility** — A legally permissible model is commercially fragile if it relies on a small number of banks or partners whose risk appetite can change; design to the most demanding counterparty and keep backups. · *model:* [[Concentration Risk]]
→ explains: Three Tests, Outsourcing Limits

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument of the framework?
**A**: Regulatory strategy cannot be separated from business strategy: a licence is not an advantage in itself, but a coherent structure built from a transaction-first analysis widens products and markets and strengthens counterparty relationships.

**Q2**: Why does it matter whether customer crypto is held in custody, as a debtor-creditor claim, or under security?
**A**: The classification determines licensing as well as who bears loss if the platform or custodian fails, whether assets can be reused, how collateral is enforced and what the customer recovers in insolvency.

**Q3**: How should a board decide which regulated capabilities to take on?
**A**: For each capability choose Own, Partner or Restrict, treat licensing as a roadmap revisited as the business scales, and test the structure against the three-year plan and the board's ten questions.
