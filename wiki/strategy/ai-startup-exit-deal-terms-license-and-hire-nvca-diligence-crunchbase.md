## Summary

[[Aaron Rubin]] and [[Greg Volkmar]] argue that AI startups, moving fast on rich valuations, set financing and compute, data and distribution contract terms early and in a hurry, and some will later slow a sale, add cost and erode value. Investor approval rights, data and code provenance, and inherited compute commitments all surface in buyer diligence. Founders should know what each signature gives up so speed does not cost flexibility at exit.

## Core Concepts

- [[License-and-Hire Deals]]: Buyer licenses technology and hires the team instead of acquiring the company.
- [[National Venture Capital Association]]: Its model charter now requires a preferred-stockholder vote on such deals.
- [[Strategic Investors]]: [[Nvidia]] and NVentures invested in more than 260 startups since 2024.
- [[Right of First Negotiation]]: Can force a founder to sideline competing bidders.
- [[Due Diligence]]: Buyers probe training-data rights, AI-written code and compute contracts.
- [[Change of Control]]: Termination and consent terms in commercial contracts.
- [[Indemnity and Escrow]]: Tools buyers use when data rights are unclear.
- [[Compute Commitments]]: Minimum-spend, reserved-capacity and exclusivity terms stay with the company.
- [[Optionality]]: Early terms narrow the options available at exit.

## Key Takeaways

- **Valuation Gap**: AI companies raised at up to 2.5x non-AI median pre-money valuations.
- **Investor Say**: November 2026 NVCA update moves approval to a preferred-stock vote.
- **Data Rights**: Permission to serve a customer may not cover training for everyone.
- **Code Scrutiny**: Acquirers ask how much code is AI-written and whether it pulled copyleft.
- **Inherited Burden**: A buyer with its own GPUs will discount unwanted reserved capacity.
- **Negotiating Capital**: Spend leverage on a few provisions that matter most in a sale.
- **Five Actions**: Map decision rights, track data and code, stress-test infrastructure, keep strategic rights narrow, read contracts for exit.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Early commitments constrain later choices** — Terms signed quickly at the start become fixed constraints at exit, and are cheaper to fix at the negotiating table than during a buyer's diligence. · *model:* [[Optionality]]
→ explains: Investor Say, Inherited Burden, Five Actions

**T2. Leverage is finite and should be concentrated** — What you can win depends on how much investors want your deal, so spend it on few provisions that matter most.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core claim?
**A**: Terms in AI startups' financings and compute, data and distribution contracts are set early and hurriedly, yet they later shape how smoothly and at what value the company can be sold.

**Q2**: How do compute contracts affect an acquisition?
**A**: Minimum-spend commitments, reserved capacity and exclusivity terms generally stay with the company, and a buyer with its own GPUs will factor the remaining payments into its offer.

**Q3**: What should founders do now to protect exit flexibility?
**A**: Map who approves a sale, track data and code rights, stress-test infrastructure commitments, keep strategic rights narrow, and read commercial contracts for change-of-control and termination terms.
