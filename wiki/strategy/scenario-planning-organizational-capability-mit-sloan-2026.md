## Summary
MIT Sloan Winter 2026 proposes a 3-phase scenario planning model (development → integration → embedding) supported by three organizational capabilities — adaptive strategies, external monitoring, and learning culture — arguing that scenario planning fails not because of poor scenario quality but because organizations lack the connective tissue to translate insights into decision architecture and capital allocation.

## Core Concepts
- **[[3-Phase Scenario Planning Model]]**: (1) Development — identifying key uncertainties, building narratives, stress-testing existing strategies; (2) Integration — linking scenarios to strategic planning cycles, decision-making, and capital allocation; (3) Embedding — making scenario thinking a continuous organizational capability, not a one-off exercise.
- **[[Adaptive Strategies]]**: Pre-planned responses to different scenario outcomes — real options, contingency plans, and flexible commitments rather than rigid forecasts. The goal is to have decision rules ready before scenarios materialize, not to predict which one will.
- **[[External Monitoring / Strategic Radars]]**: Systematic scanning of weak signals indicating which scenario is unfolding. Requires "strategic radars" distributed across the organization, not centralized in a strategy team. Linked to agile management — real-time data updates scenario probability assessments.
- **[[Learning Culture for Scenario Planning]]**: Psychological safety to challenge assumptions; willingness to revisit decisions as new information emerges; shared vocabulary for discussing uncertainty. Without this, scenario exercises become rituals rather than genuine planning tools.
- **[[Shell Scenario Planning Gold Standard]]**: Royal Dutch/Shell's practice — scenarios integrated into planning cycles and used to question assumptions rather than confirm existing strategies — remains the benchmark for organizational scenario planning.
- **[[Scenario Planning Failure Modes]]**: Outputs siloed in strategy teams; scenarios not linked to capital allocation; scenarios treated as predictions to bet on rather than possibilities to prepare for; exercise becomes a ritual without updating.

## Key Takeaways
- **3 phases**: development → integration → embedding.
- **3 capabilities (connective tissue)**: adaptive strategies, external monitoring (strategic radars), learning culture.
- **Primary failure mode**: scenario insights never reach decision-makers or capital allocation — silo problem.
- **Shell gold standard**: scenarios used to question assumptions, not confirm them; integrated into governance cycles.
- **Practical integration**: link to existing governance (budget cycles, board reviews); never run as standalone activity.
- **Regular discipline**: leaders ask "which scenario is this data consistent with?" as a routine question.
- **Update cadence**: review and update scenarios at least annually or after major external shocks.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Prepared Mind Principle]]**: Louis Pasteur's "Chance favors the prepared mind" — the core argument for scenario planning. Organizations that have stress-tested strategies against multiple futures are prepared to act decisively when one materializes; unprepared organizations freeze or over-react.
- **[[Decision Architecture]]**: Scenario planning creates the most value when pre-linked to explicit decision rules (trigger conditions, pre-planned responses) — otherwise it produces insight without action, which is worse than no insight because it creates false confidence.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: Why do most scenario planning exercises fail to influence strategic decisions?
**A**: Four failure modes: (1) outputs remain siloed in strategy teams; (2) scenarios not linked to capital allocation or investment decisions; (3) leaders treat scenarios as predictions to bet on rather than possibilities to prepare for; (4) the exercise becomes a ritual without updating as the environment changes.

**Q2**: What are the three organizational capabilities the MIT Sloan framework identifies as the "connective tissue" of scenario planning?
**A**: (1) Adaptive strategies — pre-planned responses and real options for different scenario outcomes; (2) External monitoring — systematic weak-signal scanning with distributed "strategic radars"; (3) Learning culture — psychological safety to challenge assumptions and shared vocabulary for discussing uncertainty.

**Q3**: How should organizations link scenario planning to day-to-day operations?
**A**: Connect it to existing governance processes (budget cycles, board reviews); train leaders to regularly ask "which scenario is this data consistent with?"; build monitoring dashboards with leading indicators for each scenario; review and update scenarios annually or after major external shocks.
