## Summary
A roundup of 10 mental models for decision-making, ranging from mathematically grounded tools like [[Bayes' Theorem]] to established frameworks already covered in this vault like [[Margin of Safety]], [[Confirmation Bias]], and [[Hanlon's Razor]] — framed around Charlie Munger's idea that facts only make sense once seen through a "latticework" of multiple models applied together.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Bayes' Theorem]]**: A mathematical framework for updating the probability of an event given new evidence and historical data, offered as an alternative to relying on unstructured assumption.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: The value of the best alternative given up when choosing one option over another; makes trade-offs explicit rather than implicit.
- **[[Backward Chaining]]**: Planning by starting from the end goal and working the steps backward, which keeps focus on the outcome rather than getting lost in forward-looking detail.
- **[[Preferential Attachment]]**: The tendency for resources to concentrate further on whatever is already succeeding — the article frames it as a counterweight to the [[Pareto Principle]], recommending balance between doubling down on winners and still testing other opportunities.
- Also covered: [[Redundancy]], the [[Pareto Principle]], law of diminishing returns, [[Margin of Safety]], [[Confirmation Bias]], and [[Hanlon's Razor]] — see their dedicated notes for depth.

## Key Takeaways
- **Munger's "latticework of theory"**: no single mental model is sufficient; facts only cohere when multiple models are applied together.
- **Bayes' Theorem replaces assumption with structured probability**: using historical/situational data rather than gut-feel to estimate an outcome's likelihood.
- **Opportunity cost analysis makes abandoned alternatives visible**, enabling unbiased comparison between choices.
- **Preferential attachment and the Pareto principle can pull in opposite directions**: over-committing to current winners can mean missing the next one — deliberate testing of alternatives is the counterbalance.
- **Negative models exist too**: confirmation bias is presented as a mental model to actively guard against, not adopt.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's core framing device for why mental models matter?
**A**: Charlie Munger's idea that facts only "hang together" once seen through a latticework of multiple theories — a single mental model is rarely sufficient for good decisions.

**Q2**: How does Bayes' Theorem improve on typical decision-making?
**A**: It replaces assumption-based probability estimates with a structured mathematical approach that incorporates relevant historical data and situational factors.

**Q3**: How might preferential attachment and the Pareto principle create tension in resource allocation?
**A**: The Pareto principle says to focus resources on the 20% of efforts driving 80% of results, but preferential attachment shows that over-concentrating resources on current winners can starve untested opportunities of a fair chance — the article recommends balancing both rather than fully committing to either.
