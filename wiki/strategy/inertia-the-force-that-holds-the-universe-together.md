## Summary

Inertia — resistance to change in motion — is a fundamental physical law that also governs human cognition and behavior. Cognitive inertia keeps us anchored to existing beliefs and habits even when they no longer serve us, manifesting as status quo bias and confirmation bias. Overcoming inertia requires only an initial push of activation energy; once momentum begins, progress becomes self-sustaining.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Inertia]]** — Newton's first law: objects (and minds) resist changes to their current state unless acted on by an external force
- **[[Cognitive Inertia]]** — the tendency to cling to existing ideas and habits even when disconfirmatory evidence emerges
- **[[Status Quo Bias]]** — preferring the default option to avoid the mental effort of change; closely related to [[Loss Aversion]]
- **[[Confirmation Bias]]** — seeking information that validates existing beliefs rather than updating in light of new evidence
- **[[Activation Energy]]** — the initial force required to overcome inertia and set a new behavior in motion
- **[[Johannes Kepler]]** — coined the term "inertia" from the Latin for idleness/inactivity
- **[[Ernest Hemingway]]** — used deliberate momentum-building (stopping mid-thought each day) to overcome writing inertia
- **[[Robert Nozick]]** — proposed the "experience machine" thought experiment illustrating status quo bias

## Key Takeaways

- **Physical foundation**: Without inertia, the universe would collapse to a single clump of matter.
- **Cognitive parallel**: Minds resist updating beliefs just as objects resist changes in motion.
- **Status quo bias**: People choose defaults not because they're optimal but because change costs effort.
- **Experience machine flip**: Opposition to the machine is opposition to leaving the status quo, not the machine itself.
- **Hemingway's trick**: Stop each session with momentum intact so the next session starts in motion.
- **One true sentence**: Writing a single honest sentence is enough activation energy to sustain flow.
- **Switching costs**: High switching costs (e.g., changing banks) reinforce inertia even when better options exist.
- **Initial push rule**: Only the first step is hard — momentum compounds after that.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Status Quo Bias]]**: Departing from the current state feels like a loss even when the alternative is objectively better — the De Brigard experiment shows people resist leaving a simulation they're already in far more than they resist entering one.
- **[[Activation Energy]]**: Just as chemical reactions require a minimum energy input before they self-sustain, behavioral change requires a deliberate initial investment — Hemingway's one-sentence trick is an engineered activation-energy minimizer.
- **[[Newton's First Law]]**: The same principle that keeps planets in orbit explains why habits persist: an object (or routine) in motion stays in motion unless acted upon by an external force, making external accountability structures and environment design powerful change levers.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is cognitive inertia and why does it persist?
**A**: Cognitive inertia is the tendency to stick with existing beliefs and habits even when they no longer serve us; it persists because questioning the status quo requires mental effort and carries perceived risk, making the default path of least resistance.

**Q2**: How did the De Brigard experiment reveal the true nature of status quo bias?
**A**: When participants were told they were already plugged into an experience machine, far fewer wanted to leave than wanted to avoid entering it — showing that opposition to the machine is really opposition to departing from whatever state feels like the status quo.

**Q3**: How can Hemingway's writing technique be applied to overcome inertia in other domains?
**A**: By stopping any daily task while momentum is still present — rather than when energy is exhausted — you ensure the next session begins in motion, reducing the activation energy needed to restart.
