## Summary

The Vasa Effect describes how the accumulation of individual positives can eventually produce a catastrophic negative, illustrated by the 1628 sinking of the Swedish warship *Vasa* after King Gustavus Adolphus kept adding to its design until it became structurally unstable. Research by Dr. Leidy Klotz shows that humans have a systematic bias toward additive solutions, even when subtractive ones are cheaper and more effective. Sahil Bloom argues we should deliberately practice subtraction — asking not what to add, but what to remove.

## Core Concepts

- **[[The Vasa Effect]]**: Adding individual positives (guns, decks, carvings) compounded into catastrophic structural failure; a template for how unchecked scope creep destroys systems.
- **[[Subtraction Bias]]**: [[Leidy Klotz]]'s research (UVA) shows people default to additive solutions; in a Lego stabilization task, only 41% chose the subtractive fix, rising to 61% when reminded it was free.
- **[[Additive Transformation Default]]**: Humans systematically search for what to add rather than what to eliminate, in products, habits, organizations, and life design.
- **Lao Tzu's distinction**: Knowledge is built by adding; wisdom is built by subtracting — a compression of the core argument.

## Key Takeaways

- **Vasa sank in 1628**: overloaded warship capsized after < 1 mile; 30 crew killed.
- **Root cause**: unchecked addition — each individual addition seemed positive; the sum was fatal.
- **Klotz study**: only 41% chose subtraction by default; 61% when told it was free.
- **Systematic bias**: humans "default to searching for additive transformations."
- **Better questions**: What am I leaving behind? What can I subtract?
- **Wisdom ≠ knowledge**: Lao Tzu — attain knowledge by adding, wisdom by subtracting.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Complexity Tax]]**: Every addition to a system increases its failure surface; the Vasa shows that complexity accumulates non-linearly until a single trigger causes total collapse.
- **[[Chesterton's Fence]] (inverse)**: Just as you shouldn't remove something without understanding why it's there, you should question why you're adding something — the default toward addition bypasses this check entirely.
- **[[Occam's Razor]]**: The simplest solution (subtracting the extra Lego brick) is often optimal, yet our cognitive defaults prevent us from even considering it.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the Vasa Effect and what historical event does it illustrate?
**A**: The Vasa Effect is the phenomenon where adding individual positives eventually creates a catastrophic negative; it is illustrated by the 1628 sinking of the Swedish warship *Vasa*, which capsized after relentless additions made it fatally unstable.

**Q2**: What did Dr. Leidy Klotz's Lego experiment reveal about human decision-making?
**A**: Only 41% of participants chose the cheaper subtractive solution by default; when explicitly reminded that subtraction was free, the rate rose to 61%, showing that people systematically default to additive transformations even when subtraction is clearly better.

**Q3**: How can you apply the principle of subtraction in your own life or work?
**A**: Instead of asking what to add, deliberately ask "What am I leaving behind?" and "What can I subtract?" — treating removal as a first-class option rather than an afterthought.
