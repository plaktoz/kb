## Summary
Game theory is the study of decision-making in situations where multiple players' strategies jointly determine outcomes, and each player must anticipate that everyone else is simultaneously trying to anticipate them. It's part of the Farnam Street mental-models latticework, most useful in interdependent, multi-actor competitive situations like oligopoly, and largely irrelevant in monopoly or pure-competition settings.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Game (Formal Definition)]]**: A situation of conflict or cooperation requiring (1) at least two players, (2) each with multiple possible strategies, (3) chosen strategies jointly determining the outcome, and (4) each outcome carrying a numerical payoff per player.
- **[[Interdependent Decision-Making]]**: Per Dixit & Nalebuff, "everyone's best choice depends on what others are going to do" — applies from oligopoly pricing to traffic-jam maneuvering to war, and requires viewing the whole system rather than optimizing in isolation.
- **[[Oligopoly and the Prisoners' Dilemma]]**: Game theory is essential in oligopoly (few sellers, each firm's profit depends on rivals' output) but unnecessary in pure competition or monopoly; firms are collectively best off cooperating like a monopolist, yet individually incentivized to defect — the classic prisoners'-dilemma structure.

## Key Takeaways
- Simple decisions with known options and consequences (e.g., picking an elevator floor) don't need game theory — it applies specifically when your environment is also trying to strategically respond to you.
- Michael Mauboussin's business application: game theory forces managers to view competitive situations from rivals' perspectives, and to think about how firms co-evolve rather than just compete head-on.
- The store-owner pricing example illustrates the core loop: any strategic move (lowering prices) must account for anticipated competitor reactions, not just its isolated effect.

## 🃏 Review Questions
**Q1**: What four conditions formally define a "game" in game theory?
**A**: At least two players, each with a number of possible strategies, where the strategies chosen jointly determine the outcome, and each outcome carries a numerical payoff for every player.

**Q2**: Why is game theory essential for understanding oligopoly but unnecessary for pure competition or monopoly?
**A**: In oligopoly, each firm's profit depends directly on the output/pricing decisions of a few identifiable rivals, making strategic anticipation essential; in pure competition there are too many small firms for any one to matter, and in monopoly there are no rivals to strategize against.

**Q3**: What tension does the prisoners'-dilemma structure reveal about oligopoly firms?
**A**: Firms are collectively best off cooperating (behaving like a single monopolist), but each individual firm faces a strong incentive to defect from that cooperation for its own short-term gain — the same logic as the classic prisoners' dilemma.
