## Summary

Using Kahneman and Tversky's classic "Steve the librarian" exercise, the author shows how people ignore base rates when personality traits feel representative of a category. He argues the popular "Everywhere Millionaires" narrative about ordinary business owners like Dick Portillo commits the mirror-image error: overweighting a rare success without asking how many people tried and failed. Both mistakes, he concludes, stem from ignoring the denominator — the true population of outcomes — behind a vivid story.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Representativeness Heuristic]]**: Judging likelihood by how well someone fits a stereotype (Steve the "librarian") rather than by actual population base rates.
- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Ignoring that there are ~140,000 librarians versus 13 million+ salespeople in the US, so "librarian-like" traits are still statistically improbable.
- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: Overweighting rare outliers like the "Everywhere Millionaires" without counting the far larger number of failed attempts.
- **[[Daniel Kahneman]]** and **[[Amos Tversky]]**: Originators of the base-rate classroom exercise the author builds his argument on.
- **[[Dick Portillo]]**: Hot dog entrepreneur cited by the *Wall Street Journal* as an "Everywhere Millionaire" whose business survived 50 years, far past the typical failure curve.
- **[[Entrepreneurship]]**: The author critiques economists who apply base-rate reasoning selectively — noting most founders are non-wealthy without asking how many total people (wealthy or not) attempt entrepreneurship.

## Key Takeaways

- **Steve exercise**: Most guess "librarian" over "salesperson," ignoring the 140k vs. 13M+ base rate.
- **Mirror-image error**: "Everywhere Millionaires" narrative overweights outcomes, ignoring failure base rates.
- **Rare but not that rare**: ~3 million "Everywhere Millionaires" among 340 million people, or 1–2 per hundred.
- **Survival curve**: Roughly a third of businesses survive 10 years; Portillo's lasted fifty.
- **Selective rigor**: Economists note founders are usually non-wealthy but skip the reverse base-rate calculation.
- **Unexplained ratio**: "Rich kids have 6x the odds of a successful business" lacks a comparison base rate.
- **Takeaway line**: Don't base major decisions on outlier stories — "don't expect the yacht."

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Representativeness Heuristic]]**: The Steve example is a textbook case — people substitute "does this match my stereotype" for the actual conditional probability implied by population base rates.
- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: The "Everywhere Millionaire" story is precisely the failure-data gap Wald-style reasoning warns about — the article never sees the businesses that didn't survive to be profiled.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the article?
**A**: People make base-rate errors in both directions — ignoring true population frequencies when a personality trait feels representative (Steve the librarian), and ignoring failure rates when a rare success story feels inspiring (Everywhere Millionaires).

**Q2**: What data point illustrates the scale of the "Everywhere Millionaire" outlier?
**A**: About 3 million people out of 340 million (roughly 1–2 per hundred) fit the profile, while only about a third of businesses survive even 10 years — Portillo's lasted fifty.

**Q3**: How should this change how you read entrepreneurship success stories?
**A**: Ask how many people attempted the same path and failed before crediting a survivor's traits or decisions as the reason for their success — and avoid using outlier stories to justify major life decisions.
