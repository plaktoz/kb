## Summary

Five decision-making models — Rational, Intuitive, Bounded Rationality, Vroom-Yetton, and Recognition-Primed — cover the range from slow, data-driven, high-stakes choices to fast, experience-driven calls under pressure, with the right model chosen based on time available, reversibility of the decision, need for team buy-in, and data availability.

## Core Concepts

- [[One-Way Door vs. Two-Way Door]] — Jeff Bezos's framework distinguishing irreversible decisions (deserving deep caution) from reversible ones (made quickly with ~70% of desired information)
- [[Bounded Rationality]] — Herbert Simon's concept of "satisficing": picking the first option that clears an acceptable threshold rather than searching for a theoretical optimum, since perfect information is unrealistic
- [[Recognition-Primed Decision Model]] — Gary Klein's model of expert decision-making under pressure: recognize a pattern, mentally simulate a response, act if it works, adjust if it doesn't
- [[Vroom-Yetton Decision-Making Model]] — a seven-question decision tree for choosing how much to involve a team, from fully autocratic to full group consensus

## Key Takeaways

- **Rational model**: best for high-stakes, "one-way door" decisions with time to gather data; risks analysis paralysis. Example: Unilever's 2024 Growth Action Plan concentrated resources on 30 "Power Brands" (75% of turnover), expanding gross margin by 280 basis points.
- **Intuitive model**: pattern recognition built from experience; best under extreme time pressure or with hard-to-quantify variables, but prone to bias.
- **Bounded Rationality**: appropriate for operational choices where "good enough now" beats "perfect later" — e.g., Microsoft Fabric adoption cut processing time 67% without evaluating every possible tool.
- **Vroom-Yetton**: matches decision style to whether team buy-in is required — P&G's restructuring paired autocratic strategic exits with more consultative factory-floor changes.
- **Choosing a model**: weigh time available, reversibility/risk, need for stakeholder buy-in, and data availability.
- **Common biases to guard against**: confirmation bias, anchoring bias, overconfidence bias, and groupthink — countered by devil's-advocate roles, self-set anchors, and explicit dissent norms (Amazon's "Have Backbone; Disagree and Commit").

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the difference between a "one-way door" and a "two-way door" decision in Bezos's framework?
**A**: A one-way door decision is irreversible and warrants careful, methodical deliberation; a two-way door decision is reversible and can be made quickly, even with only about 70% of the desired information, since you can step back through if it doesn't work out.

**Q2**: What does "satisficing" mean in the Bounded Rationality model, and why is it necessary?
**A**: Setting a minimum acceptable threshold and choosing the first option that clears it, rather than searching for the theoretically optimal choice — necessary because markets move faster than perfect information can be gathered.

**Q3**: When is the Vroom-Yetton model most useful, and what does it help a leader determine?
**A**: It's most useful for decisions requiring team execution and buy-in; it helps a leader determine, via a structured set of questions, whether to decide alone, consult others, or build full group consensus.
