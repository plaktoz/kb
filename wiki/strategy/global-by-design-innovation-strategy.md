## Summary
Dartmouth's Vijay Govindarajan and MIT's Amos Winter argue companies should design for the toughest constraints (affordability, portability, limited infrastructure) in emerging markets *first*, rather than building for wealthy markets and adapting downward — a "reverse innovation 2.0" that can outperform in both emerging and developed markets simultaneously.

## Core Concepts
- **[[Reverse Innovation 2.0]]**: Govindarajan's original reverse-innovation thesis (a business-strategy framing) combined with Winter's engineering perspective, focused on designing high-performance, low-cost solutions for resource-constrained markets that can then disrupt premium-priced products in wealthy markets.
- **[[The Three Merits Framework]]**: A screening tool for evaluating opportunities — **problem merit** (does solving this meaningfully impact many people, with a viable business behind it?), **innovation merit** (is genuinely new technology required, or can existing solutions be adapted?), and **value merit** (will stakeholders support both creating and disseminating the solution?).
- **[[Dominant Logic]]**: Govindarajan's term for the entrenched systems, incentives, and capabilities multinationals build around serving rich-country markets first — described as the real barrier to reverse innovation ("like moving the Titanic 180 degrees"), not technology or market understanding.
- **[[Tempered Dreamer]]**: Winter's concept of recognizing your own limitations as an innovator and finding the specific niche where you can make the largest realistic impact, rather than trying to solve an entire systemic problem at once.

## Key Takeaways
- **Case example**: GE Healthcare's India team lived in rural villages for two months, distilling the challenge down from broad healthcare access to a $400 EKG machine (vs. the $20,000 US version), meeting requirements of affordability, portability, no-grid-power operation, and easy repair.
- **Timeline discipline**: The EKG project took 18 months end-to-end, with 3 months dedicated specifically to requirements-gathering — "fast and fail is no good."
- **AI's role and limits**: AI accelerates background research but can't yet directly solve these design problems — it lacks access to unrecorded tacit knowledge, has gaps in physical-world understanding, and can't replace face-to-face stakeholder rapport-building.
- **Resource counterintuition**: Govindarajan advises giving innovation teams *less* money to force out-of-the-box thinking, and deliberately including "maverick" team members who propose unconventional ideas.
- **Scaling risk**: The biggest obstacle multinationals face when scaling reverse-innovation products into home markets is fear of cannibalizing premium-priced offerings — reframed as expanding the overall market rather than one-to-one substitution.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Inversion]]**: Instead of asking "how do we adapt our premium product for poorer markets," the framework inverts the question to "what would the best-performing, lowest-cost solution look like if designed from the toughest constraints first" — then checks whether that answer also wins in wealthy markets.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this podcast episode?
**A**: Companies achieve better global outcomes by designing products for the toughest market constraints (emerging markets) first, rather than building for wealthy markets and adapting them downward.

**Q2**: What are the "three merits" used to evaluate a global-by-design opportunity?
**A**: Problem merit (large, meaningful impact with business viability), innovation merit (genuine need for new technology), and value merit (aligned incentives for both creating and distributing the solution).

**Q3**: According to Govindarajan, what is the biggest real barrier to reverse innovation, and why?
**A**: Organizational "dominant logic" — the entrenched systems, incentives, and capabilities built around serving rich-country markets first — not a lack of technology or market understanding; it's a mindset problem, not a technical one.
