## Summary

In this interview, Farnam Street founder [[Shane Parrish]] traces how mental-model thinking (drawn from [[Charlie Munger]] and [[Warren Buffett]]) shapes better decisions, arguing their edge isn't inventing new ideas but applying existing ones — psychology, competitive strategy — more rigorously than the originators.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: Asking "and then what?" after a decision — Parrish argues most people stop at the first-order effect and never trace the ripple consequences (e.g., a cost-saving purchase whose savings eventually get competed away).
- **[[Decision Journal]]**: Documenting the variables considered and expected range of outcomes *before* a decision is made, exposing the reasoning process to later challenge and feedback rather than just judging by results.
- **[[The Feynman Technique]]**: Explaining a concept on paper as if teaching someone with no background — an inability to do so simply flags a gap in real understanding.
- **[[Map Is Not The Territory]]**: Mental models are reductions of reality, not reality itself (quoting George Box: "all models are false but some are useful") — useful but inherently limited.

## Key Takeaways

- **Institutionalized debiasing**: Munger and Buffett's edge is applying Kahneman-style bias research more consistently than Kahneman himself claims to manage in his own decisions.
- **Reading depth over volume**: Parrish deliberately cut his book count after realizing reading 140+ books/year for the sake of finishing meant shallow synthesis — "it's not about how many books you read but what you get out of the books you read."
- **Toolbox framing**: Mental models are tools; effective decision-making requires many of them plus judgment about when each applies, not blind application of a favorite model.
- **Berkshire's information-flow trick**: Buffett and Munger manage information overload by focusing only on companies/industries that change slowly, deliberately ignoring fast-moving sectors where they can't reliably predict outcomes.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: Explicitly the interview's central recurring theme — treated as a distinct, underused discipline rather than just "thinking more," because most decision failures stem from stopping analysis at the first visible effect.
- **[[Circle of Competence]]**: Buffett/Munger's practice of focusing only on slow-changing industries they deeply understand is a direct application of staying within a known circle of competence rather than chasing every opportunity.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Shane Parrish argues that superior decision-makers like Munger and Buffett succeed not by inventing new ideas but by applying existing mental models (psychology, strategy, incentives) more rigorously and consistently than most people, including the original researchers.

**Q2**: What specific technique does Parrish recommend for exposing decision-making to feedback?
**A**: Keeping a decision journal that records, in advance, the variables considered and the range of outcomes expected — creating a record that can later be checked against what actually happened, rather than judging decisions only by results.

**Q3**: How would you apply the second-order thinking concept to a real decision?
**A**: Before acting on an attractive-looking option (e.g., adopting new software to cut costs), explicitly ask "and then what?" — who else reacts to this change, and what does the *next* round of consequences look like — rather than stopping at the first, most visible benefit.
