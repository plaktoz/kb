## Summary

Tit for tat is a [[Game Theory]] strategy considered most effective for iterated (repeated) games: start by cooperating, then mirror whatever your opponent did in the prior round. Codified by mathematical psychologist [[Anatol Rapoport]], it reflects our instinctive sense of reciprocity — helping those who help us and withdrawing from those who don't. A refinement called "tit for tat with forgiveness" prevents endless mutual defection cycles by occasionally cooperating even after the opponent defects.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Tit for Tat]]**: Start cooperative, then mirror the opponent's last move in every subsequent round.
- **[[Iterated Prisoner's Dilemma]]**: The class of repeated games where tit for tat consistently outperforms other strategies.
- **[[Anatol Rapoport]]**: Mathematical psychologist who formally codified the tit-for-tat strategy.
- **[[Reciprocity]]**: The psychological and strategic instinct underpinning the strategy — cooperation rewarded, defection met in kind.
- **Tit for Tat with Forgiveness**: A refinement that occasionally cooperates after a defection to break cycles of mutual punishment.
- **[[Peter Kaufman]]**: Advises to "go positive and go first" — the practical life application of tit for tat's opening move.

## Key Takeaways

- **Start cooperative**: The strategy always opens with cooperation, not defection.
- **Mirror behavior**: Each subsequent move copies what the opponent did the round before.
- **Reciprocity wins long-term**: Cooperation benefits groups over time in repeated interactions.
- **Forgiveness breaks deadlocks**: Occasional forgiveness after defection prevents endless retaliation spirals.
- **Life is iterative**: In repeated relationships, signaling trustworthiness early pays compound dividends.
- **Strategic forgiveness**: When mistakes happen without malice, forgiving is the rational choice.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Reciprocity]]**: Tit for tat is the formalized expression of reciprocity — the baseline human norm of matching others' generosity or hostility — applied as an optimal algorithm in iterated games.
- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: "Tit for tat with forgiveness" exemplifies second-order thinking: the first-order reaction to defection is retaliation, but the smarter move accounts for how cycles of mutual punishment destroy long-run value for both parties.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core mechanic of the tit-for-tat strategy?
**A**: Start by cooperating, then in every subsequent round mirror whatever your opponent did in the previous round — rewarding cooperation and punishing defection in kind.

**Q2**: Why does "tit for tat with forgiveness" outperform pure tit for tat in some scenarios?
**A**: Pure tit for tat can lock both parties into endless cycles of mutual defection after a single misstep; adding occasional forgiveness breaks the cycle and restores cooperative equilibrium.

**Q3**: How does Peter Kaufman's advice apply the tit-for-tat insight to everyday life?
**A**: Kaufman's "go positive and go first" captures the opening-move principle — extending trust and goodwill proactively signals cooperative intent and increases the odds of durable, mutually beneficial relationships.
