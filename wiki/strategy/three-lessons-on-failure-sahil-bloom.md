## Summary

Sahil Bloom shares a vulnerable account of DNF-ing (Did Not Finish) a marathon at mile 21.45 after dehydration on an unexpectedly hot, humid day caused his heart rate to spike and his hamstring to lock up. Rather than a planned success story, he pivots to three lessons about failure: staying in the game long-term, categorizing failure types to learn from them, and building an identity that doesn't depend on achievement.

## Core Concepts

- **[[DNF (Did Not Finish)]]** — Bloom stopped at mile 21.45 despite running a 6:24 average pace; conditions (70°F+, 90% humidity) triggered physiological breakdown
- **[[Compounding]]** — long-term availability and health compound over time; protecting it outweighs short-term toughness
- **Three Types of Failure** framework:
  - *[[Failures of Nature]]* — uncontrollable external forces
  - *[[Failures of Effort]]* — lack of energy or commitment
  - *[[Failures of Execution]]* — gaps in planning or follow-through
- **[[Identity Beyond Achievement]]** — separating self-worth from outcomes; growth happens during pursuit, not at the finish line
- **[[Sahil Bloom]]** — author and newsletter writer focused on personal growth and mental models

## Key Takeaways

- **Survive and Advance**: prioritize long-term availability over short-term toughness.
- **Compounding requires staying healthy**: fitness and life gains require continued participation.
- **Classify your failure**: execution failures are learnable; nature failures are not your fault.
- **Bloom's DNF = Failure of Execution**: missed hydration adjustments for unexpected conditions.
- **"Be grateful for failures that forge you"**: failures with lessons are assets.
- **Identity test**: post-race calm signals maturity — not needing the medal to feel "enough."
- **Cool Runnings quote**: "If you're not enough without it, you'll never be enough with it."

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Compounding]]**: Staying in the game is the prerequisite for compounding; one catastrophic exit resets the curve — Bloom's "survive and advance" principle is compounding logic applied to health and endurance.
- **[[Locus of Control]]**: Classifying failures into nature, effort, and execution maps directly onto what you can and cannot control — acting only on what's within your locus prevents wasted energy and enables genuine learning.
- **[[Identity vs. Achievement]]**: Tying identity to outcomes (medals, finishes) creates fragility; decoupling it means setbacks don't threaten the self, which is the psychological foundation for sustained long-term effort.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central argument Bloom makes about how to respond to failure?
**A**: Failure should be classified by type — nature, effort, or execution — because only certain categories are learnable and actionable; the goal is to extract lessons while preserving long-term participation through a "survive and advance" mindset.

**Q2**: How did Bloom categorize his marathon DNF, and why does the classification matter?
**A**: He classified it as a Failure of Execution — specifically, missing hydration adjustments for unexpectedly hot, humid conditions — which makes it a learnable, correctable error rather than a reflection of character or effort.

**Q3**: What is the practical implication of building identity beyond achievement?
**A**: When your sense of self is not tied to outcomes, setbacks don't trigger identity-level devastation, making it easier to stay in the game, iterate, and compound progress over time.
