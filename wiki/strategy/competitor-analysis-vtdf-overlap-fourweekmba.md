## Summary

Competitor analysis identifies which firms compete with you and how strongly each can affect your business, using [[Ming-Jer Chen]]'s two tests of market commonality and resource similarity. For tech companies, the guide maps competition by overlap across customers, technology, distribution and financial model, plus future technology development. Today's filings from Nvidia, Microsoft, Chegg and Alphabet show rivals who are also customers, partners and substitutors from outside the product market.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Competitor Analysis]]**: Judging how, and how hard, each rival can hit your business; popularized by [[Michael Porter]]'s *Competitive Strategy* (1980).
- **[[Market Commonality]] and [[Resource Similarity]]**: Chen's (1996) two tests: shared customers and reliance on similar resources.
- **[[VTDF Framework]]**: Value, Technology, Distribution and Financial model, applied to map competitive overlap.
- **[[Competitive Asymmetry]]**: Overlap looks different from each side, as in Nvidia's customers who also design AI chips.
- **[[Coopetition]]**: [[Microsoft]] says some AI rivals are "also current or potential partners".
- **[[Substitutes]]**: Bergen and Peteraf (2002) warn that managers scanning only their own product market miss substitutors, as Chegg saw with Google AI Overviews.
- **Case study**: [[Tesla]] competes across automaking, energy generation and storage, and autonomous driving.

## Key Takeaways

- **Two Tests**: Compare shared customers and similar resources to size a rival.
- **Five Lenses**: Customer, technology, distribution and financial overlap, plus future technology development.
- **Relationship Column**: Mark rivals that are also customers, suppliers or partners.
- **Start Point**: Build the rival list from customers' alternatives, including doing nothing or using AI.
- **Direct Fight**: High customer and resource overlap together signals head-to-head competition.
- **Read Filings**: Rivals' competition sections and risk factors name the threats they see.
- **Refresh Cadence**: Update the map each quarter and when a rival launches outside its usual market.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Competitive threat is a function of overlap** — How strongly a rival can hurt you depends on how many customers you share and how alike your resources are; neither test alone makes a direct fight.
→ explains: Two Tests, Direct Fight, Five Lenses

**T2. One firm can hold several roles at once** — The same company can be supplier, customer, partner and rival simultaneously, so a rival list without the relationship attached misleads. · *model:* [[Coopetition]]
→ explains: Relationship Column, Read Filings

**T3. The sharpest threats come from outside your own product market** — Scanning only your own market misses substitutors, so the rival list must start from what customers could use instead.
→ explains: Start Point, Refresh Cadence

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core purpose of competitor analysis and its two classic tests?
**A**: It identifies which firms compete with you and how strongly each can affect your business. Chen (1996) proposed two tests: market commonality (shared customers) and resource similarity (reliance on similar resources).

**Q2**: What evidence from company filings shows competition is now multi-role?
**A**: Nvidia lists cloud companies that design AI chips as competitors while saying its customers include all major cloud providers. Microsoft says some AI rivals are "also current or potential partners".

**Q3**: How should you build and maintain a rival list?
**A**: Start from customers' alternatives, including doing nothing or asking an AI assistant, score customer and resource overlap separately, and mark rivals that are also customers, suppliers or partners. Refresh the map each quarter and when a rival launches outside its usual market.
