## Summary
Vertex executive chairman Jeffrey Leiden argues that more innovation bets do not produce more successes; choosing the right target does. Vertex narrowed R&D and business development to diseases with validated targets and raised its Phase II success rate above 70%, versus a 10%–20% industry average. He says innovation needs integrated functions, serial self-improvement, and the discipline to say no.

## Core Concepts
- [[Jeffrey Leiden]]: Vertex Pharmaceuticals executive chairman, joined in 2009.
- [[Vertex Pharmaceuticals]]: moved from 20 programs and one breakthrough product to multiple transformative medicines.
- [[Target Selection]]: failures stemmed from choosing the wrong biological target, not from missing it.
- [[Strategic Focus]]: a "sandbox" of diseases with genetically or pharmacologically validated targets, shared by R&D and business development.
- [[Innovation Silos]]: research, development, manufacturing, commercial, and business development working on separate tracks.
- [[Serial Innovation]]: improving on your own breakthroughs before competitors do.
- [[Opportunity Cost]]: the question of what leaders are willing to stop doing.
- [[Systems Thinking]]: innovation judged by how the whole organization converts ideas into products.

## Key Takeaways
- **Wrong Premise**: More bets create the appearance of innovation without improving effectiveness.
- **Industry Odds**: Over 90% of drugs entering clinical development fail; approval takes 10–15 years.
- **Vertex Starting Point**: 20 programs over two decades yielded one product, soon made obsolete.
- **Sandbox Strategy**: R&D and business development stay inside validated-target diseases; walk away otherwise.
- **Integration**: Include all functions from the earliest decision stages.
- **Results**: Phase II success exceeded 70% across six diseases versus 10%–20% industry.
- **Discipline Questions**: Leaders ask why innovation failed before and what to stop doing.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Target quality beats shot volume** — When the target is wrong, accuracy and activity do not matter; success odds are driven by choosing where to aim, so constraining choices raises the hit rate.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Leiden's core argument about innovation?
**A**: More bets do not produce more successes; "it's not about the shots, it's about the target." Focus and strategy accelerate innovation rather than limit it.

**Q2**: What evidence supports Vertex's approach?
**A**: By restricting itself to diseases with validated targets, Vertex's Phase II proof-of-concept success rate exceeded 70% across six diseases, against a 10%–20% industry average.

**Q3**: How should leaders apply this before approving a new project or acquisition?
**A**: Ask why innovation failed here before, whether you hold a genuine advantage, what evidence shows the problem is solvable, and what you are willing to stop doing. Measure the organization's conversion of ideas into products, not the number of bets.
