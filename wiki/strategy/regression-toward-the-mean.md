## Summary

[[Regression Toward the Mean]] is a statistical phenomenon first identified by [[Francis Galton]] holding that extreme outcomes in complex systems tend to be followed by more moderate ones. Because performance measurements contain both a true component and a random error component, improvements after an intervention may reflect natural statistical regression rather than the intervention's effect. The key corrective is to introduce a control group or compare against historical benchmarks rather than attributing change to a causal story.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Regression Toward the Mean]]**: Extreme results in any noisy system will trend back toward average over repeated observations.
- **[[Francis Galton]]**: First described the phenomenon through hereditary studies in the 19th century.
- **[[Daniel Kahneman]]**: Popularized the concept through examples in sports commentary and military training feedback.
- **[[Imperfect Correlation]]**: Regression occurs whenever the correlation between two scores is less than perfect — no perfect correlation, always some regression.
- **[[Random Error Component]]**: Any measured performance has a true skill component and a random error component; extreme readings are partly driven by luck.
- **[[Control Group]]**: The methodological solution — compare intervention group to an untreated group to isolate genuine effect from regression.

## Key Takeaways

- **Spurious Improvement**: Low performers enrolled in training often improve via regression, not the program itself.
- **Sports Fallacy**: Commentators invent psychological narratives for performance shifts that are purely statistical.
- **Military Feedback Illusion**: Praise appeared to hurt, criticism to help — Kahneman showed this was regression, not feedback effects.
- **Treatment Headlines**: Extreme groups (e.g., severely depressed children) naturally trend toward average, making any intervention look effective.
- **Galton's Rule**: "Whenever the correlation between two scores is imperfect, there will be regression to the mean."
- **Track Records Over Single Events**: Prioritize multi-observation track records; single extreme results are unreliable signals.
- **Control Group Fix**: When a control group is impossible, compare cautiously against historical baselines.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Regression Toward the Mean]]**: In any noisy system, selection of extreme cases guarantees the next observation is less extreme — this is a mathematical certainty, not a causal force, yet humans persistently misattribute it to interventions or feedback.
- **[[Resulting]]**: Judging decisions by outcomes rather than process is compounded by regression — a great result after a mediocre process may just be luck reverting, not skill, making the decision look better than it was.
- **[[Base Rate Neglect]]**: Ignoring how often extreme performance naturally regresses causes observers to overweight a single intervention's causal power and underweight the statistical baseline pull toward average.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of regression toward the mean?
**A**: In complex systems with a chance component, extreme outcomes tend to be followed by more moderate ones — because extreme measurements are partly driven by random error that will not persist.

**Q2**: What mechanism causes regression, and what does Kahneman's rule state?
**A**: Regression occurs whenever the correlation between two variables is imperfect; Kahneman states that wherever correlation is imperfect, there will always be regression to the mean.

**Q3**: How should decision-makers guard against mistaking regression for a causal effect?
**A**: Introduce a control group to isolate genuine improvement from natural regression; when that is impossible, compare results against historical benchmarks rather than attributing change to the intervention.
