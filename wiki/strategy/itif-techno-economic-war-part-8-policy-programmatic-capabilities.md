## Summary
The U.S. government lacks the data, analytical capacity, and lead organization needed to craft and run a national industrial strategy against China. Atkinson argues the process-based American state must become more goal-driven ("teleological"), building on the national security state model. He proposes better data, economy-wide industry analysis, and a new quasi-public agency, NAITA, to lead the effort.

## Core Concepts
- **[[National Economic Power Industries]]**: advanced traded-sector industries critical to security or sovereignty, spanning defense, dual-use (semiconductors, pharmaceuticals, aerospace), and enabling industries.
- **[[Developmental State]]**: a government with the analytical and organizational capability to craft and execute industrial strategy, which the U.S. lacks.
- **[[Teleological vs. Process-Based State]]**: the U.S. derives legitimacy from procedure; the national security state is the one goal-driven exception.
- **[[Industrial Commons]]**: shared know-how, skills, and supplier ecosystems; weakness in enabling industries erodes dual-use and defense industries.
- **[[National Advanced Industry and Technology Agency (NAITA)]]**: proposed quasi-public lead agency modeled on the Ex-Im Bank, with data, advanced industry, emerging technology, and innovation systems divisions.
- **[[Net Assessment]]**: the Pentagon's strategic-analysis framework, which Atkinson wants replicated for commercial power.
- **[[Economic Defense Unit]]**: new Pentagon unit, whose scope Atkinson sees as too narrow and deal-focused.
- **[[China]]**, **[[Information Technology and Innovation Foundation]]**, **[[Milton Friedman]]**, **[[Supply Chain Vulnerability]]**.

## Key Takeaways
- **No Owner**: No U.S. office owns techno-economic industrial policy; agencies each pursue separate missions.
- **Process vs. Goals**: The U.S. prizes procedural legitimacy; only national security is goal-driven.
- **Broad Definition**: National power depends on dual-use and enabling industries, not just defense.
- **Hard to Rebuild**: Complex industries are hard to restart once lost to adversaries.
- **Supply Chain Exposure**: Unlike the Cold War, China can cut off needed U.S. imports.
- **Lead Agency Options**: Commerce is weak, the War Department is insular, and NAITA is preferred.
- **Data Gaps**: Statistical agencies lack international data and rarely interpret their own data.
- **Talent Gap**: No U.S. university teaches economic warfighting or strategic industry policy.
- **Political Hurdles**: A new agency faces committee conflicts, appropriations limits, and resistance to larger government.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Cross-cutting goals need a single accountable owner** — When every agency optimizes its own mission, a goal that spans all of them is owned by none. A lead institution is needed to accumulate specialized knowledge and coordinate the rest. · *model:* [[Coordination Problem]]
→ explains: No Owner, Lead Agency Options, Data Gaps

**T2. A system built to follow process cannot pursue an outcome** — Institutions legitimized by procedural fidelity have no fixed destination, so they do not organize around winning a contest unless explicitly reoriented toward a goal.
→ explains: Process vs. Goals, Political Hurdles

**T3. Dependence on a rival creates leverage for the rival** — The ability to cut off critical inputs gives an adversary bargaining power, and capabilities lost in complex industries are hard to rebuild. · *model:* [[Single Point of Failure]]
→ explains: Broad Definition, Hard to Rebuild, Supply Chain Exposure

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is Atkinson's central argument about U.S. government capability?
**A**: The U.S. lacks the analytical, organizational, and implementation capacity to run a national economic power industry strategy against China. It needs to shift from a process-first state to a goal-first, developmental one.

**Q2**: Why does the article say a lead agency is needed rather than relying on interagency coordination?
**A**: Sector-specific knowledge has to be accumulated in one institution, and agencies pursue their own missions without weighing competitiveness. The IMF analysis of the Asian Tigers cited in the article credits "a network of institutions with a leading agency at its center."

**Q3**: What organizational solution does the author prefer, and what is its main obstacle?
**A**: He prefers a quasi-public National Advanced Industry and Technology Agency modeled on the Ex-Im Bank. The obstacles are the political difficulty of creating a new agency and securing adequate appropriations.
