## Summary
BCG's analysis of the 2,000 most valuable US-listed companies from 1990 to 2025 finds that leaders are staying at the top longer: median giant churn fell from 24% to 13%. Persistence is not permanence, and not all lock-in is equal, since some leaders still capture most new value while others are being bypassed. Leaders should treat stability as something to test, not trust, by asking whether their advantage is compounding and whether a challenger could change the game.

## Core Concepts
- [[Giant Churn]]: share of a sector's top-ten market cap held by firms absent from the top ten five years earlier.
- [[Leadership Persistence]]: 10 of 15 industries saw churn decline from 2005 through 2025.
- [[Network Effects]], scale, data, brand and distribution: self-reinforcing advantages behind lock-in.
- [[Value Pools]]: new areas of value creation that leaders may or may not capture, such as AI-driven power demand for utilities.
- [[Tesla]]: captured nearly all new auto-industry value with a direct-to-consumer model and an AI-company valuation.
- [[Disruption]]: successful disruptors become incumbents, so disruption can itself produce stability.
- [[Artificial Intelligence]]: may test whether IT leaders' advantages keep compounding.

## Key Takeaways
- **Falling Churn**: Median giant churn dropped from 24% (1990-2004) to 13% (2005-2025).
- **Persistent, Not Permanent**: 62% of companies reaching the top ten later fell out.
- **Exit Causes**: 78% of exits came from rival displacement; only 16% from M&A.
- **Digital Platforms**: Churn fell from nearly 100% to about 5% as the category consolidated.
- **Consumer Staples**: Only 42% of top-ten entrants were displaced, the lowest of any industry.
- **Utilities Gap**: Top ten captured just over half of new value; AI power demand benefited others.
- **IT Concentration**: Top five captured nearly 70% of new value, but AI may change the assumptions.
- **Auto Concentration**: Tesla's $1.5 trillion valuation is nineteen times the second-largest, O'Reilly.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Endurance is not strength** — Longevity at the top shows who has survived, not whether the advantage still compounds; advantages that persist only because no challenger has yet appeared are untested. · *model:* [[Economic Moat]]
→ explains: Persistent, Not Permanent, IT Concentration, Utilities Gap

**T2. Challengers win by changing the basis of competition** — A challenger need not outperform incumbents on the old rules; it can shift what customers value so incumbent strengths matter less.
→ explains: Auto Concentration, Digital Platforms

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central finding of BCG's leadership analysis?
**A**: Among the 2,000 most valuable US-listed companies, leaders are staying at the top longer, with median giant churn falling from 24% to 13% between the 1990-2004 and 2005-2025 periods. Leadership is more persistent but not permanent, since 62% of companies that reached the top ten eventually fell out.

**Q2**: How does BCG measure whether lock-in is still being rewarded?
**A**: It compares the share of new industry value created from 2015 to 2025 captured by the top five players against how many of 2025's top five had long tenure. Long tenure plus continued value capture is the strongest form of lock-in, while a falling capture share signals more exposed leadership.

**Q3**: What questions should a leader ask to test whether their position is durable?
**A**: Are we capturing the next value pool or just holding the current one, is our advantage still compounding or merely untested, and what if a challenger changes the game itself? Leaders should treat stability as something to test, not something to trust.
