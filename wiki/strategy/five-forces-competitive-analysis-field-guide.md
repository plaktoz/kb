## Summary

A field guide to [[Porter's Five Forces]] explains the five structural forces that determine industry profitability, Porter's generic cost-leadership vs. differentiation strategies, and how the durability of competitive moats depends on whether their underlying advantage can persist rather than whether it exists today.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Porter's Five Forces]]**: Competitive rivalry, threat of new entrants, bargaining power of suppliers, bargaining power of buyers, and threat of substitutes — five structural forces that together determine an industry's average profitability, developed by Michael Porter in 1979.
- **[[Complementors]]**: Brandenburger and Nalebuff's extension to Five Forces — parties who increase demand for a firm's products without being direct competitors, especially relevant in platform and ecosystem businesses.
- **[[Cost Leadership vs. Differentiation]]**: Porter's generic strategies — a structurally lower cost base versus offering something buyers perceive as meaningfully distinct and worth a premium — with a "stuck in the middle" position resulting from pursuing both without a clear primary commitment.
- **[[Competitive Moat]]**: An entry barrier applied at the firm level (network effects, switching costs, scale, proprietary technology, brand); the critical test is whether it's likely to persist, not whether it exists today.

## Key Takeaways

- Rivalry intensifies when competitors are similarly sized, industry growth is slow, products are undifferentiated, and exit costs are high; concentrated industries can show cooperative pricing without explicit coordination.
- Entry-barrier height (economies of scale, capital requirements, switching costs, regulation, brand) determines whether an industry sustains above-average profitability or sees margins compete down to the cost of capital.
- The internet has structurally increased buyer power in both consumer and B2B markets by improving price transparency and reducing search costs.
- Five Forces is explicitly a cross-sectional, point-in-time tool — useful for diagnosing why profitability is high or low in an industry, but not for understanding dynamic processes like first-mover advantage or disruptive innovation.
- Moats can and do erode: network-effect moats have failed once competitors reach a comparable user threshold, regulatory moats can vanish with policy changes, and brand moats weaken when a new entrant offers a meaningfully better experience.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of this article?
**A**: Porter's Five Forces remains a durable framework for diagnosing industry profitability because it analyzes structural, industry-level dynamics rather than firm-specific tactics — but it's a point-in-time diagnostic tool, not a predictor of how competitive dynamics will evolve.

**Q2**: What determines whether a competitive moat provides lasting protection versus temporary advantage?
**A**: Not whether the moat exists today, but whether its underlying mechanism is likely to persist — network effects can erode once a rival reaches comparable scale, regulatory moats can disappear with policy change, and brand moats weaken when a better alternative emerges.

**Q3**: Why does the article warn against pursuing both cost leadership and differentiation simultaneously?
**A**: Without a clear primary commitment to one strategy, a company risks landing "stuck in the middle" — capturing neither the premium margins that differentiation provides nor the volume economics that cost leadership provides.
