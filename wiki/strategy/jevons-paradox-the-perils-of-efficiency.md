## Summary

Jevons Paradox, originating from economist William Stanley Jevons's 1865 study of coal, holds that efficiency improvements in resource use typically *increase* total consumption rather than reduce it — because lower costs enable new and expanded uses. Sahil Bloom extends this beyond economics to personal productivity, arguing that tools meant to save time often generate more demand for that time instead. Escaping the trap requires intentional pre-assignment of saved time and stopping the broadcast of efficiency signals to others.

## Core Concepts

- [[Jevons Paradox]]: efficiency gains expand total usage rather than conserve resources
- [[William Stanley Jevons]]: 19th-century economist who first documented the coal consumption paradox
- [[Satya Nadella]] invoking [[Jevons Paradox]] during the [[DeepSeek]] AI moment to explain why cheaper AI increases infrastructure demand
- [[Oliver Burkeman]]'s observation on the external trap: high-output individuals signal their time is cheap, inviting more demand
- Two root causes: **internal** (guilt-driven work expansion) and **external** (signaling effect attracting more requests)

## Key Takeaways

- **Historical origin**: Jevons (1865) found coal efficiency gains led to *more* coal consumption, not less.
- **Modern examples**: Smartphones → 10x more emails; video calls → more meetings; AI → more job applications, fewer interviews.
- **Internal trap**: High achievers fill freed time with more work to avoid the guilt of rest.
- **External trap**: Demonstrated efficiency signals others that your time is now cheaper to consume.
- **Burkeman's law**: "People who transform themselves into 26-lane highways of productivity often find they're no less busy."
- **Audit step 1**: List 3–5 areas of recent efficiency gains; ask if each created or consumed time.
- **Fix for internal**: Pre-assign saved time *before* you save it.
- **Fix for external**: Stop broadcasting the efficiency signal (e.g. email delays, phone lockboxes).
- **Core principle**: Efficiency only has value when it delivers more of what you actually want.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Jevons Paradox]]**: Lower cost per unit of a resource reliably expands total demand — the same dynamic that caused coal over-consumption now causes time over-consumption when personal productivity improves.
- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: When efficiency becomes the goal, people optimize for its appearance (busyness, output volume) rather than the underlying value — exactly the trap Bloom warns against.
- **[[Signaling Theory]]**: Demonstrating productivity efficiency publicly changes others' expectations and demand, turning a personal gain into a collective tax on your time.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of Jevons Paradox as applied to personal productivity?
**A**: Efficiency improvements typically *increase* total time consumption rather than free up time, because lower "cost" of an activity enables expanded use by both yourself and others.

**Q2**: What are the two root causes Bloom identifies for the Jevons Trap in personal life?
**A**: Internal — high achievers fill freed time with more work to avoid guilt of rest; External — demonstrating efficiency signals others that your time is now cheaper, attracting more demands.

**Q3**: How does Bloom recommend breaking out of the Jevons Trap?
**A**: For internal issues, pre-assign saved time before you save it; for external issues, stop broadcasting your efficiency gains (e.g. use email delays or phone lockboxes to mask availability).
