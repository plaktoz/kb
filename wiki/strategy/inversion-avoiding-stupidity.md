## Summary

Inversion, popularized by [[Charlie Munger]] and rooted in mathematician [[Carl Jacobi]]'s motto "invert, always invert," is a mental model that solves hard problems by thinking backward. Rather than asking how to achieve a goal, you ask what would cause failure — then eliminate those conditions. Avoiding stupidity is easier and more reliable than seeking brilliance.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Inversion]]**: Reframing problems by working backward from failure rather than forward toward success.
- **[[Charlie Munger]]**: Berkshire Hathaway vice-chairman who applied inversion extensively in business and life decisions.
- **[[Carl Jacobi]]**: 19th-century mathematician whose problem-solving principle "invert, always invert" underlies the model.
- **[[Additive vs. Subtractive Thinking]]**: Forward thinking adds actions and solutions; backward thinking removes obstacles and mistakes.
- **[[Avoiding Stupidity]]**: The practical upshot — reducing obvious errors is more tractable than generating brilliant moves.

## Key Takeaways

- **Backward reasoning**: Ask "what kills innovation?" then stop doing those things.
- **Subtractive edge**: Removing obstacles carries less risk of unintended harm than adding interventions.
- **Munger's prescription**: Identify where you don't want to end up — then stay away.
- **Not a complete solver**: Inversion sidesteps avoidable errors; it won't always produce the full answer.
- **Asymmetry of effort**: Eliminating obvious mistakes is easier than engineering brilliant outcomes.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Inversion]]**: Problems that resist forward-thinking often yield when attacked in reverse — Jacobi's insight is that the solution space becomes visible only when you map the failure conditions first.
- **[[Via Negativa]]**: Wisdom accumulated by removal rather than addition; avoiding what harms is more robust than prescribing what helps, because negative knowledge is harder to misapply.
- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: Inversion is one instantiation of thinking one step further — not "what do I want?" but "what generates the opposite, and can I remove it?"

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim of inversion as a mental model?
**A**: Many hard problems are best solved by thinking backward — identifying what causes failure and eliminating those conditions — rather than pursuing the positive goal directly.

**Q2**: How does subtractive thinking differ from additive thinking, and why does it matter?
**A**: Additive thinking adds actions and interventions; subtractive thinking removes obstacles and mistakes. The subtractive approach is less risky because it avoids introducing unintended harm.

**Q3**: How would you apply inversion to improving team performance?
**A**: Instead of asking "what drives high performance?", ask "what consistently destroys team performance?" — then systematically eliminate those behaviors, which is a more tractable starting point.
