## Summary

[[Robert MacArthur]] and [[E.O. Wilson]]'s [[Theory of Island Biogeography]] argues that island species counts trend toward equilibrium through the interplay of immigration (species arriving) and local extinction (species dying out), not just slow speciation. The equilibrium point shifts based on island size and remoteness: small, isolated islands sustain fewer species and are more fragile. This biological model generalizes broadly — isolation, immigration pressure, and extinction dynamics apply to businesses, ideas, and ecosystems alike.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Island Equilibrium]]**: The stable species count where immigration rate equals local extinction rate; specific species turn over (turnover) while total count stays constant.
- **[[Theory of Island Biogeography]]**: Developed by [[Robert MacArthur]] and [[E.O. Wilson]]; reframed species counts as a dynamic equilibrium driven by ongoing immigration and extinction rather than slow speciation.
- **Species-Area Relationship**: Larger islands support more species; smaller islands support fewer, making them more ecologically fragile.
- **Remoteness Effect**: The further an island from a mainland or island cluster, the lower the immigration rate, producing fewer resident species.
- **[[Turnover]]**: One species arrives, another goes extinct — the roster changes but the equilibrium count holds steady.
- **[[Red Queen Effect]]**: Referenced as a parallel — businesses and products that fail to co-evolve go extinct, mirroring the biological extinction dynamic on islands.
- **[[Speciation]]**: The historical (and slower) mechanism once thought to drive species increase; MacArthur and Wilson showed immigration and extinction dominate on practical timescales.
- **[[Jared Diamond]]**: Noted for extending isolation dynamics to human groups, paralleling the island model.

## Key Takeaways

- **Equilibrium defined**: Opposing forces (immigration vs. extinction) balance at a crossing point.
- **Turnover is normal**: Identity of species changes even when the total count stabilizes.
- **Steep curves = fewer species**: Sharp extinction rise or sharp immigration decline shifts equilibrium toward zero.
- **High extinction + low immigration = impoverished ecosystem**: A simple but powerful predictive rule.
- **Size matters**: Larger island → more species, more resilience.
- **Distance matters**: More remote island → lower immigration → fewer species, more fragile.
- **Cross-domain relevance**: Equilibrium logic applies to business competition, cognitive balance, and organizational isolation.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Equilibrium Thinking]]**: Every stable system has opposing forces in balance — identifying what the "immigration" and "extinction" equivalents are in any domain reveals fragility and intervention points.
- **[[Red Queen Effect]]**: Organisms (or businesses) must continuously evolve just to maintain their position; failure to keep pace with co-evolution leads to local extinction — the island model makes this dynamic visible in population terms.
- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: A steep extinction curve doesn't just reduce species count today; it shifts the equilibrium point itself, compounding fragility — a second-order consequence invisible from first-order observation alone.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of MacArthur and Wilson's Theory of Island Biogeography?
**A**: Island species counts reach equilibrium through the ongoing balance of immigration and local extinction — not slow speciation — and this equilibrium point is determined by island size and remoteness.

**Q2**: What is "turnover" in the context of island equilibrium, and why does it matter?
**A**: Turnover is the continual replacement of species — one arrives as another goes extinct — such that the total species count stays near equilibrium even though the roster constantly changes.

**Q3**: How can the island equilibrium model be applied outside biology?
**A**: The same logic applies to businesses ([[Red Queen Effect]]), isolated communities ([[Jared Diamond]]), and mental/physical balance — any system where immigration of new inputs and extinction of old elements must stay balanced to avoid impoverishment.
