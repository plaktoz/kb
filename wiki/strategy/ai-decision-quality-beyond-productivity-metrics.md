## Summary
Judging AI by hours saved or tasks automated measures efficiency, not effectiveness, and a company can save thousands of hours yet decide worse. The author argues AI should sharpen human judgment rather than replace it, and proposes adding decision quality, employee capability, and clear accountability to the AI scorecard.

## Core Concepts
- [[AI + Human Hybrid Workforce]]: AI handles routine work; people own exceptions, context, and accountability.
- [[Decision Quality]] versus speed: faster answers are not better decisions unless they surface risks or challenge assumptions.
- [[Productivity Metrics]] such as hours saved show workload change, not work quality.
- [[Critical Thinking]] and subject-matter expertise: needed to judge whether polished AI output is right.
- [[Accountability]] in AI-assisted decisions: someone must be named as verifier or challenger.
- [[Stanford Human-AI Accounting Study]]: over 200,000 transactions; errors spiked when accountants deferred to the system over industry standards.
- [[Human-in-the-Loop]] boundaries: leaders define what AI handles, when people intervene, and who is responsible.

## Key Takeaways
- **Efficiency vs. Effectiveness**: Hours saved say nothing about whether decisions improved.
- **Three Added Questions**: Did the decision improve, did the employee grow, is accountability clear?
- **Stanford Evidence**: AI raised accuracy overall, but errors spiked when accountants deferred to it.
- **Exceptions Need Humans**: Context, relationship stakes, and accountability decide off-process cases like urgent client orders.
- **No Rubber Stamps**: Employees need to know why review matters, hold authority, and know when to escalate.
- **Expertise Gains Value**: Polished AI output makes it harder to spot weak reasoning without domain knowledge.
- **Ask a Better Question**: Replace "what can we automate?" with "what problem should no longer exist?"

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Proxy metrics can diverge from the real goal** — Measuring hours saved or tasks completed tracks workload, not decision quality, so optimizing the proxy can leave the actual outcome worse. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
→ explains: Efficiency vs. Effectiveness, Three Added Questions

**T2. Deferring to a system without expertise degrades judgment** — Overreliance on AI erodes critical thinking, and people lacking expertise cannot tell when a plausible answer is wrong. · *model:* [[Automation Bias]]
→ explains: Stanford Evidence, Expertise Gains Value

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the article's central claim about measuring AI value?
**A**: Metrics like hours saved and tasks automated measure efficiency, not effectiveness. A company can save thousands of hours and still make worse decisions, so AI should be judged on whether decisions improve.

**Q2**: What evidence does the author cite that deferring to AI can hurt outcomes?
**A**: A 2026 Stanford study of over 200,000 accounting transactions found AI raised overall classification accuracy, but errors spiked when accountants deferred to the system over established industry standards.

**Q3**: What three questions should executives add to their AI scorecard?
**A**: Did the decision improve, did the employee become more capable, and is accountability still clear? Leaders should also define which decisions AI handles, when people intervene, and who takes responsibility.
