## Summary

Critical mass (also called tipping point or percolation threshold) describes the threshold at which something becomes prevalent enough to sustain its own growth. The concept originated in nuclear physics and was later applied to sociology, business, and psychology to explain S-curve adoption dynamics. Research suggests that once committed opinion holders exceed roughly 10% of a population, ideas spread rapidly and become self-reinforcing.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Critical Mass]]** — the minimum threshold needed for a system to become self-sustaining, borrowed from nuclear physics where it describes the minimum fissile material for a chain reaction
- **[[Tipping Point]]** — popularized by [[Malcolm Gladwell]], the moment when a social phenomenon crosses the threshold into rapid, exponential spread
- **[[Thomas Schelling]]** and [[Mark Granovetter]] — collaborators who formalized the concept for social science in 1971
- **[[Morton Grodzins]]** — first applied critical mass to sociology in the 1960s, studying racial segregation and "white flight" feedback loops
- **[[Network Effects]]** — the mechanism by which value increases with adoption, making the critical mass threshold pivotal for platforms
- **[[S-Curve Dynamics]]** — the characteristic pattern of slow accumulation, rapid acceleration after threshold, then saturation
- **[[Law of the Few]]** — Gladwell's observation that spread depends on Connectors (vast networks), Mavens (information specialists), and Salesmen (charismatic persuaders)
- **[[Stickiness Factor]]** — how memorable and compelling something is, governing both adoption speed and rate of decline
- **[[Nassim Taleb]]** — coined "f*** you money" as a metaphor for personal financial critical mass; also described the "Dictatorship of the Minority" whereby a committed minority can shift social norms
- **[[Gustav Le Bon]]** — identified three crowd psychology processes (anonymity, contagion, suggestibility) that generate collective critical mass

## Key Takeaways

- **Origin in physics**: Critical mass = minimum fissile material for a self-sustaining nuclear chain reaction.
- **Sociology first**: Morton Grodzins observed threshold dynamics in neighborhood "white flight" feedback loops.
- **PayPal case study**: Peter Thiel paid users $10 to join, deliberately manufacturing the critical user threshold.
- **10% Rule**: RPI research found ideas spread "like flame" once committed believers exceed 10% of a population.
- **Gladwell's three drivers**: Law of the Few, Stickiness Factor, and Context must align for a tipping point.
- **Taleb minority rule**: A sufficiently committed minority can impose preferences on a passive majority.
- **S-curve universal**: Virality, tech adoption, disease spread, and revolutions all follow slow → rapid → plateau arcs.
- **China 1989**: Individual student protests reached critical mass, sparking demonstrations across 300+ cities.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Feedback Loops]]**: Below the threshold, adoption decays; above it, each new adopter makes the next one more likely — the core first-principles mechanism that generates all tipping-point phenomena.
- **[[Schelling Points]]**: Critical mass thresholds often act as focal points around which coordination suddenly becomes easy, explaining why small incremental gains can trigger discontinuous jumps.
- **[[Power Laws]]**: The non-linear acceleration after the threshold reflects an underlying power-law distribution of influence, where a small number of highly connected nodes (Connectors, Mavens) disproportionately drive spread.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core claim of the critical mass concept?
**A**: A system crosses a threshold at which adoption becomes self-sustaining — below it the phenomenon fizzles, above it it accelerates exponentially without further external input.

**Q2**: What does the Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute research say about the 10% rule?
**A**: Once committed opinion holders exceed 10% of a population, ideas spread rapidly "like flame"; below that threshold, adoption is negligibly slow.

**Q3**: How did PayPal deliberately engineer critical mass, and what does this imply strategically?
**A**: Peter Thiel paid new users $10 to join, subsidizing early adoption until the network was large enough to become self-valuable — implying that manufactured momentum can substitute for organic growth when the threshold is known.
