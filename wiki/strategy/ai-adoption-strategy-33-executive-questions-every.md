## Summary

Every's consulting team synthesized 33 of the most common AI questions from 400 executives, covering skeptics, governance, tool selection, and organizational restructuring. The central thesis is that AI is "a tool, not a strategy" — it should serve an existing roadmap rather than replace it. Organizations succeed by building fluency incrementally, focusing on champions over skeptics, and measuring concrete cycle-time and quality metrics rather than vague productivity proxies.

## Core Concepts

- **[[AI Adoption Levels]]**: Eight levels of AI adoption exist; most workers remain at levels 1–2 (basic [[ChatGPT]] use). Moving workers up requires building with their *real* work, not abstract training.
- **[[AI Champions]]**: Peer demonstration drives organic adoption — focusing on AI champions is more effective than converting skeptics.
- **[[AI Governance]]**: Distinguishing low-risk writing tools from high-risk database-access systems; [[IT]] as a design partner, not gatekeeper. AI can *increase* transparency in regulated environments via automated logs.
- **[[Token Economics]]**: Use frontier models for novel/complex tasks; optimize to smaller models for repetitive, well-defined tasks.
- **[[Knowledge Debt]]**: AI exposes documentation gaps ("knowledge debt") rather than fixing them. Requires assigning owners and freshness dates to a curated set of 10–20 high-priority sources.
- **[[Platform Lock-in]]**: Recommending a single AI platform per org to avoid switching costs — retraining staff and rewriting prompts/skills when switching.

## Key Takeaways

- **Two productivity types**: doing existing work faster vs. pursuing previously impossible work.
- **Early wins**: "Boring and frequent" tasks — meeting prep, summaries, inbox triage, structured tables.
- **Training approach**: Build something useful with *real* work immediately; avoid abstract AI training.
- **Tool selection**: Novices don't need cutting-edge tools — being a month or two behind the frontier is fine.
- **Governance split**: Separate low-risk writing tools from high-risk database-access systems in policy.
- **Measurement**: No single metric; track cycle time, error rates, rework frequency, NPS, and skill reuse rates.
- **Baseline first**: Establish pre-AI baseline before deployment, then track actual delta.
- **Ownership model**: Both central AI team *and* distributed business-team ownership are necessary.
- **Rollout sequence**: Start with executives, then 2–3 teams with painful, high-value repetitive work (200–500 person org).
- **Code vs. writing**: Code generation advanced faster than writing quality — contrary to earlier expectations.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Goodhart's Law]]**: Without a pre-AI baseline and specific outcome metrics, teams optimize for visible AI usage rather than actual results — the exact failure mode the authors warn against by insisting on cycle time and error-rate tracking.
- **[[Activation Energy]]**: Focusing on AI champions rather than skeptics lowers the organizational activation energy for adoption — early peer demonstrations reduce the perceived barrier for the rest of the org.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the central claim about AI's role in organizational strategy?
**A**: AI is "a tool, not a strategy" — it should augment an existing roadmap rather than substitute for one. Organizations that treat AI as a strategy in itself risk misaligning effort.

**Q2**: What are the "eight levels of AI adoption" and where do most workers currently sit?
**A**: The framework identifies eight adoption levels, and most employees remain at levels 1–2, characterized by basic use of tools like ChatGPT without deeper workflow integration.

**Q3**: How should organizations approach AI governance to balance risk and transparency?
**A**: Keep IT as a design partner and distinguish low-risk writing tools from high-risk database-access systems. AI can actually *increase* transparency in regulated environments through activity logs and automated reporting.
