## Summary

Newport revisits the [[Jevons Paradox]] — the 1865 finding that making a resource more efficient tends to increase, not decrease, its total consumption — and applies it optimistically to AI's effect on jobs: cheaper, faster AI output could expand overall demand for services like software development rather than shrink employment. He then turns to the paradox's "darker side," using steam-engine pollution and digital-communication overload as historical cases where efficiency gains produced serious unintended costs. He closes hoping the positive version holds for AI and labor, while cautioning that efficiency improvements often carry hidden costs.

## Core Concepts

- [[Jevons Paradox]]: efficiency gains in a resource tend to increase total consumption rather than reduce it, since new uses emerge
- [[William Stanley Jevons]]: 19th-century economist whose book on England's coal supply first documented the effect
- AI and the [[Labor Market]]: the optimistic reading — cheaper AI-driven output could grow, not shrink, demand for services like software development
- [[Steam Engine]] efficiency: historical example where gains fueled pollution and the origins of industrial climate impact
- Digital communication tools ([[Email]], [[Slack]]): made messaging so efficient that they fueled constant workplace interruption

## Key Takeaways

- **Historical origin**: Jevons (1865) found coal efficiency gains increased, not reduced, coal use.
- **AI optimism**: Cheaper AI output could expand, not shrink, demand for services like coding.
- **Podcast pointer**: Newport cites Derek Thompson and Alex Imas for more on the AI/labor angle.
- **Dark side #1**: Steam engine efficiency contributed to pollution and early industrial climate impact.
- **Dark side #2**: Efficient digital messaging (email, Slack) fueled constant interruption.
- **Interruption statistic**: Microsoft research cited shows workers interrupted roughly every two minutes.
- **Newport's stance**: Hopeful for AI and labor, but warns efficiency gains often carry hidden costs.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Second-Order Thinking]]**: The direct, first-order effect of efficiency (less input needed per unit of output) looks purely positive, but Newport's steam-engine and messaging examples show the second-order effect — expanded total usage — can dominate and turn net negative.

**A**: He references Microsoft research showing knowledge workers are now interrupted roughly every two minutes.
