## Summary

Drawing on [[Charlie Munger]]'s concept of "competitive destruction," industries are repeatedly disrupted by new waves of innovation, and those who fail to ride these waves — like Kodak with digital photography — suffer permanent decline. Early movers capture compounding advantages unavailable to latecomers. Success at the wave requires vision to spot it early, courage to act before others, and skill to time entry correctly.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Competitive Destruction]]**: [[Charlie Munger]]'s framework describing how established industries collapse when superior technology arrives, redistributing advantages to newcomers.
- **[[Innovation Waves]]**: Recurring cycles of disruption where new technology renders incumbents obsolete — e.g., [[Kodak]] inventing digital photography in 1975 but failing to commercialize it.
- **[[First Mover Advantage]]**: Early movers in a new wave compound their gains while laggards stagnate, making timing a strategic lever, not just a tactical one.
- **[[Strategic Vision]]**: The capacity to identify an emerging wave before the mainstream consensus forms — a precondition for acting with courage and correct timing.

## Key Takeaways

- **Kodak Failure**: Invented digital photography in 1975; refused to cannibalize its film business.
- **Munger's Warning**: New entrants get "huge advantages"; incumbents get "mired in shallows."
- **Three Requirements**: Vision (spot the wave), courage (move before others), skill (time entry).
- **Cost of Missing**: Competitors compound advantages while you remain stagnant.
- **Wave Dynamics**: Early momentum sustains itself; late entry yields diminishing returns.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Competitive Destruction]]**: [[Charlie Munger]]'s named pattern explains why incumbents systematically fail — their economic moat becomes a cage that prevents adaptation to the new wave.
- **[[Opportunity Cost]]**: Kodak's decision to protect film revenue was not neutral; every year of delay handed compounding advantages to digital-native competitors, making inaction a costly choice disguised as caution.
- **[[Timing as Strategy]]**: Wave-riding reframes timing from luck to skill — the model illuminates why being early (not just right) is the actual source of durable competitive advantage.
