## Summary

Sahil Bloom contrasts [[Henry Ford]]'s ruthless production focus with [[Alfred Sloan]]'s systems-driven organizational design, showing that each approach was right for its stage. The core lesson is that the mindset enabling breakthrough creation often becomes a cage at scale — and that founders must evolve from Ford-style control to Sloan-style adaptability. The framework applies equally to business, health, relationships, and money.

## Core Concepts

- **[[Henry Ford]]**: Inventor of the [[Ford Quadricycle]] who built the [[Model T]] empire by treating the factory itself as the product — obsessing over efficiency, reducing the Model T's price from $850 to $260, and capturing ~60% of the U.S. auto market by the early 1920s.
- **[[Alfred Sloan]]**: Took over [[General Motors]] in 1923 and treated the *company itself as the product* — decentralized decision-making, clear divisions of labor, data tracking, and strategic financial controls roughly doubled GM's market share by 1925.
- **[[Founder's Paradox]]**: The strengths that build a zero-to-one breakthrough (centralized control, relentless focus) become liabilities at scale; adaptability and systems thinking are required to cross the chasm.
- **[[Organizational Design]]**: Sloan's structural innovations — decentralization, divisional clarity, financial controls — enabled [[General Motors]] to surpass Ford by 1927.
- **[[Adaptability]]**: The meta-skill of recognizing which mode (Ford or Sloan) the current context demands, then switching deliberately.

## Key Takeaways

- **Ford's Insight**: Treat the factory as the product — production efficiency beats product variety at the zero-to-one stage.
- **Sloan's Insight**: Treat the company as the product — decentralized systems beat centralized control at scale.
- **Sequencing Matters**: Ford's approach enabled the breakthrough; Sloan's enabled the empire — neither works in the wrong order.
- **Rigidity as Risk**: Ford's refusal to adapt (destroying a prototype himself) turned his greatest strength into a fatal constraint.
- **Universal Pattern**: Health, relationships, and money all follow the same arc — intense focus early, adaptability later.
- **Diagnosis Questions**: Ask what single change drives maximum impact, what decision you're avoiding, and what a world-class CEO would change in 100 days.

## 🧠 First Principles & Mental Models

- **[[Stage-Gate Thinking]]**: Different phases of a system's lifecycle require fundamentally different operating modes — forcing an early-stage tool onto a late-stage problem (or vice versa) produces failure, not efficiency.
- **[[Chesterton's Fence]]**: Ford attacked the prototype because he couldn't see why adaptability was needed; questioning existing constraints only makes sense once you understand why they were built — and when they no longer serve their original purpose.
- **[[Survivorship Bias]]**: Founders who succeeded with Ford-mode control tend to over-index on it, ignoring the many ventures that failed by not transitioning to Sloan-mode at scale.

## 🃏 Review Questions

**Q1**: What is the core argument of "Be a Ford, then a Sloan"?
**A**: The mindset that creates a breakthrough (Ford's centralized, efficiency-obsessed control) is different from the mindset that scales one (Sloan's decentralized, systems-oriented design) — and success requires recognizing when to transition between the two.

**Q2**: What specific evidence shows Sloan's approach outperformed Ford's at scale?
**A**: After Sloan took over GM in 1923, GM's market share roughly doubled by 1925, and by 1927 Chevrolet outsold the Model T — while Ford had held ~60% of the U.S. auto market just years earlier.

**Q3**: How can a founder apply this framework in practice?
**A**: Diagnose operational blind spots and habits that once served but now constrain, and regularly ask: what single change drives the greatest impact, what decision am I avoiding, and what would a world-class CEO change in the first 100 days?
