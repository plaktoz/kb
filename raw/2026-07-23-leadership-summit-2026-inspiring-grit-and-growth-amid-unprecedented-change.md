# Leadership Summit 2026: Inspiring Grit and Growth Amid Unprecedented Change

source_url: https://hbr.org/podcast/2026/07/leadership-summit-2026-inspiring-grit-and-growth-amid-unprecedented-change

---

HBR IdeaCast, with Angela Duckworth (University of Pennsylvania) — July 23, 2026

Duckworth's research on grit — passion and perseverance for long-term goals — argues you should be "stubborn at the top" (a fixed top-level goal) but "agile at the bottom" (flexible day-to-day tactics), including in how you use AI. Citing cognitive scientist Anders Ericsson's work on learning curves, she argues the ability to stay on a learning curve, more than raw talent, separates high achievers.

Survey data collected with the Walton Family Foundation and Gallup found three out of four Gen Z young adults (18–28) had used AI in the last month as of October, primarily to be more productive — but with deep ambivalence: they worry AI use is a crutch that will make them lazier and less sharp over time.

## The cover-letter experiment

Duckworth's then-graduate-student Benjamin Lira ran an experiment: participants rewrote a poor cover letter either unaided, using an AI tool, or with no practice at all. The expected result was that AI-assisted writers would produce better letters in the moment but show diminished personal writing skill afterward. Instead, the AI-assisted group's own writing skill improved enduringly — a day and a week later — more than either the unaided-practice or no-practice groups.

The explanation: using AI reduces effort (fewer keystrokes, less time), which should reduce learning — and does, in isolation. But AI also improves the quality of the learning environment by demonstrating, through example, what a better output looks like — the same mechanism as having a great coach. Duckworth invokes UCLA coach John Wooden's four-step model: explanation, demonstration, imitation, repetition. Learners who "watch and learn" from AI's output, rather than simply accepting it, can come out ahead even while working less.

Duckworth's prescription: delegate the tedious, low-learning-curve tasks at the bottom of your goal hierarchy to AI, freeing time for higher-level work; treat every AI interaction as a chance to ask "could I learn something from this?"; and recognize that ambivalence about AI is often a rational response to real accountability gaps and hallucination risk, not a lack of grit. On situational agency — designing your environment rather than relying on willpower — she points to Michael Phelps: elite performance came not just from personal grit but from an exceptional coach (Bob Bowman), training facility, teammates, and culture that Phelps deliberately sought out and stayed close to.
