# The Pomodoro Technique: How 25-Minute Work Blocks Transform Your Focus

The Pomodoro Technique is a time management method developed by Francesco Cirillo in the late 1980s: work for 25 minutes without interruption, then take a 5-minute break; every four cycles, take a longer 15-30 minute break. Cirillo named it after the tomato-shaped kitchen timer he used as a student.

**Why it works:** Fixed 25-minute sessions lower the activation energy for starting a task — you're not committing to finishing the report, just to 25 minutes on it, which is usually enough to get started and dissolve resistance. Breaks protect sustained concentration, since attention degrades over hours of uninterrupted effort; a 5-minute reset keeps session four nearly as sharp as session one. Time pressure also sharpens focus per Parkinson's Law — a visible ticking clock makes the cost of distraction (email, tangential research) more apparent.

**The four-step process:** (1) Choose a single, specific task rather than a vague goal. (2) Set a 25-minute timer. (3) Work until the timer rings without stopping — if a distraction arises, note it quickly and return; stopping for more than a few minutes means restarting from zero. (4) Take a 5-minute break, then repeat; after four Pomodoros, take a longer break.

**Handling interruptions:** The session is deliberately indivisible. For non-urgent interruptions, Cirillo recommends "inform, negotiate, schedule, call back" — tell the person you're mid-session, agree on a time, capture it as a task, and return to work.

**Common mistakes:** working on too many tasks in one session (fragments focus), skipping breaks (accelerates fatigue over a full day), and starting a timer without a planned task (wastes the session's early minutes on deciding what to do).

**Adapting the method:** 25 minutes is a starting point, not a rule — deep writing or complex programming may benefit from 45-50 minute sessions, tested only after establishing the base habit for at least a week. Pairs naturally with time-blocking: schedule two or three Pomodoros as a calendar focus window, keeping deep work protected while remaining flexible for meetings and lighter work.
