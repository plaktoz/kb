# Staff Work, Part 2: The Small Stuff Is Still Part of Staff Work

source_url: https://zixianchen.com/blog/staff-work-small-stuff-is-still-part-of-staff-work

---

This is the second of three posts on staff work — the first covers thinking through an issue and communicating upwards; this one covers producing the actual package: papers, emails, slides, and records.

## A. Papers and submissions

**Put the inconvenient stuff in the main paper.** Any fact, uncertainty, or trade-off that could change a decision belongs in the main paper, not buried in an annex because it makes the recommendation less tidy. No one should discover on page 38 that the whole proposal depends on an untested assumption. The same applies to slides — don't bury a critical point in small font at a deep indentation level (the Columbia space shuttle disaster is cited as a cautionary example of this failure mode).

**Use a cover note when the paper is hard to start with.** A short cover note or executive summary helps for long submissions that have moved through several levels or changed significantly since the last version — it should explain why it's coming up now, what changed, what's recommended, and where the difficult part is, without becoming a mini copy of the whole submission.

**Write the approval paragraph literally.** State exactly what's being approved, itemized if there are multiple separate commitments (e.g., "(a) the proposed six-month extension; (b) the additional funding of $X; and (c) the revised implementation date of Y"), since people may copy the paragraph straight into meeting minutes or decision records.

**Be clear what kind of response you want.** "Approval," "information," and "endorsement" cover most cases — the recipient should know whether they're expected to decide, support, or just be aware.

## B. Emails and clearance

**Make the subject line do work.** A useful subject line shows the next person in the clearance chain, the final approving authority, any deadline, and the actual subject — e.g. "[For PS' Approval by 18 Jul] ABC Pilot Proposal." Generic subjects like "For approval pls" become useless in a crowded inbox.

**Give PAs a complete request** when booking meetings: duration, topic, attendees, venue, and whether the meeting is already approved.

**Say whether materials are coming**, and when — otherwise the meeting owner spends days wondering if something is late.

**Learn the local house style.** Different departments have different preferences for designations vs. names, numbering, sign-off conventions, footnotes, fonts, date formats, abbreviations, and annex usage — learn what the receiving office uses rather than importing conventions from a previous posting.

**Sign off submissions properly**, matching the local house practice rather than informal email conventions.

## C. Slides

Slides are part of staff work — a nice deck helps, but weak thinking still shows through, and a poor deck can bury a useful argument.

**Start with four questions:** What problem are we facing? What's causing it and how do we know? What would success look like? How will we solve it? Too many decks jump straight to solution details — timelines, workstreams, org charts — before the room has agreed on the problem.

**Move from big picture to detail:** context, principles/objectives, problem, research/data, solution, trade-offs/assessment, implementation/timelines — not a rigid template, but a way to avoid throwing people into detail before they know the frame.

**Make the title say something.** "Scope," "Background," and "Assessment" tell the audience what category a slide belongs to, not what it says. A title like "Current approval process adds six weeks without changing the risk" states the actual point.

**Read only the titles**, top to bottom, to check whether the argument holds together — if titles read like a table of contents, the deck is organized by topic instead of by argument.

**Ask "so what?" of every slide** and **"why?" of every visual** — a slide can be accurate and still contribute almost nothing.

**Try ten slides or fewer** for an elevator pitch, forcing the key messages to surface before supporting material is added — while still knowing far more supporting detail than the ten slides show, since senior people often skip straight to the thing they care about.

**How to explain a slide:** point, elaboration, example, return to the point. **Orient the room before explaining a complex visual** — tell people how to read rows, columns, and where to look, and give them a few seconds before diving into the conclusion. **Watch the room's body language**, and **check multi-screen setups beforehand** to avoid a presenter pointing at something the audience can't see.

## D. Writing and records

Use short sentences without writing like a telegram; bold only what genuinely matters; use simple tenses and active voice; keep records-of-discussion verbs plain ("said," "replied," "questioned," "added") rather than rotating through "highlighted," "opined," "queried" for variety, since a record is not a transcript or creative writing. Use ordinary words over complex ones. Common language peeves noted: "with regard to" not "with regards to"; "reply" not "revert"; "comprises" not "comprises of"; "apprise" (inform) vs. "appraise" (assess); "leverage" something, don't "leverage on" it; keep terminology, abbreviations, dates and times consistent throughout a document; and use full words in formal writing rather than "pls," "tks," and contractions.

## E. Before sending

**Read it backwards**, sentence by sentence, to catch missing words and repeated phrases that the brain otherwise autocorrects while reading normally. **Leave it and come back** — even ten minutes helps for urgent work. **Check the actual package**: correct version and annexes attached, correct addressees and classification, working cross-references and page numbers, PDF conversion not broken, comments and tracked changes handled properly, and confirming the exact attached file (not just the one reviewed) is correct.
