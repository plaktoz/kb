# AI Strategy Review Q4 2026: How the 150 Largest Companies Approach AI

The AI Strategy Index tracks how the 150 largest companies in the US and Europe, the S&P 100 and the STOXX Europe 50, set out, own and measure AI. This is our first quarterly AI Strategy Review, with figures as of November 24, 2026.

The widest gap is between why these companies want AI and what they can show for it. 91 of the 150 (61%) give efficiency as a reason for AI. Only 13 (9%) report a company-wide money figure for what AI delivers in their own business.

## The Key Numbers

15% publish a standalone AI strategy (22 of 150). For another 32%, the strategy can only be inferred from public sources.

61% cite efficiency as a reason for AI, 48% cite growth and customers, and 25% want to change how the whole company runs.

58% point AI at operations and 57% at AI tools for staff in general. 44% build it into what they sell.

61% use or plan AI agents, and 51% have their own named AI tool for staff.

29% give AI tools to all or nearly all staff, but only 12% require AI training. 14% show a company-wide fluency floor, all at Level 1, Chatting.

29% link job cuts, smaller teams or slower hiring to AI.

9% report a company-wide money figure for AI's value. Measurement and value is the most common gap between what these companies say and what they show, at 44%.

24% of companies where the CEO or a data and AI chief owns AI report a money figure, against 3% of the rest. None of the 28 companies with a dedicated AI leader do.

45% of companies that publish an AI strategy set a fluency floor, against 9% of the rest.

52% of technology companies have no stated AI strategy for their own business, against 28% across the other industries.

## The 150 Companies Behind These Numbers

Together, the 150 employ 20.8 million people and report $13.4 trillion in annual revenue. The median company has about 78,000 employees and $53 billion in revenue, based on each company's latest fiscal year.

The mix differs sharply by region. The US half is heavy in technology, with 24 of the 27 technology companies. Europe has a larger share of banks, insurers and industrial companies.

Headcount has barely moved while revenue has grown. Over each company's last four reported years, median headcount rose 1% and median revenue rose 16%, and 58 of the 150 now employ fewer people. We show this as context only: most of the period predates company-wide AI, and the Index does not link it to AI.

## 1. Where They Are Going: Efficiency First, Growth Second

Efficiency is the reason the 150 give most often, and the one they most often open with. 91 companies (61%) cite doing more with the same or fewer people, and 52 lead with it. Growth and better service for customers follow, cited by 72 (48%) and led by 33.

*Chart: 61% of the 150 cite efficiency as a reason for AI. Share of the 150 citing each reason, and how many lead with it (cites the reason / leads with it).*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. Reasons coded twice, with disagreements settled by a third review.*

Most strategies give two or more reasons. 53 companies name three or more, 50 name two and 39 name one. 48 cite efficiency without growth, 43 cite both and 29 cite growth without efficiency. Eight profiles state no clear reason at all.

A quarter want to change how the whole company runs. 37 companies (25%) describe a new operating model or call themselves AI-first or AI-native, and 25 lead with it, from Amazon, JPMorgan and Goldman Sachs to Santander, Sanofi and Zurich. Our guide to AI native organizations shows what that change asks of a company.

Risk and research speed are mostly second reasons. 38 companies (25%) name risk, safety or quality, but only 7 lead with it; banks (11) and industrial companies (8) cite it most. 25 (17%) want faster research or engineering, and 13 of them are in healthcare and pharma.

Four smaller reasons point to where AI is heading:

- Customers arriving through AI agents (16 companies, 11%): mostly banks, payments and retail, including Walmart, Visa, Mastercard, Booking Holdings, Unilever and L'Oréal.
- Competitive pressure (16, 11%): an explicit fear of falling behind or a race to lead, from Boeing and Oracle to BBVA and UniCredit.
- AI as a market for the core business (13, 9%): mostly energy companies selling power to data centers, such as NextEra, Iberdrola, Shell and TotalEnergies.
- Customer zero (8, 5%): companies that sell AI and use it on themselves first, including Microsoft, Salesforce, IBM and SAP.

The lead reason changes by industry. Efficiency leads in technology (12 of 27) and banking (9 of 24), with growth close behind in banking (8). Growth leads in retail and consumer goods (8 of 22). Healthcare splits three ways between operating model (6), efficiency (5) and research speed (5).

## 2. Where They Focus: Operations and Everyday Work Lead

Operations and everyday staff tools top the list. 87 companies (58%) point AI at operations, factories, supply chains or field work, and 86 (57%) at assistants for staff in general. The median company names four focus areas.

*Chart: Operations and staff tools are where most companies point AI. Share of the 150 naming each function as an AI focus area.*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. Focus areas coded from each profile.*

AI in the product is the third focus. 66 companies (44%) build AI into what they sell, and 64 (43%) aim it at software engineering. Customer service (50, 33%) and research and development (46, 31%) come last.

Each industry points AI at its core work:

- Banks: software engineering and staff tools (19 of 24 each), then customer service (15).
- Healthcare and pharma: operations (16 of 22) and research and development (15).
- Technology: software engineering (19 of 27), then staff tools (16).
- Retail and consumer goods: operations (16 of 22), then sales and marketing (13).
- Energy and utilities: operations (11 of 12).

## 3. What It Takes: A Written Strategy and a Clear Owner

Only 22 of the 150 (15%) publish a standalone AI strategy. Another 80 (53%) set out AI inside their corporate strategy, and for 48 (32%) we can only infer the strategy from public sources.

Not publishing an AI strategy does not mean a company has none. Many companies keep theirs internal. For every company, we looked for evidence of a strategy in annual reports, investor days, earnings calls and company statements, even where we could not read the strategy itself. We welcome corrections: if we have missed something, contact us.

The companies that sell AI say least about their own. 14 of the 27 technology companies (52%) have no stated AI strategy for their own business, against 28% of the other 123. Nvidia, Apple, Alphabet and Oracle are among them. Banks sit at the other end, with 4 of 24.

### Ownership

Ownership is spread across five kinds of owner. The CEO and a dedicated AI leader each own AI at 28 companies. Most often, though, it sits with another technology or business executive, usually a chief information, technology or digital officer.

At Johnson & Johnson, the chief information officer leads the AI work, and at Bristol Myers Squibb it is the chief digital and technology officer. Some companies look outside technology: PepsiCo gives the AI agenda to its chief strategy and transformation officer, and Union Pacific has its head of operations lead AI in running the railroad.

Industries choose different owners. Banks favor dedicated AI leaders (9 of 24) and data and AI chiefs (6). In telecom and media the CEO owns AI at 6 of 9 companies, while healthcare leaves it with a technology or other executive at 12 of 22. European companies leave AI without a single owner three times as often (22% against 7%).

## 4. What It Takes: Tools Arrive Before Training

AI agents and in-house tools are now common, and training lags behind them. 91 companies (61%) use or plan AI agents in their own operations, and 77 (51%) have their own named AI tool for staff. Only 18 (12%) require AI training.

*Chart: 61% use AI agents, but only 12% require AI training. Share of the 150 with each commitment in their public record.*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. Coded from each profile's public record.*

Tools often go out without training. 43 companies (29%) give AI tools to all or nearly all staff, and 16 of them name no AI training program at all. 69 (46%) name some training or upskilling program, but only 21 show what we call a fluency floor: a company-wide baseline set by required training, tools for everyone or near-universal use. 14 of the 21 back it with required training.

The fluency floor is low. All 21 sit at Level 1, Chatting, on our AI Fluency Matrix, the level where AI helps with isolated tasks. Citi, for example, required about 175,000 employees to complete training in prompting (Fortune).

Banks and European companies push training hardest. 7 of 24 banks require AI training, and European companies require it more than twice as often as US companies (20% against 8%). None of the 27 technology companies has a published AI training requirement.

Companies that publish an AI strategy invest more in their people. Half of them give AI tools to all staff, against a quarter of the rest. They are also four times as likely to require training and five times as likely to set a fluency floor.

*Chart: Companies that publish an AI strategy invest more in their people. Publish an AI strategy (22) vs. all other companies (128).*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026.*

Three in ten link AI to jobs. 44 companies (29%) link job cuts, smaller teams or slower hiring to AI, by their own account or in cited reports. The link follows the reason: 42% of companies that cite efficiency make it, against 10% of the rest. By count, banks (13 of 24) and technology companies (11 of 27) link AI to jobs most; energy and utilities companies never do (0 of 12).

Money and suppliers are less visible. 31 companies (21%) name an investment figure for AI or for technology that includes AI. On suppliers, 56 of the 150 have a company account with OpenAI and 69 with Anthropic, based on the public records behind their web addresses on November 21 (more in our guide on how to choose your company's AI platform).

## 5. How They Know It Is Working: Few Put a Number on It

Only 13 of the 150 (9%) report a company-wide money figure, delivered or targeted, for what AI brings their own business. A few more, such as Microsoft, Verizon and Schneider Electric, put a figure on one function without giving a company total.

Most report results without adding them up. 89 (59%) report results with no company total, 18 (12%) report only usage or activity, such as users or use cases, and 30 (20%) report no AI figures for their own work.

*Chart: Only 13 of the 150 report a company-wide money figure for AI. How the 150 report the value of AI in their own business.*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. Money figures are company-claimed.*

The few with a figure set the target first. IBM set a $2 billion productivity goal in 2023 and now reports $4.5 billion in productivity gains from AI and automation (IBM, company-claimed).

Bank of America expects $250 million to $300 million in AI benefits in 2026, and each of its AI projects in production has a defined payback (AIM Media House, company-claimed).

Money figures cluster in a few industries. Technology companies report 5 of the 13 (IBM, ServiceNow, Salesforce, SAP and Intel), banks 3 and energy companies 2. No company in manufacturing or retail reports one. 12 of the 13 cite efficiency as a reason for AI.

Measurement and value is the most common gap between what companies say and what they show, at 66 of the 150 (44%). People and jobs comes next, at 33.

*Chart: Measurement and value is the biggest gap for 44% of the 150. The biggest gap between what each company says about AI and what it shows.*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. Two companies are not yet rated.*

Each gap has its own pattern. The transparency gap belongs to the companies that say least: 22 of its 30 have a strategy we can only infer. 22 of the 33 companies with a people and jobs gap link job cuts or slower hiring to AI, with banks (9) and technology companies (8) the largest groups.

When the CEO or a data and AI chief owns AI, the money figure shows up. 10 of those 42 companies (24%) report one, against 3 of the other 105 (3%). None of the 28 companies with a dedicated AI leader does.

*Chart: A money figure shows up when the CEO or a data and AI chief owns AI. Share that report a company-wide money figure for AI, by who owns AI. All rated companies: 9%.*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. 147 companies with an owner rating.*

Both kinds of owner set a number and report against it. Deutsche Telekom, where the CEO owns AI, set a target of about €2.5 billion in savings on indirect costs from AI and automation by 2030, at its AI investor day in October 2026.

At Banco Santander, where the chief data and AI officer owns AI, the bank reported €35 million in business value from AI in the first quarter of 2026, against a target of more than €1 billion over 2026 to 2028 (Santander, company-claimed).

The pattern holds outside technology. (Four of the ten companies with a figure sell AI themselves. Without technology companies, the gap is 17% against 2%.)

Money figures also go with broad deployment. 8 of the 13 give AI tools to all staff (62%, against 26% of the rest), 12 use AI agents (92% against 58%) and 7 link AI to jobs (54% against 27%). Companies that publish an AI strategy report a figure more often too (23% against 6%).

## 6. The US and Europe Compared: Different Bets, Same Value Gap

US companies aim AI at software and products. European companies invest more in training and floors. On the result that matters most, they are close: 8% of US and 10% of European companies report a money figure.

*Chart: The US builds, Europe trains, and both rarely report value (US, S&P 100 vs. Europe, STOXX Europe 50).*

*Source: AI Strategy Index, 150 counted companies (S&P 100 and STOXX Europe 50), as of November 24, 2026. The no-owner share uses the 97 US companies with an owner rating.*

The US bets on building. 53% of US companies aim AI at software engineering, against 22% in Europe, and 51% build it into what they sell, against 30%. Both gaps hold without technology companies (45% against 23%, and 55% against 26%). US companies also cite efficiency more often (66% against 50%) and describe an AI-native operating model nearly twice as often (29% against 16%).

Europe bets on people and saying what it does. European companies require AI training (20% against 8%) and set a fluency floor (22% against 10%) more than twice as often. They are less likely to leave their strategy to be inferred (22% against 37%). But they leave AI without a single owner three times as often (22% against 7%).

Much else is the same on both sides. About 60% use or plan AI agents, about 30% give AI tools to all staff, and about 30% link AI to jobs.

Read the regional gaps with care. Europe has 50 companies and only 3 of them are technology companies, so a single company moves a European share by 2 points. The software, product and ownership gaps are the firmest; the others are directional.

## How We Built This

The AI Strategy Index covers the 150 companies in the S&P 100 and the STOXX Europe 50, built from public sources such as annual reports, investor days, earnings calls, company statements and press reporting. Figures are as of November 24, 2026. Each profile is rated on how the AI strategy is set out, who owns AI, how value is reported, the biggest gap between what the company says and shows, and the AI fluency it expects of all staff.

For this report we also coded every profile for its reasons for AI, its focus areas and a set of yes/no commitments. Reasons were coded twice by AI, in independent passes: the two passes agreed on the lead reason for 107 of the 150, and a third AI review settled every disagreement against written rules. Efficiency, for example, counts when a profile states cost, productivity, capacity or time saved as a goal, as the purpose of a focus area, or as the result the company puts forward.

Focus areas and commitments were coded once and checked by a second independent AI pass on 20 random profiles, with agreement of 85% to 100% per item. A yes/no commitment means the company's public record shows it, not that it is absent otherwise. A money figure means a company-wide amount attributed to AI, or to AI and automation together, either delivered or as a stated target; a figure for one function does not count. These figures are company-claimed.

The patterns are associations, not proof of cause. AI Pacesetters and Notable companies are profiled in the Index but not counted here. For wider research on AI at work, see our Insights hub.
