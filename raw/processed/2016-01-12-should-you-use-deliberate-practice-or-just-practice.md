# Should You Use Deliberate Practice... Or Just Practice?

There's little universal advice — a good tip for a beginner may be bad advice for an expert. Consider two people who want to get in shape: a late-thirties programmer who hasn't exercised in years, and an elite swimmer trying to shave milliseconds off his best times. For the programmer, the answer is just showing up to the gym regularly; technique and optimization can wait. For the elite swimmer, that advice is useless — he's already training hours a day and needs deliberate, specialized practice to compete.

The same logic applies to any skill. Young describes taking up painting again as an adult: the obvious "correct" answer is deliberate practice (identify weak points, seek feedback, take classes). But that's the elite-swimmer answer applied to a doughy-programmer problem. His actual bottleneck isn't the quality of his practice, it's whether he practices at all, since painting competes with easier leisure activities like TV. The same applies to learning a language: for most people the right advice is "whatever you can stick to" (Duolingo, manga, subtitled movies), not immersion and structured spaced repetition — even though the latter is technically more efficient.

Young argues deliberate practice mainly matters for people who are already doing a lot of practice but aren't improving as fast as they'd like — the full-time developer who feels behind, the novelist whose books haven't sold, the doctor aiming to become the top specialist. This is especially relevant for careers, where sufficient exposure to the work already exists and the stakes justify the discomfort of deliberate practice.

The general principle: any goal has levers like volume, efficiency, and intensity. Efficiency is worth optimizing once adding more volume becomes hard. But for most beginners, volume — just doing the thing more — should be the target, since pursuing efficiency too early can make a goal more complicated and less fun, ultimately reducing the volume you put in.
