# Sugar Rationing in Early Life Linked to Lower Dementia Risk Decades Later

People who consumed less sugar in utero and before age 2 had a lower risk of developing dementia later in life, according to data from 65,000 U.K. Biobank participants.

The study used the abrupt end of World War II sugar rationing in the U.K. as a natural experiment to test whether sugar exposure during different windows in the first 1,000 days from conception was tied to later all-cause dementia, Alzheimer's disease, or vascular dementia.

Compared with people not exposed to sugar rationing, risks of all-cause dementia (HR 0.79, 95% CI 0.66-0.94) and Alzheimer's disease (HR 0.77, 95% CI 0.59-1.00) were lower for people who had sugar rationing in utero and their first year of life, reported Jiazhen Zheng, PhD, of the Hong Kong University of Science and Technology, and colleagues, in the journal Neurology. Associations with vascular dementia were not significant.

Rationing in utero that extended to ages 1 to 2 years was tied to dementia being delayed by 2.55 years. Incident type 2 diabetes and hypertension jointly mediated 25.5% of the association. In an imaging substudy, early-life sugar rationing was associated with higher total gray matter volume, lower white matter hyperintensity volume, and better performance in processing speed and reasoning.

Several pathways may explain these findings, Zheng suggested. "Excessive sugar exposure during pregnancy may contribute to maternal hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, and inflammation, potentially affecting placental function and fetal brain development," he said. "Early-life nutrition may also shape synaptic maturation, myelination, and long-term cognitive reserve... diabetes and hypertension jointly explained about one-quarter of the association, suggesting that metabolic and vascular health may also play an important role."

Tadeja Gracner, PhD, of the University of Southern California, who has researched this cohort but wasn't involved in the study, noted persistence effects: "Sweet preferences form early, and we find that people rationed as infants or toddlers were still eating less added sugar and more fiber 50 years later." She added that rationing also shifted schooling, occupation, and wealth, which could shape later health, and that effects concentrate among people genetically predisposed to obesity — early sugar restriction narrowed the genetic gap in adult obesity by about 40% in her ongoing work.

Zheng and colleagues studied 64,737 U.K. Biobank participants born before and after WWII sugar rationing (sugar became widely available again in 1953). The cohort included 56.4% women; 40,963 participants were exposed to sugar rationing during fetal or early childhood life, and 23,774 were unexposed. Participants averaged 55 years old at study entry, with dementia diagnoses tracked via medical records up to 15 years later.

The study findings do not support restricting overall calories or essential nutrients during pregnancy or infancy, Zheng emphasized. "Rather, they suggest that guideline-consistent reductions in added and free sugars during the first 1,000 days may have long-term brain-health benefits. As this was an observational natural experiment study, further research is needed before drawing causal conclusions."
