# Cancer-fighting chewing gum cuts HPV levels by up to 93%

Researchers led by Henry Daniell at the University of Pennsylvania School of Dental Medicine developed a bioengineered chewing gum that sharply reduced microbes linked to head and neck squamous cell carcinoma (HNSCC) in tests using patient oral samples. The findings were published in Scientific Reports.

HNSCC is aggressive and often has poor outcomes when caught late; Daniell notes that many recently approved cancer drugs haven't meaningfully improved quality of life or five-year survival, motivating the search for new approaches. The study built on earlier research into chewing gum made from lablab beans containing FRIL, a naturally occurring antiviral protein.

Researchers tested the gum extract against three microbes linked to cancer in samples from HNSCC patients: human papillomavirus (HPV), and the bacteria Porphyromonas gingivalis (Pg) and Fusobacterium nucleatum (Fn) — both known to worsen survival in recurrent or metastatic oral cancer even after surgery and standard adjuvant therapy. The bean gum extract reduced HPV levels by 93% in saliva samples and 80% in oral rinse samples. A version engineered to also contain protegrin, an antimicrobial peptide, brought Pg and Fn levels down to almost zero with a single dose.

Critically, the treatment preserved beneficial mouth bacteria — unlike radiation therapy, which reduces helpful bacteria while encouraging growth of the disease-causing yeast Candida albicans. Researchers believe the gum could eventually serve as an adjuvant alongside current cancer treatments or as a preventive measure against infection and transmission. Daniell notes lip and oral cavity cancer was the seventh leading cancer type by incidence and mortality worldwide among adolescents, young adults, and middle-aged adults in 2022, underscoring the value of advancing the therapy toward clinical trials.
