# What Kindergarten and College Teaching Share (opinion)

The first time a kindergartner looked up at me from the carpet and asked whether I thought she was smart, I gave her the answer she needed and went on with the lesson. Two decades later, I heard the question again, in a different register, from undergraduates who never asked it out loud. They asked it with their posture, with their silence, with the way they watched me to decide whether the next 90 minutes would be worth their attention. The questions are the same at age 5 and age 20: Am I smart? Do I belong here? Is this person going to help or judge me?

I spent the bulk of my career teaching children to read. I taught in U.S. public schools and in international settings. I taught general education, pulled small groups as an English for speakers of other languages teacher and worked as a reading specialist, among other roles. The contexts changed constantly. The work always involved about the same thing: meeting a learner who could not yet do something and staying beside her until she could.

That work builds a professional instinct. You learn to read a room, to tell genuine confusion from quiet disengagement, to hold a high expectation and a warm relationship at the same time. You learn that the responsibility for engagement belongs to the teacher.

I carried all that into a public university, where I had just been hired as an assistant professor of elementary education. And then, for one semester, I set almost everything I knew aside.

I had a doctorate. I had a title. I had my own memory of college, where professors lectured from the front of the room and the burden of engagement sat squarely on the student. That was the model I reached for. I lectured carefully and assumed my credential would command the room. I assumed I would not need to tell undergraduates to engage honestly with artificial intelligence, to read before class or to stay off social media during it. In my naïveté, I incorrectly assumed the way I operated as an undergraduate student would be the same for students across time, states and norms. I walked into a 2024 classroom expecting 2004 students, and I was wrong in nearly every particular.

My students were unconvinced. They had grown up in a world where information is everywhere and a person talking at the front of a room has to earn their attention rather than assume it. My student evaluations told the story plainly. My institution’s survey instrument uses a five-point scale on which one means strong agreement and five means strong disagreement. For the items I care about most, such as whether the learning environment was positive and engaging and whether the course helped students think critically, the scores sat close to four. In written comments, one student called the class paper- and lecture-heavy. Another wrote, simply, “Extremely long lectures.”

It was hard to admit it, but through wounded pride I could recognize they were right. I had been treating my undergraduates as though their motivation were their own problem to manage. I had been teaching the way I was taught instead of the way I actually knew how to teach. Somewhere between the kindergarten carpet and the university lecture hall, I had absorbed the idea that adult learners require less of a teacher than children do.

In my second semester, I stopped teaching the way I had been taught and went back to teaching the way I knew. In an early literacy classroom, you never simply tell children something and expect it to take. You model it first, out loud, where they can see your thinking. You practice it together. You release it to them while you watch. Teachers call this “I do, we do, you do.” More formally, it is the Gradual Release of Responsibility model. I rebuilt my university courses on exactly that rhythm. Class time stopped being a stretch of lecture followed by an assignment students completed alone. It became a workshop. I made expectations visible instead of assuming students would infer them. I checked for understanding regularly and informally rather than waiting for a high-stakes exam to reveal what students had missed. I treated engagement as my job. When the room went flat, I did not conclude that my students did not care. I asked what had gotten in the way.

There was a change underneath all of these other changes that had little to do with method. In my first semester, I had believed my job was to appear to know everything, because that was what the title seemed to require. With my kindergartners, I had always kept a sense of humor, owned my mistakes and let real relationships form. After I flopped with my undergraduates, I decided to offer them the same. I stopped performing competence and started practicing it where they could see.

The change was measurable. By the following year, the same course that had drawn ratings close to four on those key items drew ratings near one. Students who had sat back began to lean in. Some colleagues who observed my courses later adopted elements of what they saw into their own teaching, including a chemistry professor who took a discussion activity from my early literacy course into his chemistry classes. The pedagogy of the kindergarten carpet, routed through a teacher-preparation course, ended up in a college chemistry class.

Of course, kindergarten and college are very different. But the pedagogy we treat as explicitly required for young children, the deliberate, scaffolded approach we offer because we know they cannot learn without it, turns out to serve a very different developmental stage just as well. The human conditions of learning do not vary across the years between kindergarten and college as much as we may assume.

Today’s undergraduates did not arrive less capable than earlier generations. They arrived differently situated. They have grown up able to retrieve any fact in seconds, which means the lecture has lost the monopoly it once held on information. A professor who stands and talks is now competing for attention rather than commanding it. Early childhood teachers have always known this, because a 5-year-old has never once deferred to a credential. Young children give a teacher their attention only when the teaching earns it. That is not a deficiency but instead is a more accurate reflection of how attention works at any age.

All this has practical implications for colleges and universities’ efforts to improve the quality of teaching and learning. Faculty who arrive in higher education from elementary classrooms are often made to feel that their classroom years are the least serious line on the vita, while the opposite is closer to the truth. The explicit instruction, the scaffolding, the relentless formative checking, the relationship-building and the engagement instincts a strong elementary teacher develops are the skills the scholarship of teaching and learning keeps rediscovering and recommending. Departments and faculty development offices would do well to treat new faculty with elementary backgrounds as carriers of pedagogical knowledge rather than professors-in-training who happen to have taught children first.

The kindergartner who asked me whether she was smart was asking whether the room she had walked into was a place where she could safely try. My undergraduates are asking the same question. I spent nearly two decades learning how to answer it for young children. It took one hard semester to understand that the answer for college students was the same.
