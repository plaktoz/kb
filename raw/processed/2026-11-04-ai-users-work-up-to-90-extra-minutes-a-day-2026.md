# AI Users Work Up to 90 Extra Minutes a Day [2026]

Employees who use AI at work are putting in longer days than colleagues who don’t. New data from ActivTrak, which TechRadar is reporting today, puts the gap at up to roughly 90 minutes per day. The finding lands at an awkward moment for companies that have spent heavily on AI seats and promised their boards that the tools would give time back.

ActivTrak analyzed digital-work data from 9,140 knowledge workers at 467 enterprises between April 1 and September 25, 2026. AI users’ workdays ranged from 8.42 to 9.08 hours, against 7.58 hours for non-users. That is the headline number, and it is also the number most likely to be misread, so this analysis spends real time on what the report does and does not show.

The report arrives while analysts already question whether AI-driven cost cuts hold up. We covered Gartner’s forecast that 30% of AI layoffs get rehired by 2029, and the ActivTrak numbers add a second angle: even workers who keep their jobs may be absorbing the cost of AI adoption in extra hours. The rest of this piece covers the data, the caveats, the market implications, and five predictions for how employers will respond.

## What the ActivTrak AI Report Found

The core result is simple. Across the sample, employees classified as AI users worked longer days than employees who did not use AI. The report’s range for AI users runs from 8.42 hours at the low end to 9.08 hours at the high end. Non-users averaged 7.58 hours. Subtract one from the other and the gap runs from about 50 minutes to about 90 minutes per day, depending on which AI-user group you compare.

The sample is large by workplace-analytics standards. With 9,140 workers spread across 467 enterprises, the average company contributed about 20 employees. That spread matters because it dilutes the quirks of any single employer. A company with a punishing deadline culture cannot drive the whole result when hundreds of firms feed the dataset.

The observation window also helps. April 1 to September 25 covers roughly six months, long enough to smooth out one-off crunch periods like a quarter-end close or a product launch. A single busy week does not explain a gap that persists across half a year of activity data.

Beyond the headline gap, ActivTrak reported several behavioral signals that explain how the extra time shows up in a day. Heavy AI users worked more after hours, switched between applications more often, and held shorter uninterrupted focus sessions. We take each of those in turn below.

## Who Counts as a Heavy AI User

ActivTrak defined heavy AI users as employees who used AI for more than 45 minutes across at least five sessions per day. That definition sets a fairly high bar. It describes someone who reaches for an AI tool repeatedly through the day, not someone who opens a chatbot once to draft an email.

The definition shapes how you should read the results. A worker who runs five or more AI sessions daily is likely doing work where AI is already woven into the routine, such as drafting, summarizing, coding, or research. Those are roles where output volume can scale up, and where a manager may notice the speedup and raise expectations. That is our reading, not a claim from the report.

The report also uses light AI users and general AI users as separate categories. The coverage we reviewed does not publish the exact threshold for light use, and it does not name the AI products participants used. That second gap is important. A coding assistant, a meeting summarizer, and a general chatbot can change a workday in very different ways, and the data cannot separate them.

## Why Association Is Not Causation

ActivTrak states plainly that its findings show an association, not a causal relationship, between AI use and longer workdays. That caveat deserves more attention than the headline gets. The report does not say AI adds 90 minutes to your day. It says people who use AI work longer days than people who don’t.

## Three competing explanations

At least three readings fit the data. First, AI may create new work, such as reviewing machine output, correcting errors, and managing prompts, which stretches the day. Second, ambitious or overloaded employees may adopt AI precisely because they already work long hours and want relief. Third, employers may hand the heaviest workloads to the staff who know the tools best.

The second explanation is a classic selection effect. If the busiest people reach for AI first, the tools look like a cause of long hours when they are really a response to them. The report cannot rule that out, and neither can we. Only a before-and-after study that tracks the same employees as they adopt AI could settle it.

## What the headline gets wrong

The phrase “people using AI” in many headlines is broader than the categories ActivTrak measured. The cited data concern employees classified as AI users, heavy AI users, light AI users, and non-users. It does not cover everyone who has ever tried a chatbot, and it covers knowledge workers at enterprises rather than the general workforce.

## Fragmented Focus: App Switching and Shorter Sessions

Heavy AI users switched applications 15.2% more often than non-users. The report puts the rates at 1.1 switches per hour for heavy AI users and 0.95 for non-users. On its face, that gap looks small. Spread over a nine-hour day, though, it adds up to more context changes, and context changes carry a cost that time-on-task counters do not capture.

The focus-session data tells a similar story. Uninterrupted focus sessions averaged 37.8 minutes for heavy AI users and 48.2 minutes for light AI users, a 22% difference. That is a gap of about ten minutes per session. Notably, the comparison here is between heavy and light users, not between users and non-users, so it isolates intensity of AI use rather than adoption alone.

There is a plausible mechanical link. Working with an AI tool means hopping between the tool, the document, the browser, and the communication apps. Each hop breaks the session counter. A person who prompts, waits, checks, edits, and re-prompts all morning will rack up more switches and shorter unbroken stretches than someone who writes straight through. The data fits that story, though it does not prove it.

For managers, the implication is uncomfortable. If deep work is the scarce input for hard problems, then a tool that shaves minutes off drafting while slicing focus blocks into smaller pieces may not raise net output. The report does not measure output quality or volume, so the net effect stays unknown.

## After-Hours Creep: The 18-Minute Signal

Heavy AI users worked 18 additional minutes after hours compared with non-users. Eighteen minutes sounds trivial in isolation. Over a five-day week it comes to 90 minutes of evening or weekend work, and over a year it becomes a meaningful transfer of personal time to the employer.

After-hours work is the part of the data that concerns us most, because it points toward boundary erosion rather than productive intensity. If AI tools make it easy to pick up a task from a phone or laptop at 9 p.m., the tools lower the friction of working late. Lower friction tends to increase the behavior, whatever the original intent.

The after-hours figure also complicates the story that AI buys people time. If heavy users truly saved hours on routine tasks, you would expect shorter days or at least flat ones. Instead the group works more in total and more outside normal hours. Either the saved time gets reinvested in additional work, or the tools create enough new work to cancel the savings. Both are plausible, and the report cannot choose between them.

## The Meeting Paradox

One result runs against the grain. Heavy AI users spent 3% of their work time in meetings, compared with 5% for non-AI users. That is a point in AI’s favor, or at least in favor of the people who use it most. Fewer meeting minutes should free time for solo work.

Yet those same heavy users have longer days and shorter focus sessions. So the time recovered from meetings does not show up as a shorter workday. Two readings fit. AI note-takers and summarizers may let people skip meetings they once attended, and the saved time flows into more task work. Alternatively, people who are comfortable with AI may also be the people who work asynchronously by habit, and meetings were never a big part of their schedule.

Either way, the meeting data undercuts a lazy narrative that heavy AI users are simply busier in every dimension. They are busier in some ways and lighter in others, which suggests the story is about how work reshapes, not just how much of it there is.

## What ActivTrak Told Business Leaders

ActivTrak did not just publish numbers. It also gave leaders a direction. In the on-record statement carried in the coverage, the company said: “For leaders, the goal can’t simply be AI usage; they need to treat AI usage as a means to measurable gains in productivity, efficiency and business performance.”

That statement targets a specific management habit. Many firms track AI adoption as a success metric, counting logins, seats activated, or prompts sent. Those figures show that people use the tools. They say nothing about whether the work improved. ActivTrak’s point is that adoption is an input, and leaders should judge AI by outcomes they can measure.

The framing also carries a quiet warning. If a company rewards AI usage directly, employees will use AI more, including on tasks where it adds friction. The extra sessions then show up as longer days and shorter focus blocks, exactly the pattern in the data. A usage target can manufacture the symptoms the report describes.

The commercial stakes are large. Spending on AI infrastructure keeps climbing, and we tracked how AI capex tops $740B as markets doubt the boom. Investors have pushed vendors to show returns, and workplace productivity is the most common return story. A dataset suggesting heavy users work longer without a measured output gain gives skeptics fresh material.

For AI vendors, the report creates a messaging problem. Vendor marketing often promises hours saved. Telemetry from 467 enterprises shows hours added among the heaviest users. Vendors will likely answer that longer days reflect higher output, but proving it requires output data that activity monitors do not collect. Expect more vendors to ship their own productivity dashboards in response.

## Historical Context: Tools That Stretched the Day

This is not the first time a productivity tool has widened the workday instead of shrinking it. In our analysis, earlier waves of workplace technology, including email, group chat, and the smartphone, followed a similar arc. Each one made work faster to do and easier to reach, and each one lengthened the period in which people were expected to be available. We draw that pattern from general workplace history, not from the ActivTrak report.

AI differs in one important respect. Earlier tools mostly moved information around. Generative AI produces work product, such as drafts, code, and summaries, which someone must then check. That checking step is new labor. A worker who generates three drafts in the time it once took to write one now has three drafts to review, and the review is the part machines cannot yet skip.

The labor market adds pressure. Big employers are reshaping headcount at the same moment they roll out AI, and our report on Microsoft’s $36.6B profit alongside 60,000 buyout offers captures that tension. Workers who see colleagues leave while AI spreads may stretch their hours to stay indispensable. That behavior would show up in the data as AI users working more, whatever the tools themselves do.

## Five Predictions for the Next 12 Months

These are our own forecasts, based on the data above and the broader trends we cover. They are not statements from ActivTrak or any named source.

Outcome metrics replace usage metrics. More firms will drop login counts as an AI success measure and adopt output-based targets, following the logic of ActivTrak’s advice to leaders.

Before-and-after studies arrive. Researchers and vendors will publish longitudinal data that tracks the same employees through adoption, which is the only design that can separate cause from selection.

Vendors add workload dashboards. AI tool makers will ship built-in views showing time saved and time added, partly to defend renewals against skeptical finance teams.

Unions and regulators ask about hours. Expect labor groups to cite after-hours findings when they push for limits on always-on AI use, especially in regions with strict working-time rules.

Focus protection becomes a feature. Tool designers will respond to the shorter-focus finding with fewer context switches, such as AI built into the apps where people already work.

The common thread is measurement. Until companies can show that AI shifts outcomes rather than just activity, the longer-day narrative will keep resurfacing every time a new dataset appears.

## What Teams Should Measure Now

Managers do not need to wait for the next study. A short list of internal checks can show whether AI helps or hurts your own teams. Start with a baseline from before rollout, since without one you cannot tell whether hours changed. Then track the same four things ActivTrak highlighted: total hours, after-hours work, app switching, and focus-session length.

Pair those activity numbers with outcome numbers that your business already trusts, such as tickets closed, features shipped, or error rates. If hours rise and outcomes stay flat, the tools are adding load. If hours stay flat and outcomes rise, the tools are working as sold.
