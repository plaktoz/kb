# Why productivity gains don’t always reach the bottom line

With artificial intelligence’s returns on investment increasingly under scrutiny, many businesses believe their experimentation has not resulted in the promised improvements. But Suzanne Coles, a partner with Curzon Consulting, argues that while the invoice for an AI licence will arrive on schedule, the business benefit rarely arrives with the same clarity – and firms should be cautious not to write their efforts off too soon.

Organisations can track what they spend on artificial intelligence: technology, infrastructure, implementation, training and changing how people work. Understanding what those investments have delivered is considerably harder. McKinsey & Company’s 2026 global survey reports that 80% of respondents saw improvements in their own productivity, while 37% attributed a positive enterprise-level EBIT impact to AI. These self-reported findings do not explain the gap, but they raise an important question: why aren’t improvements in how people work appearing more consistently in business performance?

We tend to assume that benefits will eventually catch up with investment. Sometimes they will. But what if the improvements have already been achieved, but the decisions needed to realise their value have not been made?

In my previous articles, I examined how measuring AI at the point of intervention can obscure its wider impact, and whether individual productivity gains strengthen organisational capability. Here, I want to explore what happens next: how improvements become economic value, and why that conversion is far from automatic.

I believe organisations need to distinguish three situations too often grouped together as disappointing AI returns. Delayed value occurs when credible benefits are developing but have yet to mature. Stranded value exists when improvements have been demonstrated, but operating structures, resource allocations or management decisions prevent them from contributing fully to the intended business outcome. Unproven value describes investments for which there is insufficient evidence that the expected improvements are materialising.

These are not semantic distinctions. Delayed value may justify continued investment against clear milestones. Stranded value requires decisions about processes, people and resources. Unproven value demands a reassessment of the original assumptions. Treating all three as a timing problem risks encouraging organisations to wait when they should be acting.

## How productivity becomes stranded

Consider an organisation introducing AI to accelerate customer enquiry handling. Employees retrieve information faster, prepare responses more efficiently and resolve routine questions with less effort. The improvement is real, but what happens to the capacity released? Keeping the same workforce can still deliver value through better service, reduced backlogs or greater resilience, provided these are intended outcomes and are measured.

I have seen process automation reduce transaction-processing effort by 20–30%, while operating costs remained largely unchanged because leadership had not redesigned roles, workflows or reporting lines. Teams remained busy, recruitment plans continued and the released capacity disappeared into existing activities. Only when management decided whether to absorb growth, reduce overtime or avoid additional recruitment did the benefit become visible. The process improvement created the opportunity; the business decision created the return.

A six-month field experiment involving 7,137 knowledge workers across 66 firms illustrates the distinction. In the second half of the experiment, workers given access to an AI assistant who used it spent approximately two fewer hours on email each week and worked less outside regular hours. Researchers detected no corresponding shift in the quantity or composition of tasks. The time savings were useful, but did not by themselves demonstrate a wider reorganisation of work.

This distinction becomes more important when capacity is fragmented. Saving 15 minutes a day across 40 people creates ten theoretical hours of capacity daily. Yet those minutes may be scattered across shifts, locations and specialist roles. They cannot necessarily be combined into a redesigned shift, a reduced supplier contract or an additional service. I have seen reported productivity improvements of 10–20% coexist with unchanged staffing because coverage requirements remained the same. The theoretical saving and the actionable saving were not equivalent.

## Five different routes to value

Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond studied an AI assistant used by 5,172 customer-support agents and found a 15% average increase in issues resolved per hour, with particularly strong improvements among less-experienced employees. The study also found benefits for customer interactions and worker retention. These findings do not, on their own, establish the financial return after technology and business-change costs.

I distinguish five types of benefit.

Productivity improvement means work takes less time or resource at an acceptable standard; it creates potential capacity.

Cashable savings occur when expenditure actually leaves the cost base, allowing for transition and ongoing costs.

Cost avoidance arises when credible future expenditure, such as recruitment or outsourcing, is no longer required.

Profitable growth depends on additional capacity, better service or decisions translating into revenue and margin.

Strategic value comes from capabilities that strengthen resilience, innovation or future competitiveness, with evidence that those capabilities are developing.

These categories are related but not interchangeable, and they must not be counted twice. Capacity used to avoid hiring cannot automatically be claimed again as a cashable saving. Nor should workforce reduction be the default objective. Redeploying people towards complex customer needs, improving quality or supporting growth may be commercially more valuable than removing roles. What matters is an explicit decision about the intended benefit, followed by evidence that it has been realised.

## When more automation is not better value

There is a further question that improvement programmes sometimes avoid: how much should organisations spend to capture the remaining benefit? I have seen programmes remove 70-80% of manual effort relatively quickly, only to discover that eliminating the remaining 20-30% would require substantial process redesign, additional controls, system changes and ongoing management effort.

Technology teams can deliver a capability successfully and employees can adopt it enthusiastically, while the financial return remains elusive because the decisions needed to capture it sit elsewhere. Finance can establish investment baselines, test benefit assumptions and distinguish cash savings from avoided costs. Operations understands which processes must change, how capacity can be consolidated and what is achievable without compromising service or quality. Neither can establish the full value independently.

The investment case must therefore explain what the organisation will do differently. Who will change workforce plans, supplier arrangements, performance measures or decision rights? Who will decide how released capacity should be used, and test whether the intended benefit appears in the accounts or operating performance? Finance should not simply validate benefits after implementation, nor should operations be held to assumptions it did not help develop. Both need to agree the intended benefits from the outset, with one accountable business leader responsible for the outcome.

## Five questions for leadership

I would bring five questions into investment approval, implementation reviews and ordinary business performance discussions, rather than create another separate AI reporting exercise.

1. What kind of value are we investing to create? Are we seeking cashable savings, avoided costs, profitable growth, improved service or strategic capability? Each requires different evidence, and the business case should make that distinction explicit.

2. Where might value already be stranded? Which improvements have been demonstrated but remain constrained by unchanged workflows, staffing arrangements or management decisions? What specifically must change to release them?

3. Is the capacity genuinely usable? Can scattered minutes be consolidated into something operationally meaningful? If not, could they support quality, resilience or growth rather than an implausible cost-saving claim?

4. Is further redesign commercially justified? What additional benefit would another round of automation produce, and at what cost and risk? Sometimes stopping short of complete automation is the better investment decision.

5. Are we exercising disciplined patience, or simply waiting? Increasing adoption, shorter processing times or declining backlogs may indicate progress if they support the intended outcome and the costs remain credible. None is sufficient on its own. Review points should reflect the investment’s purpose and expected timescale. Stalled adoption, flat operational measures or no credible connection to business outcomes should prompt a reassessment. The issue may not be timing; the original value proposition may never have been proven.

## From investment to realised value

The challenge for executives is to decide what AI-enabled improvements should mean for the organisation, and make the decisions and changes needed to translate them into sustainable performance.

Some investments need time to mature. Others have demonstrated improvements that remain constrained by operating models or management decisions. Some may never deliver the expected returns. Distinguishing these situations helps leaders decide whether to continue, redesign or stop.

Technology creates the opportunity. Management decisions determine whether that opportunity becomes economic value.
