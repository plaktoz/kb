# AI is changing how students learn to write — and think

Students are arriving at college less ready to write, and AI will now do it for them. What happens to the practice — and the thinking it is meant to teach?

Eliza Russo has a name for what happened in September 2024: Macbeth-gate. While grading her 12th graders’ papers, the head of Lincoln High’s English department noticed that they all sounded as if they had been written by the same person, or, more likely, by an AI model. “Polished turds,” she called them. “It looks good on the surface, but there is nothing of substance or value in what it’s actually saying.”

Russo considered an investigation, but said, “I did not become a teacher to become a cop.” So instead she sent an email to the families, informing them that the students would be required to rewrite their papers in class the following week. She asked parents to “please have a conversation with your students about what integrity and honesty mean to you.”

Macbeth-gate was the last time Russo assigned a take-home essay. She wants students to tackle ambitious writing projects, developing and refining a thesis through months of research. But, she said, so many students would turn to AI that policing its use would take more time than any teacher has.

Now she limits essay writing to the classroom, by hand, where students have little opportunity to develop longer, more complex arguments. The result, she admits, is that they may be unprepared for college-level writing.

Even her best students’ “eyes would fall out of their head,” she said, if she asked them to write an eight-page paper.

Her solution ensures that kids do their own work, but it may come at a cost. They lose the long, messy practice of learning how to compose at length and, some argue, how to think.

In a fall 2025 Pew Research Center survey, 54% of 13- to 17-year-olds said they had used AI chatbots to help with schoolwork, and 59% said students at their school used chatbots to cheat somewhat or very often.

But concerns about writing started before the AI boom. By the time ChatGPT arrived in November 2022, California was testing writing less than it once did, and fewer students were arriving at UC ready to write, by the university’s own measure.

The Standard spoke with teachers and academics about what AI is changing in the classroom and what students stand to lose. Those conversations raised questions: Were students learning to write well before AI? Does AI keep them from learning? And the fundamental question: If AI can do the writing for students, what happens to thinking, exploration, and expression?

## Fewer ways to measure writing

California has steadily narrowed the ways it measures success at writing. Fourth- and seventh-grade writing tests ended in 2013, and the state suspended its high school exit exam, which included an essay, in 2015 and eliminated it in 2017. The standardized test still includes one essay a year, but score reports fold writing into a category with research.

The national picture is similar. The National Assessment of Educational Progress last reported writing results in 2011, when 27% of eighth and 12th graders scored proficient or above. No writing tests are scheduled through 2033. The University of California stopped using SAT and ACT writing scores in 2020, and the College Board dropped the SAT essay the following year.

Bita Nazarian, who runs 826 Valencia, a San Francisco tutoring nonprofit that works with students at three centers and 14 school sites, argues that what isn’t tested doesn’t get taught. “Writing has been deprioritized in our system,” she said.

State policy has put its weight behind reading, rather than writing. Since 2021, California has passed three laws aimed at teaching children to read, the latest signed in October 2025. The state has not passed comparable legislation on writing. San Francisco has followed the same path, Nazarian said, adopting a new literacy curriculum two years ago without the same focus on writing skills.

On paper, students are faring better than ever, but fewer arrive having met UC’s writing bar. Among UC freshmen, the share with a 4.0 weighted GPA or higher went from 28.6% in 1994 to 74.9% in 2022 before easing to 68% in fall 2025, according to UC data analyzed by The Standard.

“Many students who enter the University of California are straight-A students, so you can no longer use A’s to differentiate them,” said Jon Lang, who directs the Berkeley Writing Assessment and has worked at the university for more than 30 years. Many students UC holds for the requirement are surprised, he said, because their grades told them they were proficient.

He blamed high school instruction that teaches writing as a template, especially the five-paragraph essay. “Instead of thinking about why they are writing what they’re writing, how to organize it, why one paragraph follows another, why they’ve decided on specific pieces of evidence, you’re given a formula, and everything you have to say fits in the formula,” he said.

One of the few remaining tests of students’ writing ability comes when students enter college — but fewer students clear that requirement than a decade ago. Every UC first-year student must meet the Entry Level Writing Requirement, often through an AP, International Baccalaureate, or ACT score. UC announced the end of its systemwide placement exam in 2021, leaving individual campuses to assess students’ writing ability on their own. Berkeley gives a two-hour timed essay; students who don’t pass take a writing course called R1A.

And last fall, 1,109 incoming freshmen at Berkeley had not met the writing standard, more than five times as many as in 2016, even amid meager growth of the class size from 4,652 to 4,970. Across UC, about 29% of 12,000 California freshman arrived without meeting the requirement, versus 23% in 2015.

For San Francisco high schoolers, the numbers are even worse. In fall 2023, fewer than half of the city’s high school graduates who enrolled in the UC system had met the requirement. Last fall, they cleared it at a rate 11.8 points below the UC average, the fourth-lowest of the 44 counties UC reports.

But other measures paint a more reassuring picture. After 2021, the College Board changed how it sets passing scores on its most popular AP exams. The pass rates for both AP English exams, which are majority essay-writing, rose sharply. AP English Literature and Composition jumped 34 points in 2022 and averaged about 75% pass rates from 2022 to 2025, up from about 57% over the previous two decades.

Lang also sees a problem with high school teachers assigning essays to be written in class. “They’re not prepared for college-level work, because college-level work is more complex,” he said. “It requires that they draft and redraft. If they’re only taught how to write to the exam, they never revise.”

Admission to Berkeley doesn’t guarantee a student can write at Berkeley’s level. Last fall, 1 in 5 incoming freshmen arrived without meeting the writing requirement.

A lecturer who teaches College Writing 1A, Berkeley’s course for students who haven’t met the requirement, said many arrive with significant gaps in writing skills. She asked for anonymity in order to stay in good standing with the university and her students. She was surprised to find that her Berkeley students were no better equipped for college-level writing than those at the state university and community college where she previously taught.

She recalled one student who couldn’t write in the past tense. Many, she said, “hadn’t even read a full book in high school.” “They’ll say, ‘Oh yeah, we’ve never had to read a whole book. We read like a poem or a chapter or something.’”

## Built to avoid thinking

Dan Willingham, a cognitive psychologist at the University of Virginia, said the pull toward shortcuts is how the brain works.

“We’re very good at thinking relative to other species, but thinking is not anything that most of us want to do most of the time,” he said. “The brain is really designed to help you avoid thinking.” If writing doesn’t come easily, he said, “your brain is really telling you, go ahead and have Gemini do this.”

But the effort, he said, is what produces learning.

Ben Riley, who runs Cognitive Resonance, an organization that argues against AI in education, compared AI to a hot dog in an essay for The Verge. Humans evolved to crave fatty food when it was scarce, and now that it’s plentiful, the craving works against us. AI, he wrote, “appeals to us in the moment, but undermines the health and sustainability of cognitive systems we’ve evolved over several millennia.” The danger, he wrote, is not occasional use but “making it a regular part of our mental diet.” Students have to build knowledge themselves, he told The Standard. “You really do have to go through the hard part first.”

AI may help students get better grades on homework while leaving them less prepared for exams. A study followed nearly 27,000 students in grades seven through 12 in one county in central China for 30 months. Students decided on their own whether and when to start using AI. After they started using AI, their homework scores rose 18%, and their time per assignment fell from 64 to 45 minutes, but their scores on monthly closed-book exams fell 20% within six months.

Riley, who has written about the Chinese study, said the students who used AI “cognitively offloaded their homework. Therefore, they weren’t learning.” The Chinese study suggests that how students use AI may matter as much as whether they use it. But it doesn’t measure what years of AI-assisted schoolwork will mean for long-term writing ability.

## Is writing the point?

Is writing even essential to the business of thinking?

Some have cast doubt on this from the earliest times. In Plato’s “Phaedrus,” Socrates tells the story of Theuth, the Egyptian god who invents writing and presents it to the king, Thamus. The king is unimpressed. Writing, Thamus says, will make people forgetful, because they will trust the written word instead of their own memories. Socrates agrees. Writing, he says, is like painting. Its creations look alive, “and yet if you ask them a question they preserve a solemn silence.”

Joe Cruz, chair of the philosophy department at Williams College, makes a modern version of that case. In a New York Times column called “I’m a college professor. Writing isn’t as important as we think” (Cruz did not write the headline), he argues that colleges should consider other ways to assess and develop students’ thinking, such as discussion or oral exams.

“Having a durable record of complicated steps in thinking isn’t the same as being insightful,” Cruz told The Standard. “The insightful part comes from the active capacity to be creative and tenacious.”

The column provoked a strong reaction — not all of it positive. (“What stage of grief is this? Bargaining or acceptance?” one commenter asked.) Cruz said he put his thoughts out in public because of AI.

“Probably if AI tools didn’t exist, I would cruise along, business as usual, and assign writing assignments to my classes,” he said. “That’s the way I did it for 25 years. … But the AI tools, I think, give us a chance to maybe revisit what we’re trying to do in the academy and maybe ask ourselves, have we been doing it right?”

Nazarian, of 826 Valencia, sees it differently from Cruz, partly because much of the writing she works on with students is creative. She says the writing process can help people reflect on what they truly value and believe. She worries about the personal essays students write for college applications, which she has seen “become more of a monolith instead of something individualized to a young person’s own experience.”

“These tools actually take away what is special and important about the young people who are applying,” Nazarian said. “They think it’s helpful, but it actually is counter to what universities are looking for, which is the individual.”

Writing matters long after the college application is in. To write well is to be able to tell your life story to a potential employer, to convince a politician from afar, to land a crucial client online.

It is also how you can enchant your crush, give a toast people remember, and find the words to honor someone you’ve lost. It is how people who are gone or far away speak to us. Sometimes it is all we can offer when we can’t be in the room.

Russo doesn’t blame her students for turning to AI. “They’re a product of us as a society raising them,” she said. But she sees another consequence: Students are losing confidence in themselves. “If they feel even a shred of doubt, the first thing is, I have to look at the AI response,” she said. “They’re not struggling with things that are hard.”

Russo remembers writing essays as a teenager when, unlike now, she had no choice but to do it herself.

“I did not care about ‘The Crucible’ when I was 17, but I wrote about it,” she said. “Medicine doesn’t taste good, and yet you must take it anyway.”
