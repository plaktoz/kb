# Margin of Safety: Expect the Unexpected

From *The Great Mental Models v3: Systems and Mathematics* (Farnam Street).

A margin of safety is "a buffer between safety and danger, order and chaos, success and failure." When dealing with complex systems, we must anticipate failure and build in appropriate buffers.

## Design for Extremes, Not Averages

Engineers build bridges far beyond average load requirements because conditions vary unpredictably. The bridge that handles only typical traffic will eventually fail under extreme conditions. The same principle applies across domains: design for edge cases, not central tendencies.

## Investing Application

In investing, the margin of safety represents the gap between an asset's intrinsic value and its purchase price. Buying below intrinsic value creates a buffer that allows for estimation errors and unforeseen events.

## Backups and Redundancy

Redundant systems allow continued function when components fail. The higher the stakes, the more backups you need. Mission-critical systems warrant more redundancy than low-consequence ones.

## Risk Compensation

Safety buffers can breed overconfidence. The margin of safety can "creat[e] perverse incentives" that cancel out its own benefits when people behave more recklessly because they feel protected. Seatbelts, for instance, may cause drivers to take more risks — the Peltzman effect.

## Balance Is Essential

Too little margin invites destruction. Too much wastes resources and breeds complacency. The optimal margin depends on the cost of failure, the cost of the buffer, and the uncertainty involved.

## The Core Question

"What if I'm wrong? What if things don't go as planned?"

Margin of safety is unglamorous but foundational — "not flashy...not exciting, but it's the foundation on which everything else is built." Building this buffer often costs more upfront but separates long-term survivors from those undone by inevitable shocks. It is "what separates the winners from the losers" in navigating an uncertain world.
