# AI Is Making Employees More Productive. Why Aren't Companies Getting Better?

The AI Value Paradox: Companies are eliminating human talent before proving that AI creates greater value, and why organizational design may be the missing ingredient in the AI revolution.

Something troubling is happening across corporate America and beyond. Companies are investing billions of dollars in artificial intelligence while simultaneously announcing significant workforce reductions. Executives are promising extraordinary productivity gains, greater efficiency, and improved financial performance. Entire business strategies are being reorganized around the assumption that AI will fundamentally change how work gets done.

Yet the emerging research tells a more complicated story. AI is making individuals more productive, but those productivity gains are not consistently translating into measurable improvements in overall business performance. That raises a question I believe every CEO and board of directors should be asking: If AI is making our people more productive, why is our first instinct to eliminate those people rather than use their increased capacity to create greater value?

And perhaps an even more important question follows. Are we genuinely transforming our businesses, or are we simply using new technology to make our existing business models less expensive?

I've spent decades leading organizational transformations, scaling businesses, and integrating acquisitions. One lesson has become increasingly clear to me: technology rarely represents the greatest limitation on organizational performance. The limitation is often the organization itself. How decisions are made. How authority is distributed. How teams collaborate. How information moves. How people are empowered to act. And, ultimately, how leadership defines value.

AI is bringing those limitations into sharper focus than ever before.

## The Numbers Should Make Every CEO Pause

In August 2026, McKinsey published its latest global research on the state of AI. The findings reveal a significant disconnect between individual productivity and enterprise-level performance.

Eighty percent of respondents reported that AI had improved their individual productivity. Yet only 37% attributed any positive impact on their organization's earnings before interest and taxes to AI. Just 6% qualified as AI high performers, reporting a significant impact that included at least a 5% contribution to earnings.

In other words, while AI is helping a large majority of individuals work more productively, substantially fewer organizations are reporting measurable financial benefits.

That doesn't mean AI isn't valuable. It certainly doesn't mean the technology lacks transformative potential. Some companies are already achieving meaningful results, and investments of this magnitude can take time to generate returns. We should also recognize that these figures are based on survey responses, not an independent audit of every organization's financial performance.

But the gap is too significant to ignore. We have a technology that is demonstrably improving how individuals perform their work, yet many businesses are struggling to convert those improvements into meaningful organizational outcomes. Meanwhile, companies are making consequential decisions about their workforces based on the benefits they expect AI to deliver.

In October, Reuters reported that a major European bank was cutting approximately 5,000 jobs as it increased its use of AI and other technologies to reduce operating costs. Similar announcements across industries reflect a broader shift toward automation and workforce restructuring.

Not every layoff attributed to AI represents a direct replacement of a person by a machine. Some reflect broader restructuring, financial pressures, or investments in capabilities that have yet to mature. Nevertheless, the underlying leadership question remains. Why are we so willing to make immediate, measurable reductions in human capacity while accepting uncertain, longer-term assumptions about the value that will replace it?

We seem remarkably comfortable measuring what we save. I'm not convinced we're applying the same discipline to measuring what we might be losing.

## Are We Creating Value or Simply Extracting It?

There is an important distinction between improving efficiency and creating value. Efficiency allows an organization to accomplish something using fewer resources. Value creation allows it to accomplish something more meaningful for the people it serves. The two can reinforce each other, but they are not interchangeable.

Imagine an organization discovers that AI enables its employees to complete their work 30% faster. Leadership now has a choice. It could use that additional capacity to serve customers more effectively, improve product quality, accelerate innovation, strengthen relationships, pursue new markets, or solve problems the organization previously lacked the resources to address.

Alternatively, it could conclude that it needs fewer employees.

Both approaches might improve short-term financial performance. But they represent fundamentally different visions of what a business can become. One asks how to create more value. The other asks how to deliver the existing value at a lower cost.

There is nothing inherently irresponsible about reducing costs. Businesses must remain financially healthy, and automation will inevitably change the work people perform. In some circumstances, workforce reductions may be justified by genuine, sustained improvements in productivity.

But I worry that too many executives are treating cost reduction as the primary definition of successful AI transformation. That is a remarkably limited ambition for a technology with such extraordinary potential.

If all we accomplish with AI is producing the same outcomes with fewer people, have we really transformed the business? Or have we simply automated the limitations of the organization we already had?

A cheaper business is not necessarily a better business. And that distinction becomes especially important when we consider the people who ultimately determine whether any business succeeds.

Its customers.

## Where Is the Customer in the AI Conversation?

Listen to the language surrounding many corporate AI initiatives. Automation. Productivity. Efficiency. Workforce optimization. Operating margins. Cost reduction.

These are legitimate business considerations, but something essential is frequently missing. The customer.

Businesses don't exist simply to minimize operating expenses. They exist because they create something valuable enough that customers choose to exchange their money, time, attention, and trust for it. So why isn't customer value at the center of every AI transformation conversation?

Are customers receiving better products? Are their problems being solved more effectively? Are relationships becoming stronger? Are organizations responding more intelligently to their needs? Are we creating experiences that customers genuinely prefer?

Those questions should matter at least as much as the number of hours automated or positions eliminated.

Consider customer service. An AI-powered system might help an employee retrieve information, summarize customer history, identify solutions, and prepare responses in seconds rather than minutes. That's a meaningful productivity improvement.

But suppose that employee still lacks the authority to resolve the customer's problem. Suppose every exception requires multiple approvals, every decision must move through a rigid hierarchy, and every department operates according to different priorities.

The employee now has better information and can work faster. The customer may still have the same frustrating experience.

AI improved the task. It didn't improve the outcome.

And if leadership responds by eliminating employees without redesigning the process, customers may find themselves navigating an even less responsive organization.

We should be careful about assuming that workforce reductions automatically demonstrate a lack of concern for customers. That would be an unfair conclusion without examining the results. But I believe it's entirely reasonable to ask: If an organization defines AI success primarily by how many people it can eliminate, what does that reveal about how it understands customer value?

Because a business that measures only what it extracts risks overlooking what makes customers want to stay.

## AI Is Exposing a Problem We've Had for Decades

Here's where I believe the conversation becomes much more interesting.

What if AI isn't failing to deliver enterprise value because the technology is insufficiently capable? What if organizations are failing to realize that value because they were never designed to operate at the speed, flexibility, and intelligence AI now makes possible?

For decades, businesses have built organizational structures around centralized decision-making, functional specialization, management hierarchies, and elaborate systems of approval. These structures were often designed to create control, consistency, and accountability. They made sense in environments where information was difficult to access, expertise was concentrated, and coordination was expensive.

But they also created unintended consequences. Employees became dependent on management approval. Departments developed competing priorities. Information moved slowly through organizational layers. Accountability became disconnected from authority. People learned to escalate decisions rather than exercise judgment.

In many organizations, the ability to act became increasingly separated from the ability to understand what needed to be done.

Now we're introducing AI into those same environments. We're giving people unprecedented access to information, analysis, recommendations, and problem-solving capabilities. Yet we're often leaving the organizational structures that govern their decisions largely unchanged.

We're accelerating intelligence while preserving the systems that prevent people from acting on it.

In October 2026, McKinsey published additional research examining precisely this challenge. Among its findings, nearly three-quarters of AI high performers reported substantially redesigning their workflows, compared with approximately one-quarter of other organizations.

The distinction is important. The organizations reporting the greatest benefits are not simply adding AI tools to existing processes. They are rethinking how work happens.

That finding aligns with something I've observed throughout my career. When organizational design is fundamentally inefficient, improving the productivity of individual employees doesn't necessarily improve the performance of the whole organization.

You can make every department faster while the business remains slow. You can provide better information while decisions remain delayed. You can automate tasks while preserving unnecessary handoffs. You can increase individual capacity while leaving collective capacity constrained.

And when the anticipated improvements fail to materialize, the temptation is to conclude that the organization needs fewer people.

Perhaps the more important conclusion is that it needs a different design.

## Decentralized Empowerment Is the Missing Multiplier

I believe one of the most important prerequisites for realizing AI's full transformative potential is the decentralization of decision-making. Not the elimination of leadership. Not the absence of accountability. And certainly not allowing AI systems or employees to make consequential decisions without appropriate safeguards.

I'm talking about intentionally distributing meaningful authority to the people and teams closest to the information, the customer, and the work.

This is what I mean by decentralized empowerment.

In a traditional hierarchical organization, intelligence frequently travels upward so that authority can travel downward. An employee identifies a problem, gathers information, develops a recommendation, presents it to a manager, and waits for approval. That manager may need to escalate the issue again. By the time a decision is made, the opportunity may have changed or disappeared.

AI can dramatically improve the employee's ability to identify the problem and develop a recommendation. But if the decision still requires the same journey through the hierarchy, much of that advantage is lost.

Now imagine a different operating environment.

The employee understands the organization's strategic priorities, has access to relevant information, knows the boundaries of their authority, and is empowered to make decisions that advance clearly defined outcomes. AI helps that employee recognize patterns, evaluate options, anticipate consequences, and act with greater confidence.

The result isn't simply faster work. It's faster organizational learning, more responsive customer service, and greater capacity to create value.

This is where the opportunity becomes truly transformative. AI can accelerate intelligence. Decentralized empowerment allows that intelligence to become action.

Of course, not every decision should be decentralized. Regulatory compliance, security, financial exposure, and other high-risk activities require clear oversight and appropriate controls. But those safeguards should define the boundaries within which people can act, rather than become a justification for requiring permission at every step.

The objective is not uncontrolled autonomy. It is autonomy with clarity, accountability, and purpose.

And that requires a fundamentally different approach to organizational design.

## What Happens When You Combine AI With an Empowered Organization?

Consider two organizations implementing the same AI technology. Both have access to similar models, analytical capabilities, automation tools, and data.

The first organization operates through traditional hierarchies. Decisions are centralized, departments function independently, and employees are measured primarily by the completion of assigned tasks.

AI helps those employees perform their tasks more efficiently. Reports are produced faster. Emails are drafted faster. Analyses are completed faster.

But the underlying operating model remains unchanged. The organization becomes better at executing its existing processes, including the inefficient ones.

Now consider a second organization.

Its teams are aligned around shared outcomes. Decision-making authority is distributed appropriately. Employees understand the boundaries of their autonomy. Collaboration occurs across functions rather than being restricted by them. Customer needs are visible, and performance is measured by meaningful results.

When AI enters this environment, something different becomes possible.

People can act on insights without unnecessary delay. Teams can adapt workflows as new information emerges. Customer problems can be addressed closer to their source. Improvements in one area can be incorporated into other parts of the organization more quickly.

AI doesn't simply accelerate individual tasks. It strengthens the organization's ability to learn, decide, coordinate, and respond.

And because those capabilities reinforce one another, the potential value can become multiplicative rather than merely additive.

That's what I mean when I talk about the possibility of exponential value. Not a guaranteed mathematical outcome from installing AI, but the potential for compounding improvements when technology amplifies an organizational system already designed for autonomy, alignment, and continuous learning.

An employee becomes more effective. The team becomes more responsive. The organization makes better decisions. Customers receive greater value. And the insights generated through those interactions create opportunities for further improvement.

This is a fundamentally different model from simply automating tasks and reducing headcount.

AI is not the foundation of this transformation. Organizational design is. AI becomes the accelerator.

## Why Leadership Must Change Before the Technology Can Deliver Its Full Potential

There is an uncomfortable implication in all of this.

If decentralized empowerment is essential to unlocking the greatest value from AI, then many executives will have to reconsider how they lead.

Traditional management structures often associate authority with position, information with power, and oversight with control. But when employees and teams have access to increasingly sophisticated intelligence, the justification for concentrating routine decisions at the top becomes weaker.

Leadership's role must evolve.

Rather than serving as the central point through which decisions flow, leaders need to establish direction, clarify boundaries, develop capability, remove obstacles, and ensure that people have the information and authority required to achieve shared outcomes.

That doesn't diminish leadership. It makes leadership more consequential.

The leader becomes responsible for creating an environment where the organization can perform effectively without requiring constant intervention.

This is not simply a structural change. It's a behavioral one.

Leaders who have built their identities around being the primary decision-makers may find it difficult to distribute authority. Managers accustomed to directing work may struggle to become coaches and facilitators. Employees who have spent years being punished for mistakes may hesitate to exercise autonomy even when it is formally granted.

That's why organizational redesign must address both operating systems and human behavior.

You cannot announce empowerment and expect people to suddenly feel empowered. You have to redesign decision rights, establish trust, align incentives, build capabilities, and reinforce new behaviors through consistent experience.

AI may make this transformation more urgent. But it cannot do the human work of creating the conditions in which empowerment becomes real.

## Before Cutting People, Measure What AI Is Actually Creating

I believe executives need to apply greater discipline to how they evaluate AI investments, particularly when those investments are being used to justify significant workforce reductions.

Cost savings are important, but they are only one dimension of performance.

A more complete evaluation should examine whether AI is improving the outcomes that determine long-term business success.

Are customers more satisfied? Are problems being resolved more effectively? Are products and services improving? Is innovation accelerating? Are teams making better decisions? Are cycle times decreasing? Are customer relationships strengthening? Is the organization becoming more adaptive?

And, importantly, are those improvements translating into sustainable financial performance?

We should also measure the organizational conditions that make those outcomes possible.

How much authority do employees actually have? How many decisions require unnecessary escalation? How much time is consumed by coordination rather than value creation? How effectively do teams collaborate across functions? How quickly can the organization act on new information?

These are not peripheral management questions. They are central to determining whether AI will produce meaningful business results.

If AI makes employees more productive but decision-making remains slow, the organization hasn't captured the full opportunity.

If AI reduces operating costs but customer satisfaction deteriorates, we need to question the quality of the value being created.

If AI enables workforce reductions but removes institutional knowledge, trusted relationships, or capabilities that are difficult to replace, the financial benefits may conceal longer-term liabilities.

And if AI allows an organization to achieve more with fewer people, that outcome should be demonstrated through credible evidence, not simply assumed because the technology is powerful.

This isn't an argument against responsible automation. It's an argument for holding AI investments to the same standards of business value, customer impact, and leadership accountability that should govern any major transformation.

## The Real AI Revolution Is an Organizational Revolution

I believe AI represents one of the most extraordinary technological opportunities of our lifetime. But I also believe we're at risk of approaching that opportunity with an outdated philosophy of business.

A philosophy that treats employees primarily as costs. That equates productivity with efficiency. That measures success through transactions rather than relationships. And that assumes improving technology will automatically improve the organizations using it.

The evidence increasingly suggests otherwise.

AI can make individuals faster and more capable, but enterprise value depends on how those capabilities are connected, coordinated, and converted into meaningful outcomes.

That is an organizational design challenge. And it is fundamentally a leadership challenge.

The organizations that realize the greatest value from AI may not be those that eliminate the most positions or deploy the most sophisticated tools.

They may be those that recognize the extraordinary opportunity to combine artificial intelligence with human judgment, creativity, empathy, and decentralized empowerment.

Organizations where people are trusted to act. Where teams are aligned around outcomes. Where authority exists close to the customer. Where technology expands human capability rather than simply replacing it. And where success is measured by the value created for customers, employees, and the business as a whole.

The EmpathIQ Framework™ was built around those principles before AI became part of the equation. My belief is that they are even more important now.

Because if we want AI to transform business performance, we must first be willing to transform the environments in which that intelligence operates.

Otherwise, we risk spending extraordinary amounts of money to make fundamentally ineffective organizational structures operate a little faster. And we may sacrifice valuable human capabilities along the way.

The real opportunity is far greater than that.

It requires us to stop asking how many people AI can replace and start asking what people, empowered by AI, can accomplish that was never possible before.

## Key Takeaways

Individual productivity is not the same as organizational performance. AI can make employees significantly more productive without necessarily improving the overall effectiveness or financial performance of the business.

Cost reduction and value creation are fundamentally different objectives. Eliminating positions may improve short-term financial results, but it doesn't automatically improve customer experiences, innovation, or sustainable business performance.

Organizational design determines how effectively AI-generated intelligence becomes business value. Centralized decision-making, unnecessary approvals, fragmented accountability, and organizational silos can prevent individual productivity gains from translating into meaningful outcomes.

Decentralized empowerment is a critical multiplier. When people have the authority to act within clear boundaries, AI can accelerate decisions, strengthen collaboration, and improve responsiveness to customer needs.

The EmpathIQ Framework™ provides the organizational foundation. By integrating Force Field Analysis, Design Thinking, Collaborative OKRs, Self-Management, and Neuroscience-Based Coaching, organizations can unlock greater human and collective potential even without AI. Introducing AI into that environment creates the opportunity for significantly greater, potentially compounding value.

AI transformation must ultimately be measured by the value it creates. Customer outcomes, organizational effectiveness, innovation, adaptability, and sustainable financial performance matter more than simply counting automated tasks or eliminated positions.

## Final Thoughts

“If AI makes your employees more productive, but your first instinct is to eliminate them rather than expand the value your organization creates, perhaps the limitation was never your people or your technology. Perhaps it was your imagination of what your business could become.”

The future of AI won't be determined by technology alone. It will be determined by whether leaders have the courage to redesign their organizations around the full potential of human and artificial intelligence working together.
