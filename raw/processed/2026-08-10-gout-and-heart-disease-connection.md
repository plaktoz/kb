# Gout and heart disease: What's the connection?

People with gout face elevated risk for heart disease, including coronary artery disease, heart failure, and arrhythmias. Gout occurs when uric acid builds up in the bloodstream — either overproduced or under-eliminated — and can form needle-shaped crystals in a joint, triggering an immune attack (a gout flare).

The gout–heart disease link is complex but plausible: shared risk factors like obesity, diabetes, and high blood pressure raise both uric acid levels and cardiovascular risk. Gout flares themselves produce inflammation, and low-grade inflammation can persist between flares — damaging blood-vessel linings and promoting the fatty plaque buildup that reduces blood flow, per Dr. Robert Shmerling (Harvard Health Publishing senior faculty editor, former clinical chief of rheumatology at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center).

Treating gout may lower heart-disease risk: a study in the March 2026 issue of JAMA Internal Medicine found that people who lowered blood uric acid below 6 mg/dL with medication were 9% less likely to have a heart attack, stroke, or die from heart disease over the next five years. Flare-relief medications include NSAIDs, colchicine, and corticosteroids; uric-acid-lowering medications (most commonly allopurinol or febuxostat) are typically recommended for anyone with two or more flares per year.

Lifestyle measures that help both joints and heart: the Mediterranean and DASH diets (vegetables, fruits, whole grains, olive oil, low-fat dairy, fish, legumes, nuts; limiting red meat and added sugars) keep uric acid low and support cardiovascular health, alongside 150–300 minutes/week of moderate aerobic activity and strength training at least twice a week.
