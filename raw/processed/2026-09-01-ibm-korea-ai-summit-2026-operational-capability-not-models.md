# South Korea IBM Chief: "The Core of AI Competition Is Operational Capability, Not Models"

IBM Korea hosted the "IBM AI Summit Korea 2026" in Seoul on September 1st, unveiling its enterprise AI transformation strategy. President Lee Soo-jung emphasized that the core of AI competition lies not in adopting better models, but in the operational capability to connect AI to data, applications, infrastructure, and security systems — and scale it enterprise-wide.

The battleground for enterprise artificial intelligence (AI) adoption is shifting from model performance to operational capability. In her keynote address, Lee Soo-jung, President of IBM Korea, said: "AI competition is no longer about adopting better models. It hinges on the capability to connect AI to enterprise data, applications, infrastructure, and security systems — and to scale it enterprise-wide."

She assessed that enterprise AI adoption has moved beyond the stage of individual productivity gains into a phase that fundamentally changes how work is done, how decisions are made, and how systems interconnect. Document drafting or meeting summarization alone is insufficient; AI must be applied to core business processes and systems to deliver tangible business outcomes.

Lee identified operational capability as the key to successful AI transformation. In enterprise environments, data quality and freshness, application integration, security and access management, cost control, and regulatory compliance must all be managed alongside the AI model itself. "The problem is not the technology itself, but operations," she said. "When AI agents begin performing actual work, you must control what data they can access and what actions they can execute with what permissions."

IBM's core strategies were presented as:
- **AI-First Enterprise**: places AI at the center of enterprise operations and decision-making
- **Hybrid cloud**: flexibly connects distributed data and systems
- **Quantum computing**: for solving highly complex computational problems

The building blocks of an AI-First Enterprise were outlined as "Intelligence" for real-time insights, "Action" for connecting to actual work execution, "Operation" for enterprise-wide execution and management frameworks, and "Trust" encompassing security and governance.

Lee also stressed that AI strategy and cloud strategy cannot be viewed separately. For AI to deliver value, it must run securely where the data resides, and enterprises must be able to manage data and workloads distributed across on-premises environments, public clouds, and multiple application ecosystems in an integrated manner.

## Development and Operations Challenges in the AI Agent Era

Nill Sundaresan, IBM's General Manager of Automation and AI, introduced development, data, and operations management approaches needed for the AI agent era. He characterized AI as the next major shift in enterprise software following the internet, mobile, and cloud — noting that while past technology shifts changed how applications are accessed and deployed, AI is adding intelligence and action capabilities to software itself.

The first requirement he cited was fresh data. For AI agents to make real-time judgments and take action, they need a continuous supply of data generated by current enterprise systems — not data refined days or months earlier.

Sundaresan cited Confluent, the data streaming company IBM acquired, as an example, explaining that connecting real-time data to AI systems is the starting point for agentic AI operations. At the stage of converting data into action, he argued that software development approaches must change. Through "IBM Bob," an AI development partner, he projected that AI will move beyond simply generating code to participating across the entire software development lifecycle.

"Building agents is the easy part," he said. "Deploying them, managing them, securing them, and applying governance is far more difficult." As AI-generated code and agents proliferate, identifying vulnerabilities, policy violations, and failure points becomes increasingly complex.

Agent identity and permission management was raised as a new challenge. In environments where not just humans but numerous agents access enterprise systems, organizations must track which data agents accessed, whose authority they were delegated, and what actions they took. The need to issue time- and purpose-limited credentials instead of long-lived authentication tokens — and to apply the principle of least privilege — is growing.

Cost control was also identified as part of the AI operating model. "Every AI action incurs a cost," Sundaresan said. Organizations must track usage and cloud costs by department, service, and model, and measure outcomes against AI investments.

## Domestic Enterprise Case Studies and Quantum Computing Roadmap

A panel discussion moderated by Lee Ji-eun, CTO and Executive at IBM Korea, featured AI transformation case studies from KB Securities, SK Telecom, and Samsung Life Insurance. Panelists agreed that achieving AI outcomes requires establishing data quality and metadata management foundations, and connecting existing core business systems to AI. In environments with growing numbers of AI agents, they noted the need for integrated management of data access permissions, work execution authority, quality, and cost — along with human verification at critical stages.

Dr. Baek Han-hee of IBM Research presented the quantum computing roadmap. He explained that quantum computing is not a technology that replaces existing supercomputers or GPUs, but rather one pillar of "quantum-centric supercomputing" that combines CPUs, GPUs, and quantum processing units (QPUs). IBM presented a hybrid computing environment — where HPC, AI, and quantum computing each handle the workloads where they excel — as the future direction.

"The value of AI does not end with automating a single task," Lee said. "It lies in redesigning how enterprises work, how decisions are made, and how systems interconnect."
