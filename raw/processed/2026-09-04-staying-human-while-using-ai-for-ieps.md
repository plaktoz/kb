# Staying Human While Using AI for IEPs

Special education teacher Casey Celeste uses AI to dramatically cut IEP paperwork time. Without it, drafting a solid IEP took "four to six hours"; AI has cut that by more than half.

A 2025 University of Virginia study compared teacher-written IEP goals against ChatGPT-drafted ones using identical student profiles. Researchers found "no statistically significant difference in their quality ratings" across six dimensions including clarity and measurability. Most participating teachers rated AI-generated goals as equal or better quality.

Lead researcher Danielle Waterfield, a former special education teacher, notes that over half of special educators feel unprepared to independently write IEPs after completing their training programs. She argues AI can bridge that gap while returning time to direct student interaction — since "the more a student with a disability is face-to-face with their teacher, the better the educational outcomes."

Celeste's approach: she provides a detailed "brain dump" about each student (without identifying information), then lets AI translate those observations into formally structured IEP language. She also uses AI to brainstorm implementation strategies for nonverbal students.

Both Celeste and Waterfield emphasize caution — AI should assist an iterative drafting process, not generate finished products independently. The teacher's knowledge, observations, and judgment remain central. As Celeste puts it, "the human connection between you and the student is the most important part."
