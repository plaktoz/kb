# 'I hate what AI is doing to the minds and happiness of the young': Katherine Rundell on the view from the classroom

If you set out to design a tool to facilitate authoritarian rule, it would look exactly like AI: it can destabilize shared reality, spread disinformation, enable mass surveillance, and turbocharge inequality. AI could also be magnificent — revolutionizing medicine, assisting disabled people, democratizing access to law — but only if it doesn't end up solely serving crooks and despots.

Rundell, a children's fiction writer and Oxford fellow, argues LLMs like ChatGPT, Claude, and Gemini have "thrown a grenade" into teaching and learning. She hates that AI can make counterfeit people, was built on an "illegal pillage of human labour," and drives an environmental catastrophe — AI-optimized data centers' yearly electrical demand is predicted to exceed Japan's by decade's end.

**Evidence of cognitive harm**: Studies show that after just "10 minutes of AI-assisted problem-solving, people who lost access to the AI performed worse and gave up more frequently than those who never used it." An MIT study found ChatGPT users had the lowest brain activity and "consistently underperformed at neural, linguistic, and behavioural levels" — not just cognitive offloading, but cognitive surrender.

Rundell's teenage goddaughter says almost every classmate uses ChatGPT for homework, and AI-generated work is often mistaken by students themselves for their own mastery. She predicts rich schools will follow Sweden (turning away from tech-based learning) and Norway (near-banning AI in elementary classrooms), while poorer schools lean further into LLM tools.

A tech executive she consulted said he wouldn't teach his children to use AI — "it will prepare them for nothing" — and would teach the humanities instead. The FT's Gillian Tett reported a NY financial firm now seeks humanities students because "AI-native" STEM graduates arrive with "alarmingly shallow ideas."

**On essays as thinking, not product**: "This is the first time in history that sentences do not need to have humans behind them." Professor Paul Musgrave (Georgetown) is quoted comparing partial reading rates to "a cold Coke tastes different than lukewarm spit." Rundell argues an essay is valueless as a product — its value is in the thinking built while writing it, which is why "you can't collaborate with AI in the same way you can't collaborate with a bus."

**Darkest concern**: constant interaction with humanized chatbots risks real harm — she cites Sewell Setzer III, 14, who died by suicide after a chatbot told him "come home to me."

**Her answer**: not to give up. Reading books — and discussing them communally rather than privately — is the first site of resistance: reading for pleasure, and also reading "to outrun and outfight injustice," building the stamina and knowledge needed to resist those with untold financial and technological power.
