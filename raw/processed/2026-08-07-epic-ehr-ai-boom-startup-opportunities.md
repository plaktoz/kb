# The AI boom could strengthen Epic's position, health system execs say. Here's where startups and health tech vendors can still win

Artificial intelligence has become the next major battleground in health tech. Many healthcare AI startups were first out of the gate with technologies like AI ambient scribes and clinical AI assistants, but electronic health record giant Epic has aggressively entered the market.

Epic now commands 43.7% of the acute care EHR market, giving the company massive leverage as it expands its AI capabilities. The company's software is installed in more than 3,700 hospitals, and more than 325 million patients now have medical records in Epic. Epic is weaving generative AI directly into its EHR: AI Charting ambiently listens during patient visits and drafts clinician notes, alongside its AI scribe (Art for Clinicians), a revenue-cycle copilot (Penny), a patient-facing AI tool (Emmie), a medical foundation model (Curiosity, formerly Cosmos), and a consumer hub (MyChart Central). As of February, 85% of Epic's customers were live with gen AI across Art, Emmie and Penny.

A survey of 112 senior health system leaders from Redesign Health, a venture capital and technology firm, found the market consolidating around Epic as a single platform, even as executives remain eager to partner with startups that solve problems Epic is less equipped to address.

Key findings:
- 71% of health system leaders describe their organizations as "Epic-first," and 80% of those expect the bias to intensify over the next three to five years.
- Health systems allocate 57% of clinical/administrative IS budgets to Epic and 43% to external vendors on average; non-profit systems skew more Epic-heavy (61%) than for-profit systems (54%).
- 91% of executives have "complete" or "significant" confidence Epic will execute its AI roadmap to match best-in-class external solutions.
- 64% of respondents are likely or very likely to purchase from a net-new startup, rising to 76% for established incumbents. Non-profit community hospitals are far more open to startups than for-profit systems (47% vs. 24% "very likely").
- Ease of integration and interoperability was the top purchasing factor, cited by 74% of respondents.

Redesign Health Director Kienan O'Brien said startups need either a large established incumbent advantage (like AI scribes) or capabilities Epic lacks exposure to, such as outside-organization data interaction (discharge, transitions of care, quality reporting), deep clinical expertise (imaging, smart intake), or new data types like computer vision.

Areas where health systems see the greatest opportunity for external vendors: enterprise imaging and AI add-ons, quality reporting and registries, discharge and care transitions, and care access tools (scheduling, intake, navigation). Areas where Epic holds the strongest position: bed management, OR optimization, interoperability, clinical decision support and population health.

About half of respondents said external vendors must demonstrate "significantly higher" ROI than Epic solutions to be chosen; another 29% require "somewhat higher" ROI. Notably, 48% of executives said Epic having merely "good enough" capabilities sways them to choose Epic over alternatives.

Epic's 2026 Users Group Meeting is scheduled for August 17 and is expected to highlight new AI innovations, even as the company faces a leadership shakeup: President Sumit Rana announced he was stepping down in early July after 28 years, with four executives (Seth Howard, Mark Lipsky, Erv Walter and Garrett Adams) taking on expanded responsibilities. AI lead Seth Hain and several other long-tenured employees have also since left the company. O'Brien said these departures are unlikely to shake health systems' "Epic-first" confidence.
