# Hubbis Macro Corner: US Yields Rebound as AI Stocks Diverge

## At a Glance

US hiring held up and underlying inflation stayed firm. October payrolls rose by 165,000, while the headline consumer price index rose 0.4 percent on the month and the core index rose 0.3 percent. Missing October 2025 data prevented official annual comparisons for both measures. [1, 2]

The Treasury rally gave way to rising yields. The US two-year yield climbed from 4.51 percent on 6 November to 4.86 percent on 12 November. This week’s US$42 billion 10-year note and US$25 billion 30-year bond auctions cleared at 5.020 percent and 5.441 percent respectively. [5, 7]

AI shares responded differently to company news. Nvidia fell sharply after Jensen Huang’s departure was announced, while the broader US equity decline was more modest. TSMC’s sales rose sharply and CoreWeave’s results showed how much financing rapid expansion can require. [9, 10, 12, 13]

Asia’s inflation figures showed contrasting pressures. China’s October producer prices rose 5.7 percent year on year against a 0.7 percent rise in consumer prices. In India, stronger food inflation pushed headline inflation to 6.09 percent, just above the Reserve Bank of India’s upper tolerance limit. [15, 17, 18]

Brent rebounded as supply risks resurfaced. The benchmark settled at US$95.82 a barrel on Thursday after disruptions and threats to Middle East supply. The UK’s new GDP estimate showed faster third-quarter growth, led by services, with construction output still falling. [20, 21]

Last week’s edition closed with investors seeking protection in US Treasuries as AI shares sold off. This week, US hiring held up and a firmer-than-expected inflation report drove yields higher. The S&P 500 still recovered part of its earlier losses. At the same time, fresh company results showed why it is becoming harder to assess the AI trade through a single equity index.

For Asian wealth managers and family offices, the week offered a useful comparison. Government bonds responded differently to an inflation surprise than they had to an equity shock. Within AI, a chipmaker’s sales, a cloud operator’s contracts and the operator’s cost of financing all told different parts of the story. The practical question is which of those risks a portfolio is taking and whether it is being paid to take them.

## US Payrolls Recover and Core Inflation Holds Its Pace

The October employment report, released after last Friday’s Asia morning cut-off, showed a 165,000 increase in nonfarm payrolls. Unemployment was little changed at 4.1 percent. The US Bureau of Labor Statistics revised August and September payroll gains up by a combined 84,000, taking September’s gain from 29,000 to 60,000. Average hourly earnings on private nonfarm payrolls rose 0.4 percent on the month. [1]

On 10 November, headline consumer prices rose 0.4 percent on a seasonally adjusted monthly basis, down from 0.6 percent in September but above the 0.3 percent expected by economists polled by Reuters. Energy prices rose much less than in September, while core CPI increased 0.3 percent for a third consecutive month. Because the BLS did not collect survey data in October 2025 during a lapse in US government funding, it could not publish the usual October year-on-year figures for headline or core CPI. The monthly figures show slower headline inflation and an unchanged pace for core prices. [2, 3]

Citi Wealth’s 10 November update looked at how widely the increases were spread. On its measure, 56 percent of CPI components rose at an annualised monthly pace of at least 5 percent, up from 44 percent in September. Ahead of the release, Federal Reserve Governor Christopher Waller said a core reading of around 0.3 percent or higher would warrant considering a 50-basis-point increase in December. The result met the condition he set out. Traders increased their bets on a move of that size, although it remained a minority outcome in market pricing on Friday morning. The next US producer price release was due later on Friday, after this edition’s cut-off. [2, 3, 4, 6, 11, 23]

## Treasury Auctions Meet a Market Repricing Rates

Treasury data show where the inflation surprise landed. The two-year yield rose 17 basis points on Tuesday, from 4.56 percent to 4.73 percent, while the 10-year yield rose 10 basis points to 4.98 percent. By Thursday, the two-year was at 4.86 percent, the 10-year at 5.10 percent and the 30-year at 5.43 percent. Shorter yields rose more over the period. Longer bonds are more sensitive to a change in yield, so a smaller increase can still mean a substantial price decline. [3, 5]

The Treasury also held the auctions for the US$125 billion refunding flagged in the previous edition. Tuesday’s US$42 billion 10-year auction cleared at 5.020 percent. Thursday’s US$25 billion 30-year auction cleared at 5.441 percent, slightly above the 5.437 percent market yield at auction time, according to The Wall Street Journal. That small gap suggests buyers required a modest concession on the day. The auction result is the yield at which the new issue sold; the daily par yield is Treasury’s estimate of the market yield at that maturity. [5, 7, 8]

The comparison with last week matters for portfolios that own bonds as a hedge. Treasuries gained when the equity sell-off drove demand for protection, then lost ground when inflation put the Fed’s next move back in play. A bond held to fund a known future payment can still serve that purpose through both moves. A position intended to offset equity losses has to be assessed against the source of the sell-off, especially where higher yields can also put pressure on the equity holdings.

## Nvidia’s Leadership Change and the Cost of AI Growth

Nvidia announced on 12 November that co-founder Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive and that finance chief Colette Kress would serve as interim CEO while retaining her finance role. Nvidia shares fell as much as 14 percent early in Thursday’s session before recovering part of the loss. The S&P 500 and Nasdaq Composite each closed down 0.4 percent, and the S&P 500 remained 2.9 percent above the previous Friday’s close. The response in Asia was also more measured than the previous Friday’s sell-off: in Friday morning trading, Reuters reported gains of about 1.4 percent in Japan’s Nikkei 225 and 2.35 percent in South Korea’s Kospi. [9, 10, 11]

Other releases gave investors fresh evidence on demand. TSMC reported October revenue of NT$586.89 billion, up 59.7 percent from a year earlier. Applied Materials reported a 53 percent year-on-year increase in fiscal fourth-quarter revenue to US$10.41 billion and US$2.6 billion in quarterly free cash flow. Those sales show what manufacturers and equipment suppliers are earning from the investment cycle. The returns on the computing capacity their customers are building will depend on how much it is used and what customers pay for it. [12, 14]

AI cloud operator CoreWeave reported third-quarter revenue of US$3.66 billion, up from US$1.37 billion a year earlier. Net interest expense of US$846 million exceeded operating income of US$207 million, and the company posted a US$709 million net loss. It generated US$1.98 billion in operating cash flow and spent US$9.44 billion on property, equipment and capitalised software during the quarter. Its US$132.5 billion revenue backlog depends on meeting delivery and service availability requirements. Management expects about US$9 billion in customer prepayments and deposits over the following three months, making the timing of those receipts relevant to the company’s financing needs. [13]

BlackRock Investment Institute’s 9 November commentary framed the issue as competition for capital between AI, governments and other large borrowers, including those investing in energy and defence. CoreWeave’s disclosures make that question concrete. Strong orders can support financing, but investors still need to know how much cash arrives before the equipment and interest bills do. The answer can differ across the companies a client owns, even when those companies are all selling into the same expansion. [13, 22]

## China and India Face Different Sources of Inflation

China’s National Bureau of Statistics reported on 9 November that October consumer prices rose 0.7 percent from a year earlier, while factory gate prices rose 5.7 percent. Raw materials prices at the factory gate were up 11.6 percent. Separately, customs data released on 10 November showed that goods exports rose 35.2 percent and imports rose 41.6 percent year on year in US dollar terms. The figures give separate readings on trade and prices: strong growth in the nominal value of shipments can coexist with modest consumer inflation and much faster price increases for industrial producers. For companies paying higher input costs, the key question is how much of the increase their customers will absorb. [15, 16]

India’s provisional October consumer inflation rate rose to 6.09 percent from 5.54 percent in September. Food inflation rose from 7.74 percent to 8.94 percent. The headline result edged above the 6 percent upper limit of the RBI’s tolerance band around its 4 percent target. A food-driven increase and a rise in industrial input prices pose different problems for local bond investors. In India, food prices are directly affecting household purchasing power and the policy outlook. In China, the large gap between producer and consumer inflation puts more emphasis on how pricing power differs across businesses. [15, 17, 18]

In Japan, the Bank of Japan’s 10 November summary of its October meeting showed differing views on how quickly to move towards a neutral policy rate. Some opinions favoured a quicker adjustment; others stressed monitoring the impact of past increases. The yen traded close to 160 per US dollar on Friday morning. In its quarterly report released on 12 November, the New York Fed disclosed that the US Treasury had bought yen between July and September. For investors measuring Japanese returns in another currency, both the rate differential and the possibility of official intervention matter when assessing an unhedged yen position. [11, 19, 24]

## Oil Supply Risks Return and UK Services Lift Growth

Brent crude futures settled 3.3 percent higher on Thursday at US$95.82 a barrel after reports of attacks on Saudi energy infrastructure and renewed disruption to shipping through the Strait of Hormuz. The move came a day after Reuters reported, citing OPEC data, that OPEC+ output in October had returned to roughly 97 percent of its February level. The distinction is between what is pumped and what reaches refineries. For economies importing fuel, the duration of disruptions to shipping and processing can matter more for inflation than the headline production figure. [20, 25]

The UK’s first estimate, released on 12 November, put real GDP growth at 0.7 percent in the third quarter, up from 0.5 percent in the second. Services output grew 0.9 percent, while construction fell 0.7 percent. The quarterly figures describe activity before the Bank of England’s November rate increase. They show that the economy entered this period with stronger overall growth than in the prior quarter, even as parts of it were already contracting. For sterling portfolios, this makes the performance of individual sectors and borrowers more informative than the GDP headline alone. [21]

## What This Means for Wealth Managers and Family Offices

The last two weeks gave advisers two useful scenarios for testing their bond holdings: an equity sell-off that drew investors into Treasuries and an inflation surprise that sent yields higher. A bond allocation intended to cushion equity losses can be tested against both. When the purpose is to fund a known payment, the maturity and currency of the cash flows matter more than how the bond trades on a particular day.

In AI, look at cash received and cash committed over the same period. A prepayment expected next quarter can affect near-term financing more than a much larger backlog spread over several years. For portfolios that hold an operator, its suppliers and the lenders funding its expansion, compare where those exposures share customers or rely on the same delivery dates. The breadth of the holdings can otherwise overstate how much risk is diversified.

Hubbis’s assessment is that the equity rebound left two important questions open: how far rates may need to rise and which businesses can fund growth at those rates. For Asian investors in US assets, the currency exposure deserves a separate decision. If the dollar strengthens against a client’s home currency, it can cushion a loss on an unhedged US holding when translated back into that currency. It also makes a dollar liability more expensive to meet from home-currency cash.
