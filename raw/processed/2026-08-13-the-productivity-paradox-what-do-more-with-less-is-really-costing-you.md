# The Productivity Paradox: What 'Do More With Less' Is Really Costing You

Andy, a funny and empathetic nursing director at a regional healthcare system, said, "Our role has changed a lot in the last five years, but our job description has not. Fifty percent of my job is now therapist, psychologist and relationship counselor. I come to my office after a meeting, and there is a line of people waiting for me to talk."

Everyone in the room started laughing. Andy wasn't the only one experiencing new demands in her role. She was not exaggerating. She was describing a new reality—a newly acquired second job that remains undisclosed.

This story is playing out across every industry right now, not just healthcare.

When we run a root cause analysis on people challenges, the answer usually traces itself back to a leadership practice. In this case, we tracked the challenge to a misguided staffing policy that has quietly reshaped nursing education, recruiting, onboarding, training and the expectations of new nurses (versus reality) in intensive care units.

The second job Andy is talking about has a strong impact on retention. Our need to control originates from fear of uncertainty. The opposite of fear is not stability, but a strong sense of community. When employees feel seen, heard and validated, they tend to stay and become more determined to support change. Our innate human need is to bring value and feel valued, yet digital overload and profit-hijacked companies are taking their toll on trust. This workforce recognizes that they are replaceable, and the drive to make more and work less is part of their response system.

## The Math Does Not Add Up

Managers are expected to produce more, automate faster, all while holding their teams together. AI claims to buy back time, yet research finds that automation frequently expands what people do rather than shrink their workload. An eight-month Berkeley Haas study followed a 200-person tech company. It concluded that employees are working faster, taking on more task loads, and staying connected longer to solve challenges they face. In fact, heavy users of AI reported higher burnout than non-users. AI tools are evolving, but doing more with less is not.

Managers face a workforce that is less equipped to absorb disruption than generations before it. Aflac's most recent workforce survey reports that Gen Z has overtaken millennials as the most burned-out generation, with 74% sharing moderate to intense burnout. The American Psychological Association concluded that 65% of workers, ages 26 to 43, identify job insecurity as a top stressor. Among 18- to 25-year-olds, that figure is 75%. Instead of starting their adult lives with a curious and optimistic mindset, the newest workforce is bringing along significant distress and anxiety.

The math does not add up. The APA Workforce report shows a significant perception gap between how senior leaders feel about change versus people executing it. While most leaders share optimism about change, their frontline teams share stories of overwhelm and constant rework. Managers are navigating these two realities. They show enthusiasm when they are handed another change, and must translate strategy while absorbing the imminent distress employees feel from more change. That mental absorption is not in their job description either.

The newest workforce is showing significant gaps in social readiness and resilience, shaped by years of disrupted schooling and global uncertainty. The labor market is experiencing the largest disruption in its history of work. Managers are learning to manage around a workforce that expects a quality of life that does not get consumed by work demands. Doing more with less is not only a work output challenge, but a driver for retention. Managers are facing a productivity paradox against a generation that expects more personal time and clearer boundaries.

## The Manager Role Calls For A Redesign

When a system change is implemented, the impact lands on the manager, who picks up the operational and emotional burden with insufficient communication and updates on scope or impact. While Andy is not expected to become her unit's therapist, her motivation is to keep things moving or fail. When staffing drops, no one else is positioned to take on the pain.

Across every industry running lean, you have an entire layer of managers doing multiple jobs under one title. What you might perceive as incompetence is a real struggle for managers to hold their teams accountable.

## Resilience Is Not A Slogan

You have likely invested thousands in workshops to teach people resilience. The question is how vested leaders are in bringing along the conditions that produce it. How confident are you in nurturing trust, predictable decision-making and psychological safety? How confident are you that managers feel heard, supported and validated?

How often does your team experience steadiness instead of showing up quiet, stressed or exhausted?

A wellness workshop gives you the time to breathe… today. Resilience requires a daily commitment to breathing, pausing, reflecting and solving problems together. It is built the same way we nurture trust—through consistent, repeated behaviors that override the unexpected challenges our brain searches for to keep us safe.

## What This Actually Requires

This dilemma cannot be solved by adopting one more smart tool. The solution begins with exploring the root cause of the issue. Through clarity, you begin to build reliable support systems that drive clear decision making, and sincere collaboration across disciplines. When you support workload audits that reflect emotional and task labor, you train your managers to hold the hard conversations before they become resignations. We want to create a space where learning closes the infinite loop of confusion.

A more deliberate approach to discovery and root cause analysis will initiate the right strategies that can uncover and recover what might be lurking in plain sight. The role of the manager has changed. How you approach the productivity paradox can help you drive change faster, with better results. When you include your managers in problem solving and decision making, they become determined to hold themselves and others accountable to support change beyond launch.
