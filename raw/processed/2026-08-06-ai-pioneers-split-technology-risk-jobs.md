# AI pioneers are split on the technology's risk to jobs

Even among AI's pioneers, there isn't consensus on the technology's biggest challenges, or just how profoundly it will reshape society. At Ai4, dubbed the "world's largest AI conference," in Las Vegas, several AI trailblazers sparred over the technology's biggest opportunities and risks.

Sharing the stage during the keynote were Nobel Prize laureate and University Professor Emeritus at the University of Toronto, Geoffrey Hinton, known as the "godfather of AI"; the "godmother of AI" Fei-Fei Li, a Stanford professor, co-founder and CEO of AI systems startup World Labs, and former chief scientist of AI at Google Cloud; and Andrew Ng, co-founder of Coursera, founder of DeepLearning.AI and former head of Google Brain.

Together, they offered starkly different perspectives on AI's biggest risks, debating job displacement, open-source models, regulation, and the possibility of superintelligent systems. While Hinton warned that society should take AI's long-term dangers seriously, Li and Ng argued that much of today's public conversation has become dominated by fear rather than facts.

"If nurses and doctors become much more efficient, society will simply consume more healthcare. But in jobs like call centers, where demand isn't as 'elastic,' AI-driven productivity is more likely to reduce the number of workers needed," Hinton said. Asked whether AI has already surpassed human intelligence, Hinton said not yet, but perhaps not for much longer: "We're getting to the point when it's going to be smarter than us at everything," estimating that could happen within "the next few to 20 years," or "probably less."

Ng argued that many of the industry's biggest fears have been overstated and are causing "fearmongering." He pushed back on the idea that AI will simply replace knowledge workers, arguing instead that the technology is changing the scope of jobs by automating repetitive work and letting employees take on broader, more strategic responsibilities. "We're not laying off marketing coordinators," Ng said — instead they're becoming "full-cycle marketers." He said the same pattern is emerging in software engineering, where developers spend less time writing code and more time designing systems, coordinating projects, and solving higher-order problems.

Li largely agreed, arguing that conversations about AI and jobs have become overly simplistic. Rather than asking whether AI will replace workers, she said it's more useful to recognize that every profession is made up of many different tasks — AI may automate some while making others more valuable. She urged policymakers, educators, and business leaders to focus on "good, healthy, scientific communication" that helps society understand AI as "a tool" rather than framing it through dystopian or utopian extremes.

Despite their differences, the three pioneers found common ground on one point: education will determine whether AI ultimately benefits society. That theme carried into a later presentation from economist Anton Korinek, who recently joined Anthropic's Economic Research team while on leave from the University of Virginia. Korinek predicted the AI economy could increasingly separate from the human economy, with AI driving exponential gains in research, healthcare, and scientific progress while reshaping the value of human work. The jobs that remain, he argued, are likely to center less on routine cognitive tasks and more on governance, oversight, interpersonal skills, and ensuring AI systems serve human interests. "If labor suddenly becomes reproducible," Korinek said, "economic growth is going to accelerate even more." But he warned that because human labor would become less scarce, "the share of labor in the economy as a whole is likely to decline."
