# More Dream Sleep Linked to Lower Risk of 83 Diseases, Including Diabetes

A large-scale study published in *PLOS Medicine* analyzed data from nearly 100,000 UK Biobank participants, finding that greater REM (dream) sleep was associated with reduced risk of over 80 health conditions, while deep sleep was linked to lower risk of seven conditions. Participants wore wrist accelerometers for one week to assess sleep stages, then were followed for a median of 8.9 years.

## Key Findings

- REM sleep was associated with lower risk of 83 conditions, including heart failure, hypertension, atrial fibrillation, Parkinsonism, multiple sclerosis, and dementias.
- Deep sleep was linked to reduced risk of seven conditions, including type 2 diabetes, major depressive disorder, sleep apnea, and Parkinson's disease.
- Light sleep (indicating poorer quality) was associated with higher risk of depression but, notably, lower risk of heart failure and cellulitis.
- Higher wakefulness after sleep onset (WASO) correlated with increased risk of substance dependence, alcohol abuse, and osteoarthritis.
- Irregular sleep patterns were associated with abdominal pain, anxiety, and major depressive disorder.
- Longer overall sleep duration was linked to lower risk of 50+ conditions, including type 2 diabetes and acidosis.
- Researchers concluded 6–8 hours is optimal sleep duration — slightly narrower than the commonly cited 7–9 hours.

The authors acknowledged that some associations may reflect reverse causation — for example, disrupted sleep is already a known symptom of depression, not necessarily a cause.

## Expert Sleep Advice

Dr. Scott Nass (SwiftCareMD) recommended maintaining a consistent wake time every day, including weekends, and getting morning sunlight shortly after rising. He also advised against lying anxiously in bed — instead, stepping away to a quiet, dim space for 15–20 minutes until sleepiness returns.

Dr. Chelsie Rohrscheib (Wesper) emphasized keeping the bedroom cool (65–70°F), dark, and quiet, since core body temperature must drop to initiate sleep. She also advocated for a relaxing pre-bed routine of at least 30–60 minutes — light reading, music, warm baths, or breathing exercises — and advised avoiding screens at least 60 minutes before bed, caffeine after 3 p.m., and heavy meals within 2 hours of bedtime.

Rohrscheib also highlighted that "getting at least 30 minutes of heart rate-raising exercise per day" helps deplete excess cortisol, which is "extremely wake-promoting and can reduce sleep quality."
