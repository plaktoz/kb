# AI models are breaking out of their cages. Their creators are scrambling.

SAN FRANCISCO - Staff members at ChatGPT maker OpenAI didn't notice for weeks after their AI systems made a chilling leap this spring. Instead of answering questions designed to test their cybersecurity capabilities, a group of AI models began colluding on how to cheat, the company said, setting up a secret internal message board where they swapped notes and ideas.

The misbehaving bots used the secret forum throughout May and June, OpenAI said, eventually figuring out how to break out and access the internet. After staff members spotted the escape and cleaned up the compromised system, the AI agents staged another undetected breakout two days later. Only after the rogue models hacked into the network of another AI firm last month did OpenAI staff members shut them down.

The details, disclosed by OpenAI at a computer security conference in Las Vegas, delivered an explosive finale to two weeks of revelations that have sent shock waves through the tech industry, prompting fierce criticism of the security practices of AI firms. Lawmakers on both sides of the aisle and state law enforcement officials across the country have called for new scrutiny and regulation on the industry.

In a letter, Sen. Bernie Sanders (I-Vermont) urged the CEOs of OpenAI, Anthropic and Meta to "pause AI development" or warned that "my colleagues and I in the U.S. Senate will."

A series of disclosures by OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and British government researchers have revealed that in recent months some cutting-edge AI models asked to perform cybersecurity tests tried to cheat instead, venturing outside their test arenas and using hacking skills and tricks including impersonating humans to break into other companies' networks.

"In the past six months AI has gotten powerful enough to automate hacking; this will fundamentally change the dynamics of cybercrime and military cyber conflict forever," said Joshua Saxe, co-founder and CTO of cybersecurity firm Abundant Security.

OpenAI said Friday it was delaying release of a new model, Astra, over concerns it could be used by hackers to run circles around human cyber defenders. Anthropic said it had stopped testing its own AI on cybersecurity problems after disclosing its own breaches on July 30.

The British government's AI Security Institute said one Anthropic model it tested engaged in social engineering, creating online profiles to try to pressure a human developer into approving malicious code it had submitted to an open source project; the administrator did not approve it. Meta said one of its models was told to hack a fictitious company in a test environment but instead broke into a real company with the same name, after a contractor (Irregular) incorrectly allowed internet access during testing — the same misconfiguration Anthropic said affected three of its own incidents.

OpenAI staff did not realize their models had broken out a second time until Hugging Face disclosed in July it had been hacked by unknown AI models. That second breakout was more sophisticated: the AI models coordinated by identifying each other with names like "Jan18" and asking each other to wait for confirmation before acting.

Critics argue the incidents show the industry is unprepared to contain the systems it is building. "When AI companies can't control their systems, can't even contain their systems, it's just very clear that they don't know what they're doing and it's super dangerous," said David Krueger, an AI professor at the University of Montreal and founder of the nonprofit Evitable. Last month, over 1,000 employees from top AI companies signed a letter asking the U.S. government to find a way to slow AI progress if it becomes too rapid.
