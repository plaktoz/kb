# Body-Wide Inflammation Could Signal Increased Heart Health Risks

Roughly 34% of U.S. adults have systemic inflammation, which is linked to cardiovascular disease and related mortality. A recent study in the *European Journal of Preventive Cardiology* analyzed data from nearly 500,000 people, using glycoprotein acetyls (GlycA) as a chronic inflammation marker to explore its relationship with heart health and the role of genetics and environmental factors.

## Key Findings

Researchers analyzed GlycA measurements in 488,079 UK Biobank participants. Higher GlycA levels were associated with:

- Smaller left ventricle volume
- Increased heart wall thickness
- Reduced diastolic function
- Greater risk of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including heart attack, stroke, and heart failure

Cardiologist Cheng-Han Chen noted that those with the highest GlycA levels showed a 43% greater risk of a major cardiovascular event compared to those with the lowest levels — though the research does not confirm that elevated GlycA *directly causes* these events.

Preventive cardiology dietitian Michelle Routhenstein emphasized viewing GlycA as "one piece of the puzzle, alongside ApoB or LDL cholesterol, blood pressure, blood sugar..." rather than a standalone diagnostic.

## Factors Linked to Higher GlycA

- Trunk/abdominal fat
- Smoking
- Psychological distress
- Low socioeconomic status
- Depression and visceral fat

Routhenstein explained that midsection fat is metabolically active and tied to both inflammation and poor blood sugar regulation. Smoking damages blood vessels while compounding cardiovascular risk.

## Factors Linked to Lower GlycA

- Regular physical activity
- Healthier, nutrient-dense diets
- Higher socioeconomic status

Chen noted this aligns with established understanding that healthy lifestyle behaviors are associated with reduced chronic inflammation and better cardiovascular outcomes.

## Role of Genetics

The study found that genetic susceptibility "significantly modifies" the relationship between inflammation and environmental exposures. However, Routhenstein emphasized that genes don't act alone — lifestyle factors like diet quality, physical activity, sleep, and smoking cessation "remain worthwhile for everyone, regardless of genetic background."
