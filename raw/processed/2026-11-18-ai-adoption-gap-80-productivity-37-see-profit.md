# AI Adoption Gap: 80% Productivity, 37% See Profit

Most employees who use AI at work say it makes them faster. Far fewer executives expect it to move the income statement. That gap sat at the center of a 20-minute session on November 12, 2026, when Utsav Garg of Alvarez & Marsal told the Singapore Business Review Technology Enterprise Summit that the upward curve of AI returns is not automatic. About 80% of AI users reported significant personal productivity gains, he said, while only 37% believed their companies could see an impact on profit and loss.

Garg is Managing Director and Head of Southeast Asia and Australia at Alvarez & Marsal. His talk, titled “The AI Adoption Gap: Why early adopters pull ahead, and how to catch up,” fit the summit’s theme of “AI at Scale: Proving Enterprise Value.” Coverage from Singapore Business Review, Alvarez & Marsal, and syndicated outlets including EIN Presswire, PR Newswire and Yahoo Tech repeated the same core numbers. This analysis walks through what was reported, what the figures can and cannot prove, and what the AI adoption gap means for buyers, vendors and investors.

The timing matters. Boards are asking where the returns are, and the question of whether document management decides enterprise AI ROI is only one example of how the debate has shifted from model quality to operating discipline. Garg’s argument lands squarely in that shift. Better models help, but the reported evidence points at people, incentives and process design as the real constraint.

Singapore Business Review organized the day around one question: how do enterprises prove that AI delivers value once pilots end? The agenda slotted Garg’s session from 2:00 to 2:20 PM, a short window that forced a tight argument rather than a product tour.

Executives across Singapore, Indonesia, Malaysia, Thailand, Vietnam and Australia face similar pressure to show returns, yet they work with very different data rules, labor markets and cloud footprints. A session aimed at all of them has to stay general, which is why the framework he offered concerns allocation of effort rather than any one vendor stack.

It keeps the debate on what organizations do with AI instead of which logo is on the tool.

## The AI Adoption Gap in Numbers: 80% vs 37%

Reports on the session cite a survey in which about 80% of AI users described significant improvements in personal productivity. In the same reporting, 37% believed their companies could see an impact on profit and loss. The distance between those two figures is 43 percentage points, which is simple subtraction on our part and not a number Garg presented.

Two cautions apply. First, the 37% reflects belief about possible impact, not measured profit. Second, the survey populations and sample sizes were not part of the published fact base, so treat the figures as directional. They still describe a pattern that finance chiefs recognize. Staff feel faster, yet the quarterly numbers barely twitch.

## Why Personal Productivity Does Not Become Profit

An analyst who drafts a report in 40 minutes instead of two hours has gained time. Whether the company gains money depends on what happens to those saved hours. If the analyst simply finishes early, the benefit evaporates into the workday. If the team takes on more billable work, ships a product sooner, or cuts a cost line, the benefit reaches the P&L. Garg’s point, as reported, is that nothing guarantees the second outcome.

## The measurement problem

Most firms measure AI usage, not AI outcomes. Seat counts, prompt volumes and self-reported time savings are easy to collect. Revenue per employee, cycle time on a defined process and cost per transaction are harder, because they require a baseline and agreement on who owns the result. Without that baseline, the 80% figure and the 37% figure can both be true at once.

## The incentive problem

People optimize for what the organization rewards. If a manager’s bonus does not change when a team automates a workflow, the team has little reason to rebuild that workflow. Garg’s 141-versus-3 comparison, covered below, points straight at this issue. The technology was identical, so the difference lived in how each organization motivated and organized its people.

## 141 AI Solutions vs 3: Same Technology, Different Outcome

The most vivid detail in the coverage is a comparison of two organizations using the same technology over two years. One produced 141 AI solutions. The other produced three. Alvarez & Marsal’s summary of the talk and Singapore Business Review’s report both frame the gap as an execution story and not a tooling story.

A 47-to-1 spread is hard to explain with model quality. Both firms had access to the same stack. The difference had to come from how many teams were allowed, encouraged and equipped to build, from who owned the backlog, and from whether leadership treated AI as a priority or a side project. Energy-sector vendors have started to package that thinking into products, as seen in how Quorum’s Qnect AI places agents across four energy workflows, from field work to finance. Workflow-first design is the common thread.

The comparison does not say what counts as an “AI solution,” how big each one was, or whether the 141 delivered measurable returns. A count of solutions is an activity metric. Still, a firm that ships 141 and learns from each will compound its knowledge faster than one that ships three and waits for results.

## Why Early Adopters Pull Ahead

The session title promises an answer to “why early adopters pull ahead,” and the fact base supports a few plausible mechanisms. The first is learning speed. Teams that started earlier have already hit the awkward failures, such as poor data, unclear ownership and brittle prompts, and they have fixed them. Latecomers meet the same walls on a later calendar date.

The second is internal credibility. Once a function has a working example, the next function asks for the same help instead of arguing about whether AI is worth trying. The third is talent. Employees with real AI delivery experience are scarce, and they gravitate toward employers that let them ship. Each mechanism feeds the next, which is how a small head start becomes a 141-versus-3 gap.

None of this means late movers are doomed. Garg’s framing, per the title of his talk, includes how to catch up. The argument is that catching up requires deliberate work on process and people, and that copying the tool stack alone buys little.

## Historical Context: From Solow’s Paradox to the AI J-Curve

Economists have seen this movie before. In 1987, Robert Solow remarked that computers showed up everywhere except in the productivity statistics. A few years later, economic historian Paul David argued that factories took decades to gain from electricity because they first had to redesign floor layouts and work routines around electric motors. Researchers later gave the pattern a name, the productivity J-curve: measured output dips or stalls while organizations invest in complements, then rises sharply.

Garg’s message maps cleanly onto that history. Technology arrives first, and the complementary changes in process, skills and management arrive later. His 70% allocation to people and processes is the modern version of rebuilding the factory floor. His remark that returns will not simply arrive on their own echoes the lesson of every earlier general-purpose technology.

What differs now is speed. Model vendors ship upgrades in months, and the claims keep escalating, as in the reported gains and lower costs we covered for OpenAI’s GPT-6.2 Astra. Cheaper, stronger models lower the technology cost, which only raises the relative importance of everything else. If the model share of spend is already small, a further price cut barely shifts the economics of a failed rollout.

## What Garg Said: Verbatim Quotes From the Summit

Singapore Business Review captured several direct remarks. On the idea that returns will come on their own, Garg said:

“The upward curve is not automatic. It’s not like you know over a period of time, we’ll automatically all get there. There has to be work required.”

Asked in effect whether strong usage and spending add up to a bubble, he separated demand from profit:

“There is genuine demand [for AI]. However, that is not a guarantee for profits.”

He closed on the competitive cost of waiting:

“If we don’t do it, somebody else will, and hesitation can be fatal here.”

The three remarks form one argument. Demand is real, profit is not guaranteed, and standing still carries its own risk. That balance separates his message from both the boosters who promise automatic returns and the skeptics who call the whole market a bubble.

## Market Impact: What the Gap Means for CFOs, Vendors and Consultants

For finance chiefs, the 37% figure is a warning label. Budget approvals based on self-reported productivity gains rest on weak evidence. Expect more CFOs to demand baselines, named owners and stage gates before releasing the next tranche of funding, which pushes enterprise AI ROI from a slide-deck phrase to a line in the quarterly review.

For software vendors, the 10/20/70 split suggests that selling models alone leaves most of the customer’s spend untouched. Vendors that bundle change management, templates and measurement tooling can claim a larger share of the 70%. Those that sell raw capability risk competing on price against ever cheaper alternatives.

For consultancies, including Garg’s own firm, the framework describes a service opportunity, and readers should weigh that context. A talk that says most value sits in process redesign is also a pitch for process redesign help. The argument holds up on its own logic, but it comes from a firm that sells that work.

For labor markets, the picture is tangled. Our coverage of tech layoffs reaching 206,817 workers alongside a near-$700 billion AI infrastructure estimate shows firms trimming headcount while they pour money into capacity. If returns lag, that combination turns uncomfortable fast. The adoption gap is partly a story about whether the spending and the staffing decisions can both be justified.

## Risks and Counterarguments

A fair reading must test the claims. The 80% figure rests on self-reporting, and people tend to overestimate time saved. The 37% figure reflects opinion about the future. Neither number comes with a published sample, so the gap could narrow or widen in a better-designed study.

The 10/20/70 split is a heuristic. A frontier lab building models should obviously spend far more than 10% on algorithms. The ratio fits enterprises that buy rather than build, and the reports do not claim universal validity. Likewise, the 141-versus-3 comparison involves two unnamed organizations, so readers cannot check industry, size or definitions.

Speed also carries danger. Pushing 141 solutions into production without governance invites data exposure. The incident we reported on the Chat & Ask AI leak, which exposed 300 million messages from 25 million users, shows how fast poorly governed AI products can turn into liabilities. Part of the 70% people-and-process budget must therefore go to security review, access rules and vendor due diligence.

## A Practical Catch-Up Playbook for Enterprises

Drawing on the reported framework, here is how a late mover can start closing the AI adoption gap. The steps below are our synthesis, not a checklist Garg published.

Set a baseline first. Pick three to five processes and record cycle time, cost and error rate before any AI touches them.

Move the budget. Test your plan against the 10/20/70 split and shift money toward training, workflow design and change management.

Tie incentives to outcomes. Reward managers whose teams retire manual steps, not those who merely adopt tools.

Get visible CEO backing. The 141-versus-3 comparison implies that leadership attention and team motivation, not tooling, drive the spread.

Ship small and often. Many modest deployed solutions teach more than a few polished pilots.

Govern as you go. Build access controls and vendor reviews into each release instead of retrofitting them.

Teams that want a broader view of model options and tooling can browse the site’s AI and machine learning coverage. The point of the playbook is sequencing. Measurement and incentives come before model shopping, because the model is the cheapest, fastest-changing part of the stack.

## Predictions: Five Calls for the Next 12 Months

These are our forecasts, offered as analysis and not as facts from the summit.

Outcome metrics replace usage metrics. By late 2027, expect more boards to ask for operating results tied to AI programs instead of seat counts.

The “people and process” line item becomes standard. Procurement templates will increasingly separate model cost from adoption cost.

Vendors bundle change management. Software firms will pair tools with playbooks and measurement dashboards to win a share of the 70%.

The perception gap narrows slowly. A repeat survey could show the 37% figure rising, though the climb will lag individual adoption.

Governance incidents force a reset. At least a few fast-scaling programs will pause after data-handling failures, reinforcing the case for the process-first approach.

## The Takeaway

The takeaway from Utsav Garg’s session is blunt. Individual productivity is easy to find and company profit is not, and the difference is organizational work. The 80% and 37% figures, the 10/20/70 split and the 141-versus-3 comparison all point in one direction: spend less time choosing models and more time redesigning how people work. Every claim here is bounded by what was reported, and readers should treat the numbers as directional until fuller survey details appear.
