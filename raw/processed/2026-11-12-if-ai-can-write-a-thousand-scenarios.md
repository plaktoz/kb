# If AI Can Write a Thousand Scenarios

## What a new HBR article on strategic decision-making means for foresight

Felipe Csaszar opens his article in the September–October issue of the Harvard Business Review with a question most executives will recognise. Think of your last strategy offsite. After two days of debate, how many genuinely different options did the team leave with? Three, perhaps four. Csaszar, who chairs the strategy area at Michigan's Ross School of Business, argues that this number says little about the world and a lot about the people in the room. Teams consider only as many options as their time and attention allow. Our familiar tools, SWOT or the portfolio matrix, are simple because a planning meeting needed them to be.

In his account, AI removes much of that constraint, and it changes strategic decision-making in three ways. It widens the search, generating and evaluating thousands of options before humans narrow the field. It replaces static pictures of markets and competitors with models that are updated continuously. And it makes challenge routine, by stress-testing plans in structured debates that internal politics cannot bend. Since every company can buy the same models, he expects the lasting advantage to go to those who combine them with their own data, workflows and speed of execution.

I read the article with one obvious thought. If AI can generate a thousand strategic options, it can also generate a thousand scenarios.

## The constraint scenario planning always had

Scenario planning has lived with the same limit Csaszar describes. The classic two-by-two matrix gives you four scenarios because a workshop can hold four in its head, not because the future comes in fours. Teams pick two critical uncertainties out of dozens and leave the rest on the flipchart.

So the temptation is easy to see. Let the machine build hundreds of scenario logics, test every combination of drivers, and present a set no workshop could produce.

I am not convinced this is the gain it appears to be.

## Scenarios were never about quantity

When Pierre Wack described the scenario work at Shell in his two Harvard Business Review articles of 1985, his central point was that scenarios fail unless they change the mental models of the managers who use them. He saw scenario work as a way of reperceiving the business. A set of four that makes a leadership team see its situation differently is worth more than a library of a thousand that confirm what everyone already believed.

Our own research points the same way. In Issue 2 of the Inside Foresight series, I described what 400 senior managers told us: foresight reliably produces more strategic conversations, but those conversations rarely shift decisions. They stay polite and close to the existing strategy. What they lack is cognitive friction, not input.

In The "Trendslop" Problem I reported on an experiment at the Bavarian Foresight-Institute. Teams that used AI in a scenario process converged faster, spent less time in disagreement and put much of their energy into refining prompts. Teams working without AI were quicker to name AI itself as a critical uncertainty.

More scenarios do nothing about either problem, and they may make both worse. A thousand scenarios is a document nobody inhabits.

## Where the appetite exists

Still, I don't want to stop at scepticism. In organisations where leaders already have an appetite for scenario thinking, where they are willing to hold several futures at once and let them question the plan, Csaszar's three shifts become useful for foresight. Each of them addresses a known weakness of scenario practice.

The first is search. AI is good at showing what is already thinkable. Used before a workshop, it can lay out the obvious drivers and the expected futures quickly, so the team spends its limited time on what the machine did not come up with.

The second is representation. Most scenario sets freeze on the day the final deck goes out. Signposts get defined and are then rarely monitored. A continuously updated view of markets and competitors could be tied to the scenarios, showing which of them the world is drifting toward. Practitioners have always found this part of scenario planning the hardest to sustain.

The third is challenge. Csaszar's structured AI debate is close to what business wargaming does, which I talked about on the Innovation Rockstars podcast. Testing every major decision against each scenario as a standing step, rather than once a year in a workshop, is how scenarios find their way into decisions. It is exactly the pathway between foresight conversations and strategic choices that our study found missing.

In this setting AI does not replace the scenario mindset. It gives that mindset more material and more occasions to act.

## AI amplifies what is already there

The condition is the mindset. In an organisation that wants certainty, a thousand scenarios become a thousand chances to find the one closest to the current plan.

In Robust Leadership, Felix von Held and I describe three capacities: imagining, sensing and challenging. AI can feed each of them. It can produce images of the future, scan for signals and argue the other side. It cannot do the reperceiving for a leadership team. That still happens in the room, in the friction between people who see things differently. I made a related point in Prepared Is Not Enough, where I argued that the skill leaders lack is prospective sensemaking, making sense of what has not happened yet. No volume of output substitutes for it.

Csaszar asks how many options you left your last offsite with. For foresight I would ask something else: did anyone leave the room seeing the business differently? If yes, AI can take that a long way. If not, more scenarios will not help.
