# Blood test trends may predict cancer risk before nonspecific symptoms like unexplained weight loss

A study in *PLOS Medicine* examined 275,205 patients with unexplained weight loss, nearly 14,000 of whom received a cancer diagnosis within six months. Researchers found that tracking changes in 26 common blood tests over time — rather than relying on a single reading — may better flag cancer risk, with detectable shifts occurring roughly 3 to 5 years before patients presented with unexplained weight loss.

Many of these changes stayed within normal reference ranges, except for certain markers like hemoglobin and red blood cells in older adults, CRP, ESR, plasma viscosity, and hematocrit/RDW in younger adults. Adjusting results for age and sex improved predictive accuracy, reaching an AUC as high as 0.82.

Specific trends were linked to particular cancers: white blood count and neutrophil trends with lung cancer, mean cell volume with bowel cancer and lymphoma, and several markers with prostate cancer.

Dr. Ketan Thanki, a colorectal surgeon not involved in the study, emphasized interpreting values relative to a patient's own history: "we should look at each patient's own trajectory, not just the patient in a snapshot in time."

He also noted the significance of the findings, stating results showed "a real cancer signal" when weight loss combines with abnormal bloodwork, and cautioned that overapplying this method could increase "false alarms and unnecessary investigations."

Study authors acknowledged limitations, including reliance on patients who already had blood tests on record, and noted that trends measured within the most recent year proved most accurate. They also flagged that testing bias among certain patient groups wasn't accounted for.

Thanki concluded that further clinical validation is needed, saying "external validation and demonstrating a significant improvement clinically will be the biggest hurdles" to real-world use.
