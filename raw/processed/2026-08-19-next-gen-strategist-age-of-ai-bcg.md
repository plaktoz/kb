# The Next-Gen Strategist in the Age of AI

Strategists are trained to decompose problems, build structured frameworks, and develop quantitative models. But AI is rapidly commoditizing the analytical skills that have underpinned the strategy function, making them available to a far wider set of actors across the organization. BCG asked 175 chief strategy officers to identify "untamed issues" where existing approaches fall short, and found four kinds of challenges: navigating rising complexity, reinventing advantage continuously, balancing short-term pressures with long-term value creation, and harnessing AI effectively.

**Systems thinking for complexity.** Geopolitical fragmentation, regulatory volatility, and technological disruption interact to generate nonlinear, self-reinforcing dynamics — contextual factors now account for almost half of variation in company profitability. 80% of strategists surveyed see managing this complexity as core to their work, but many feel underprepared. Systems thinking shifts the question from "how do we optimize this part?" to "how does the whole system behave?" Practical routines: cross-unit strategy forums, KPI/incentive audits for enterprise-wide alignment, and second-order-effects exercises before major decisions.

**Counterfactual thinking for reinvention.** Nearly 94% of respondents said continuously renewing competitive advantage is critical, yet only 12% feel well prepared. Counterfactual thinking asks "what would we build if we started from scratch?" — the question that separated Fujifilm (which reinvented around healthcare, materials science, and cosmetics) from Kodak (which optimized within its existing film model). Routines: scenario-based portfolio reviews, pre-mortems, and surfacing one surprising observation at each quarterly review.

**Integrative thinking to break short-term/long-term trade-offs.** Over three-quarters of strategists said managing this tension is a major part of their job, but only 2% of companies actually manage it well (outperforming on both growth and profitability in good times and bad). Integrative thinking shifts "either/or" to "both/and." Routines: dual-horizon planning/budgeting, forward-looking capability scorecards (e.g., 3M's new-product vitality index), and joint CFO-CSO governance of resource allocation.

**Critical thinking to harness AI.** Nearly six in ten strategists said AI is now central to their role, yet three-quarters said they need to develop their capabilities here. As AI automates more analysis, human judgment must focus on assessing the quality of machine-generated reasoning and detecting shared blind spots across AI tools. Routines: assumption audits before major AI-informed decisions (stress-testing the three hypotheses a model relies on most), red-team rotations that prepare the case against a recommendation, and clear rules for when human judgment overrides algorithmic output (e.g., irreversible or ethically significant decisions).

When multiple modes are triggered by a problem, that signals the decision needs several lenses applied deliberately — the strategy function increasingly lies in recognizing which kind of issue an organization faces, then activating the right mode of thinking.
