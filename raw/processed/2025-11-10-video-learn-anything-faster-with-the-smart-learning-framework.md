# Learn Anything Faster with the S.M.A.R.T. Learning Framework

Whether you want to quickly sound knowledgeable at work, ace an exam, or level up a new skill like pickleball, you can do it surprisingly quickly by focusing on five meta-learning skills from the best learning science books.

## Introduction [00:00:00]

**Narrator:** You've been learning your whole life, but you've probably been doing it half as well as you could. But that can change today. Whether you want to quickly sound knowledgeable on a subject at work, ace a certification exam, or level up your pickle ball game to beat your friends, you can do it surprisingly quick by focusing on five metalearning skills backed by science.

State optimization, mistake optimization, active recall, reduction, and turning off. They form the acronym S.M.A.R.T. I figured if goal setting had a smart acronym, learning needed one, too. So the next time you need to learn something new, use the SMART framework to cut your learning time in half.

## State Optimization [00:00:42]

**Narrator:** Metalearning skill number one, state optimization. In Peak, the definitive book on learning and mastering well-defined skills, Anders Ericsson uses the word intense 18 times to describe how high performers practice. Every world-class performer he observed, from chess champions analyzing complex endgame positions to violinists rehearsing tricky passages, entered a state of intense concentration to maximize learning. To master any material fast, you must ratchet up your ability to intensely focus before your learning sessions.

Most people just eliminate distractions. They go to a quiet room, put on noise-cancelling headphones, and silence their phone. Better learners go a step further and clear mental clutter. As Cal Newport explains in Deep Work, they eliminate attention residue by writing down anything lingering in their head — worries, obligations, unfinished thoughts — and park them to think about later.

But the best learners do something surprising. They do 15 to 30 minutes of moderately intense exercise before learning. Whether it's running, biking, or playing a sport, they elevate their heart rate enough to break a sweat but not exhaust themselves.

Why? Elevating your heart rate increases blood flow to the brain, delivering more oxygen and glucose to fuel an intense study session. Along with increased blood flow comes a neurochemical flood of dopamine and noradrenaline that enhances focus. Add in the production of BDNF, a protein that helps form new connections in the brain, and you're in the optimal learning state.

The book Spark dedicates multiple chapters to this phenomenon. One study found that students who elevated their heart rate by running before class saw significant increases in reading comprehension and grades compared to their peers.

## Mistake Optimization [00:02:31]

**Narrator:** Metalearning skill number two, mistake optimization. Making the most of your learning time means making more mistakes, just not too many. Mistakes are one of the brain's best learning hacks. Every time you slip up, your anterior cingulate cortex sounds an internal alarm, which lights up your memory circuits, making whatever you focus on next more likely to stick.

But there's a limit. Too many mistakes flood the system with stress and shut down learning. Luckily, learning science has revealed a sweet spot: 85/15. Succeed about 85% of the time and fail 15%.

A 15% failure rate keeps your brain challenged, alert, and primed for growth without tipping into "this sucks, I want to quit" mode. Leverage the 85/15 rule by designing practice drills with an 85% success rate. If you're learning to play the guitar, adjust the difficulty of your chord transitions so you're screwing up just three times for every 20 attempts. If you're learning to serve a tennis ball, adjust the target zone until you can land 17 out of 20 serves.

When learning a new subject, tell an AI chatbot, "Give me 10 sentence completion exercises using the following material. If I get more than eight right, make the next set harder. If I get fewer than eight, make it easier." But remember this above all else: when you make a mistake, resist looking at the answer and moving on. Instead, let the sting linger as you understand why you got it wrong. That's how real growth happens.

## Active Recall [00:04:06]

**Narrator:** Metalearning skill number three, active recall. Make It Stick is my favorite book on studying and it boils down to one message: effort equals retention. The more you work to retrieve information without looking at notes, the more likely you'll retain that information.

Struggling to recall information creates strong and smooth neural pathways. Think of it like paving a trail through a forest. Each time you struggle to find your way, you clear the brush and pack the dirt a little firmer. The next time you're having a conversation and need to recall information you've just learned, you can race down that path and access it with ease.

Simply reviewing your notes doesn't give you the same easy recall abilities. Therefore, if you want what you learn to stick, build these three active recall habits.

Habit number one: periodic pause. After 5 to 10 minutes of learning, pause and repeat what you've just learned out loud. For example, when you listen to an audiobook, pause every 10 minutes and pare back the most important points and stories as though you're explaining them to a friend.

Habit number two: end of session recap. At the end of a learning session, explain everything you just learned aloud as if you're teaching someone. Then, write down three to five keywords that capture the main ideas you want to remember. Add them at the top of your notes or in a dedicated recall list so you can easily find them next time.

Habit number three: start of session recall. Before diving into new material, spend 2 minutes explaining what the keywords from your last learning session mean without looking at your notes. Remember, if you find retrieving information from memory difficult, that's a good thing. Effort to retrieve equals retention.

## Reduction [00:05:51]

**Narrator:** Metalearning skill number four, reduction. Reduce what you're learning to one thing. One acronym, one sentence, one page, or one visual.

One acronym: turning what I learned about learning into a SMART acronym took effort, but it's something I'll remember for the rest of my life. One sentence: after reading Atomic Habits, I tried to capture the essence of what I learned in a single sentence — "small improvements compound over time, so focus on building systems rather than chasing goals." The effort to refine that sentence makes me more likely to remember it.

One page: when I studied negotiation for a month, I reduced everything I learned to a single page cheat sheet with six key tactics.

One visual: when learning about space repetition, I understood the concept better after I took out a sheet of paper and drew a series of forgetting curves that got progressively flatter after active recall. The more you can compress and simplify what you learn, the greater you'll understand and retain it. Complexity fades from memory. Simplicity endures.

## Turning Off [00:06:59]

**Narrator:** Metalearning skill number five, turning off. The last but most important skill to accelerate learning is to turn off your conscious mind and let your brain consolidate what you learned as you rest. You can think of studying and practicing as drafting the blueprints for improvement. When you sleep, your brain uses those blueprints to renovate itself.

Sleep is often an afterthought when people strive to master a subject. But in Peak, Anders Ericsson's research shows that masters prioritize sleep, averaging about 1.5 hours more each night than the typical adult. Matthew Walker's research in Why We Sleep found it's not just nighttime sleep that enhances learning — it's also daytime naps.

The difference between a group who napped during the day and another who didn't was a 20% greater ability to recall information at 6 p.m. that day. Sleep in the form of a full night's rest or a midday nap not only cements what you just learned, it also refreshes the hippocampus so you can learn and retain more information in your next learning session.

## Summary and Conclusion [00:08:00]

**Narrator:** So if you want to sound smart and look skilled in record time, focus on the SMART learning framework every study session or practice.

State optimization: elevate your heart rate for 15 to 30 minutes before your learning session to flood your brain with focus-enhancing neurochemicals. Mistake optimization: design practice drills to hit the 85/15 sweet spot. Active recall: periodically retrieve recent lessons from memory without looking at notes.

Reduction: always try to simplify and condense what you learn into an acronym, a sentence, a page, or a visual to make it stick. And then turn off: make sleep a priority because that's when your brain consolidates and cements what you've learned.
