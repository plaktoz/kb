# The forces driving bond yields higher may be here to stay

WASHINGTON (TNND) — A global sell-off in government bonds is driving up borrowing costs for consumers and governments alike and putting new pressures on the U.S. economy as yields hit highs not seen in two decades as markets confront stubborn inflation and longstanding concerns about outsized government debts.

Yields on the 10-year Treasury, which is used as a key benchmark to borrowing costs around the world, touched the highest level since the dotcom bust this week at 5.34%. Bond yields are a reflection of what investors demand to buy and hold government debt move in the opposite direction of bond prices.

The upward pressure is happening beyond the United States, with borrowing costs in Britain, France, Germany and Japan also climbing to levels not seen in decades amid mounting concerns about inflation and government deficits.

Bond markets have been in turmoil for weeks around the world over rising concerns about soaring oil prices, mounting government debt and high inflation. Those factors are unlikely to ease anytime soon, meaning the world may be entering a sustained period of elevated borrowing costs. Government bonds are also competing for capital with a surge in corporate borrowing to support the rapid buildout of artificial intelligence infrastructure.

Elevated bond yields impact consumers by making it more expensive to borrow money. Treasury yields are used as benchmarks for lending across the economy, with higher rates leading to more expensive mortgages, auto loans and other types of borrowing for consumers and businesses. It also increases the costs for governments to finance their debts, with interest costs already surpassing $1 trillion a year in the U.S.

Ongoing budget deficits also put pressure on the bond market by forcing governments to issue more debt to finance government spending, increasing the supply of bonds and forcing yields higher to attract buyers.

Investors have been selling off U.S. bonds again in recent days due to economic reports showing the economy is still growing and inflation is well above target, reinforcing expectations the Fed will raise rates again. Unemployment is also near historical lows around 4% with few layoffs, helping support strong consumer spending that powers the American economy.

"It's an indication that the market still is requiring the Fed to do more action. Until the market feels the Fed is being responsible — meaning addressing the inflation problem — those yields are going to continue to go up," said Mark Williams, a finance lecturer at Boston University's Questrom School of Business and former bank examiner at the Fed.

The Fed raised its benchmark rate in September and is widely expected to do so again with inflation running hot and the economy still growing.

Prices climbed 3.4% in August as measured by the Fed's preferred inflation gauge, the personal consumption expenditures. Core prices, which exclude the volatile food and energy categories, rose 3%, still well above the target.

Conflict in the Middle East has pushed oil prices beyond $100 a barrel with less movement through the Strait of Hormuz. The energy shock is adding another source of inflationary pressure, increasing concerns that it will hard to keep inflation contained and potentially forcing central banks to keep rates higher for longer.

Higher oil prices have fed into costs at the pump, with Americans paying more than $1 more for gasoline compared to before the war and diesel hitting new highs above $6. Higher diesel prices can feed into other parts of the economy with increased costs to fuel the machines that produce and transport goods.

"There are many different measures of inflation, but it's running at around a 3% rate," Neil Kashkari, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of Minnesota, said during a Wednesday event. "It's been elevated now for more than five years. I didn't think the inflation data today really changed that story for me very much."

Other Fed officials have also said they have not yet seen enough evidence inflation is on a clear path back toward 2%.

"I count only two months of data consistent with 2% core PCE inflation over the past 20 months. And I don't yet see a clear trend toward a timely return to 2%," Fed Governor Michael Barr said during a Tuesday speech at the Detroit Economic Club.

The longer treasury yields stay elevated, the more pressure they will put on borrowing costs throughout the economy. That is already evident in the housing market, where mortgage rates have climbed above 7% and weighed heavily on home sales.

The stock market has mostly been able to withstand the myriad of issues facing the economy and rise in bond yields, though there are questions about whether sustained pressure from the bond market will spill into stocks and other assets.

"This is a major shock wave and it's really reassessing other asset classes," Williams said. "It's really a risk-off market right now with the uncertainty, and as we go into the November election too, there'll be a lot of people putting money on the side, just waiting it out."
