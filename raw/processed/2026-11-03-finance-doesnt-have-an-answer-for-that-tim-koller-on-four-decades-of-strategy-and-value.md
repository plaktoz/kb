# “Finance Doesn’t Have an Answer for That”: Tim Koller on Four Decades of Strategy and Value

## Introduction

Early in his career, at the financial consulting firm Stern Stewart, Tim Koller was asked by a client how much cash it should distribute to its shareholders. It sounds like a finance question. Tim realized it wasn’t only that:

“Finance doesn’t have an answer for that unless we know what the company’s strategic opportunities are: how much they think they can grow, and what they need to invest in order to achieve that growth.”

Stern Stewart wasn’t in a position to engage with those questions. Like many financial consulting firms, they would generally take a company’s forecasts and recommend a financial policy without questioning them. “We were only dealing with a portion of the picture.”

I think that moment explains a lot about the rest of Tim’s career. In some ways, it also explains why he, Andy West, and I co-authored Strategy and Value: The Four Foundations That Drive Long-Term Performance with the support of many colleagues, which comes out November 17. Its premise is that there are a few simple, foundational concepts one must understand in order for strategy to create value.

Tim Koller is a partner emeritus at McKinsey and senior adviser to the firm. He founded McKinsey’s Strategy and Corporate Finance Insights team and has co-authored all eight editions of Valuation: Measuring and Managing the Value of Companies, the firm’s best-selling guide to corporate valuation. In more than 40 years of consulting, he has served clients globally on corporate strategy and capital markets, M&A and divestitures, and resource allocation.

Because I suspect you will want to hear from Tim more than from me, more of this post is in his words than in past interview posts. I’ll start with his intellectual arc, then share three themes from our conversation, each with a takeaway for strategy leaders. Our conversation has been edited and condensed for clarity, and Tim has reviewed it.

## The Intellectual Arc of Tim Koller

Tim went to the University of Chicago for his MBA, partly because it had one of the leading economics departments and MBA students could take a lot of economics classes. He wasn’t yet sure whether he wanted to do a PhD. Two ideas from Chicago have stayed with him.

The first was the primacy of operating performance over financial engineering, and the fact that markets see through accounting gimmicks. He is careful to point out that this is not the same as believing markets are perfect:

“A true academic would say that efficient markets capture all available public information. That doesn’t mean they’re perfect, doesn’t mean they’re omniscient. They can’t predict the future.”

The second came from microeconomics:

“Although no one said this explicitly, corporate finance is just applied microeconomics. And true microeconomics is not about perfect markets. What microeconomics is all about is understanding market failures: when they fail, and why, and what happens.”

In my view, that is a great statement for why finance and strategy belong closer than they tend to be. Understanding why some markets let companies earn returns above their cost of capital while others don’t is what market attractiveness and competitive advantage are about. Tim called it “different wording, but closely related to the foundations that we talk about.”

Next came Mobil Oil, where Tim joined a program that rotated him through departments. In the treasurer’s department, he was asked about something like share repurchases. He wrote a short note on why it didn’t fundamentally affect the economics. His boss’s boss rewrote it to focus on the effect on earnings per share. “A little frustrating,” he said.

He also saw how decisions were made at the top. Mobil was acquiring Superior Oil, then one of the biggest deals ever, and Tim was the one who crunched numbers and carried the paperwork for the CFO and the treasurer, which meant sitting in their meetings. When he left, colleagues said Tim would never be happy until he was on the 25th floor, where the senior executives sat. It was, he said, an awakening into how big companies could operate: the politics, the pressure, and doing what the bankers tell you to do.

At Stern Stewart, Tim worked with Joel Stern, one of the pioneers in applying theoretical finance to the real world. That meant working out how you actually calculate free cash flow, how you come up with the weighted average cost of capital, and why return on capital drives cash flows. Al Rappaport at Northwestern was developing similar ideas at roughly the same time. The firm spent much of its time teaching clients about the importance of return on capital, and it had a saying (and pencils to go with it): “EPS doesn’t count, or EPS don’t count, depending upon your grammar.” Tim admired what they did, but it was also where he ran into the question I opened with. In his view, “they didn’t go far enough.”

Then, by chance, McKinsey was starting its corporate finance practice. It was the era of the corporate raiders, early versions of today’s activist shareholders. Around 1988, a group of partners led by Ennius Bergsma, with the support of Fred Gluck before he became managing partner, created the practice. They brought in outsiders, including Tim and UCLA professor Tom Copeland. Two Americans in McKinsey’s European offices, Peter Bisson in Copenhagen and Jack Murrin in London, also moved back to the US to help start the practice.

The early work was outside-in ‘raider’ analysis: what would a corporate raider do to this business, and what can executives learn from that perspective? The first edition of Valuation featured a diversified company analyzed that way, by return on capital, growth potential, and competitive dynamics, to decide what to hold and what to invest in. In one market, the company had 70 or 80 percent share and priced below everyone else, so one recommendation was to raise prices. “Now, I don’t know whether that was the right answer or not, but it seemed logical at the time.”

What was new from a finance perspective, Tim says, was codifying the math. Stern Stewart talked conceptually about how growth and return on capital drive cash flows. The McKinsey team showed mathematically how it worked, in what became the value driver formula: value equals next year’s profits, times one minus growth divided by return on capital, all over the cost of capital minus growth.

The team also wanted every McKinsey consultant doing financial analysis the same way, and no adequate book existed. Practitioner books weren’t grounded in the theory. Academic books told readers to discount cash flow at the cost of capital and left the rest to them. Copeland, an academic, initially favored that approach. “Little did we expect that it would eventually take up most of the book today,” Tim said: how to calculate return on capital and free cash flow, and what’s operating and what isn’t.

So they wrote a handbook in a three-ring binder. Tim still has the original on his bookshelf. Someone suggested showing it to publishers, and Wiley told them a successful professional book would sell 10,000 copies. “We’re up over a million now. Nobody anticipated what was going to happen.”

Over the decades that followed, the work broadened. Tim’s view of market efficiency became more nuanced. Some people assume markets are efficient partly because going short is costless, and the fact that it isn’t is why bubbles happen. “I also realize that you can fool the markets for a while, but eventually, it comes down to ultimately the performance of the company.”

Teams Tim worked with studied why diversification doesn’t help, and showed executives that much of what worried them, such as whether stock options were expensed, didn’t matter, because investors see right through it. The M&A boom of the 1990s produced acquisition and divestiture frameworks and the idea of a natural or better owner, reinforcing that to create value from an acquisition, you have to create more value than the premium you pay. Research into the gap between what executives assumed investors thought and what investors actually said produced the distinction between intrinsic investors and traders. Colleagues in London conducted research showing that companies that reallocate resources more aggressively outperform. Many of these ideas were featured in our recent 25th anniversary McKinsey on Finance issue, which Tim and I worked on together.

Tim moved into the behavioral side of strategy and value, studying cognitive biases with Dan Lovallo, a longtime collaborator of Daniel Kahneman, and into the evidence on short-termism. In one well-known Duke University survey, 80 percent of 400 CFOs said they would cut discretionary spending such as R&D to meet short-term targets.

Throughout, Tim and his co-authors tried to bring the logic behind the numbers into their client work, using whichever version of market attractiveness and competitive advantage was current at the time. “We had the Dynamic SCP framework, there was Porter’s Five Forces. But in the end, to me, they all were variations on the same ideas.” That is why he was excited to write a book that “brings all these things together and distills them to their essence”:

“It’s much easier to apply four ideas than to have very complex frameworks, or multiple frameworks that you apply in different situations.”

The first 125 pages of Strategy and Value, which cover the four foundations, give you “the essential things to know about finance and strategy,” he says. “It’s not going to put you to sleep.” I agree, though I may be biased.

## Three Themes for Strategy Leaders

I was particularly interested in what advice Tim had for strategy leaders. Three themes stood out from our conversation.

## 1. Be honest about competitive advantage

Whitney: When you think about the most strategic leaders you’ve worked with, what do they do well in connecting strategy to value?

Tim: The ideas are straightforward. The differentiator is the depth of insight, and the honesty and openness with which you actually go into them. I came across too many CEOs who give lip service to competitive advantage, or who are in denial that they don’t have one. Great leaders are willing to say: we’re putting lipstick on a pig with some of our businesses, and we need to do something about that.

Whitney: Can you give an example?

Tim: During the dot-com bubble, there was a company called Webvan, which spent billions of dollars to deliver groceries. It built warehouses, bought trucks. And I’m trying to think, OK, so what is the competitive advantage that they’re going to have? They can’t price very high, because people can go to the store. Webvan quickly went bankrupt. It’s uneconomical to compete with grocery stores. And even if you did want a delivery model, it’s much easier to deliver through the existing grocery stores than to create a bunch of warehouses and trucks.

Whitney: Do you think that question is harder to ask when a market is growing fast?

Tim: Just because a market is fast growing doesn’t mean it’s going to create a lot of value. It depends on the competitive intensity of that market and whether you can create a competitive advantage. It goes back to the Warren Buffett point that the best manager in the world can’t fix a bad business. Steve Kaplan at the University of Chicago, who did a lot of work on venture capital, wrote a paper about whether you bet on the horse or the jockey. Same idea, basically: the best jockey in the world is not going to win the Kentucky Derby unless they have a good horse.

## Takeaway for strategy leaders:

In Strategy and Value, we explain that having a strength is not the same as having a competitive advantage, and that executive teams often confuse the two, cutting off important and often uncomfortable debate in the process. A true competitive advantage must be unique, valuable, and durable (a simplified version of the more robust VRIO framework from Professor Jay Barney). That is a hard test to apply honestly to your own business, which may be why 42 percent of executives in one study could not reliably say whether their firm had a competitive advantage at all.

Tim’s Webvan question is a good place to start: what will we have that competitors can’t match, and will customers pay for it? It is worth asking of every business in your portfolio, including the ones performing well. And the horse-and-jockey point is why we treat market attractiveness and competitive advantage as two foundations that have to be assessed together. As I described in my post with Frank Keil, we also try to make that easier. In the book, we introduce sources of market attractiveness that complement the better-known sources of competitive advantage, so teams can debate where to play and how to win in one language.

## 2. Understand why things are going well

Whitney: You’ve said great leaders also understand what’s behind the numbers. What does that look like?

Tim: Twenty-five or thirty years ago, I was talking to the CFO of a leading consumer foods company, and he told me the story of one of their major brands. Every year, the brand met or exceeded its plan, so people didn’t ask too many questions about how it was making its operating profits. Then all of a sudden, it didn’t. When they went back and looked, they discovered that the brand had been succeeding by raising prices. Its market share was eroding, and it was creating a price umbrella for new entrants in snack foods. That they should have known about.

The lesson I took from that, and have believed ever since, is that if I’m a CEO or on a board of directors, I probably need to spend as much time, or more, understanding why things are going well as why things are not. If things are not going well, you’d better believe management is on top of it, trying to figure out what’s going on and how to fix it. The danger is when things are going well, and everybody’s complacent and doesn’t understand what’s driving that good performance and whether it’s sustainable. Often, it is not.

Whitney: What is your simple test for this?

Tim: If you are continually relying on margin improvement instead of revenue growth to achieve your profit growth, that is a red flag. Achieving your profit number without achieving your revenue number is something you need to investigate and understand.

## Takeaway for strategy leaders:

In my last post, on strategic initiative postmortems, I argued that successes deserve the same scrutiny as failures. Tim’s story is a good example of why. It also connects to my conversation with Frank Keil about causal understanding, which may be easiest to lose when you’re successful.

Our Strategy Champions research points the same way. Under lower uncertainty, one of the biggest risk signals that a company will fall to the bottom of the power curve within five years is weakness in differentiating capabilities and competitive advantage.

## 3. There’s nothing magic here

Whitney: You’ve run a lot of trainings, with clients, internally, and at business schools. What have you seen change when leaders get grounded in the principles of value?

Tim: That’s one of the most rewarding things. With clients, the most rewarding thing is when you see them two years later and they talk about a meeting you had. It may not have been a formal training. Maybe it was a progress review where you introduced these concepts. And they talk about how it has changed their dialogue internally and what they talk about. It doesn’t always happen. But the most rewarding thing for me is when it becomes embedded in them, part of their language and the way they talk about business and strategy and value creation.

Whitney: What advice do you give young people?

Tim: On the substance, it goes back to these foundations. There’s nothing magic here. Always go back to the fundamentals. I would pound that into people when I was working with teams, by asking questions around the fundamentals. The only addition I’d make is to look at second- and third-order effects. If you do this, what will your competition do? How will the customer react? People often don’t do that naturally. I try to use more of a Socratic approach, asking teams questions to get them to think about these things.

Whitney: What should strategy executives understand about finance that they usually don’t?

Tim: Two things. First, strategy executives often focus only on growth, and not on whether a strategy will lead to attractive returns on capital. That depends, in part, on what sources of advantage may matter in a given market, and why, and whether the company has or can feasibly build true competitive advantage. Second, strategy executives should work more closely with the finance team to connect how the impacts of market attractiveness and competitive advantage show up in the financial projections.

## Takeaway for strategy leaders:

Tim’s measure of success, when the ideas become part of how a team talks, is close to what I have called strategic culture: a shared way of thinking about how the organization creates value. His Socratic approach is one way to build it. Several of the five simple rules for debating strategy in the book work the same way, starting with framing challenges before answers, and the last rule is to focus on the foundations.

One way to test it: listen to your next leadership meeting. Does anyone ask why a market is attractive, why you win in it, and what drives return on invested capital and growth? Does anyone ask how competitors and customers will respond before a decision is made? If not, start asking those questions yourself.

## The Craft Behind the Book

In closing, Tim reflected that the way he, Andy, and I worked on the book, in partnership with our (terrific, unrelenting, rarely satisfied) developmental editor, Allan Gold, and in collaboration with colleagues across McKinsey, was the kind of in-the-trenches intellectual work behind many of McKinsey’s best and most durable contributions.

For months on end, we debated drafts and perspectives daily. We tried to live the principles of the book as we wrote it: getting down to the why of each idea so that we could simplify it, and making sure, to the greatest extent possible, that we got both the facts and the ideas right.

We also felt a responsibility to the two fields we were bringing together. Strategy and corporate finance each have deep traditions, inside and outside McKinsey, and our aim was to do justice to both for leaders, without obscuring or taking credit for the important work of others. As we write in the acknowledgments, though the book carries our names, it belongs to the broader community of colleagues whose work it draws on, and the further reading at the end points to the intellectual tradition behind and beyond it.

## A Note on an Artwork

Betty Woodman spent nearly seven decades working in clay, much of it in Boulder, not far from where Tim lives. She taught at the University of Colorado for two decades, and in 2006 the Metropolitan Museum of Art gave her its first retrospective for a living female ceramic artist. Some of her most lasting work in Boulder was about access. In the 1950s she launched the country’s first city-sponsored pottery program. It began as one class of seven students, one night a week, and grew to train hundreds of people, putting expensive wheels and kilns within their reach. In a similar spirit, as I wrote in my preview of the book, we wrote Strategy and Value to be the one book you could hand to everyone in an organization to help them make better strategic and financial decisions, rather than only its executives.

Joined Vases comes from a moment when Woodman pared her work back to its essence. In 1972 she began a series of plain white vases, and by the mid-1970s she was painting her pots herself, bringing the potter’s craft and the painter’s art into one pair of hands. That is, in part, the argument of this post. As Tim discovered early in his career, finance on its own doesn’t have an answer. It needs a grasp of strategy, just as strategy ultimately benefits from a grasp of finance. The New York Times described Joined Vases as her homage to the wet-clay stage of making, when the work is purely about form. Four white porcelain pots are pressed together, their lips sealed to one another with extra clay. Each keeps its own slightly irregular shape, yet together they make a single object.

Like those four pots, the four foundations in Strategy and Value are distinct ideas joined so that none stands alone, and their simplicity belies their depth. “I don’t think anything I make is simple,” Woodman once said; the curator of her Met retrospective called her work “easy to look at, but there are layers of information.” Rothko’s color fields, which I wrote about in an earlier post on why strategy is about choices, work the same way: dozens of translucent layers of paint sit beneath an apparent simplicity, just as the best strategies hold layers of insight. Distilling ideas to their essence without diluting them was Tim’s constant refrain through draft after draft of the book. The foundations are simple to state but serious to apply. Just as Woodman moved beyond production pottery while still embracing it as her subject, we hope readers will build beliefs on them and make them their own source of judgment.

Woodman is often described as bridging the gap between art and craft. I often describe strategy as a craft. Valuation, for all its grounding in math, is in many ways an art, because its numbers rest on judgments about the future. In Woodman’s case, what connects the two is what her gallery calls her “embodied readings” of a diversity of ancient and modern art historical traditions. She absorbed those traditions by working inside them, and she wanted her work read alongside our knowledge of the art it drew on. The best thinking on strategy and value works the same way: absorbed through practice, credited openly, and made one’s own. However far she traveled, she kept the purpose in view. “My work is about function, there’s an implied function,” she told an interviewer in 1990. In strategy, that function is creating value.
