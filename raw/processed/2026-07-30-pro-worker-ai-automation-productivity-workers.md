# Automation Led to Economic Misery. AI Doesn't Have To.

source_url: https://www.theatlantic.com/ideas/2026/07/ai-automation-productivity-workers/688083

byline: Daron Acemoglu

date: 2026-07-30

---

Over the past 40 years, digital technology automation amplified inequality even as it increased productivity — enriching some groups while punishing many others, and exacerbating the crisis of liberal democracy upending much of the developed world. Generative AI is poised to supercharge those inequities, but Daron Acemoglu (MIT professor and co-recipient of the 2024 Nobel Prize in economics) argues this isn't inevitable if AI development is redirected to benefit workers instead of replacing them — what he calls "pro-worker AI": models that expand workers' capabilities, give them new tasks, and make them more productive.

Generative AI has finally fulfilled a 1960 prediction by computer scientist J.C.R. Licklider that computers would augment human cognition with highly processed, context-sensitive information. AI can now find the most relevant answers to a query and summarize them instantaneously, forming the basis of pro-worker AI that could increase wages, employment, and productivity simultaneously — restoring the link between productivity growth and good jobs that the past four decades severed.

Pro-worker AI could serve far more than knowledge workers. Electricians could use specialized models trained on niche, high-quality data — sensor readings, photographs, site reports — to diagnose equipment problems. In education, models trained on curricular material could help teachers spot patterns in test results and tailor lesson plans, potentially driving up demand for teachers and their wages. In health care, specially trained models could let lower-skilled workers perform a broader range of tasks reliant on physical exertion and social interaction rather than medical expertise — creating good service jobs for workers without college degrees and helping uproot inequality entrenched in the postindustrial economy.

Realizing these promises requires a shift in how governments, corporations, investors, and workers think about AI:

- Most Silicon Valley funding chases automation because it's lucrative for managers and shareholders — it reduces payroll and dependence on organized labor — while "reaching human parity" has become the key metric of hype and success.
- The global race for artificial general intelligence centers on replacing human labor across sectors entirely, threatening even skilled blue-collar and craft work.
- The U.S. and Europe should create AI agencies offering grants and public competitions for technologists building models designed to expand worker capability.
- Tax codes should be fixed: labor income is taxed far more than capital income in the U.S. (up to 30% tax/spending burden on $100 of wages versus under 5% on $100 of automation equipment), an asymmetry that can make automating profitable even when it's less productive than human labor.
- Society needs sustained investment in adaptive worker training that boosts existing expertise rather than trying to turn, say, coal miners into computer programmers.
- Reducing the dominance of technology monopolies — via antitrust enforcement and better-funded independent media — would open more space for pro-worker AI to develop.
- Labor unions should advocate for pro-worker AI rather than simply resist automation, since bargaining for higher wages alone often just pushes companies toward more automation.

At stake is a core tenet of the liberal economic order: that jobs and wage growth remain available to workers of different skills and backgrounds. The past 40 years jeopardized that promise, but AI could also help workers rather than sideline them — if automation isn't allowed to remain the default north star of the AI age.
