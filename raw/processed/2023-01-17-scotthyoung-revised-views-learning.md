# How My Views on Learning Have Changed Over Time

After nearly seventeen years writing about learning, Scott Young traces how his views evolved through three phases.

**Early views (Holistic Learning, Learn More Study Less):** As a college student he argued students should avoid rote memorization and instead understand concepts through analogies and connections. He now sees these ideas as flawed: memorizing is often a necessary step to understanding complex ideas; analogies suffer from a "bootstrapping problem" since you often can't spot a good analogy until you already understand a concept's deep structure; and while declarative memory is associative, not every connection is equally useful — practice questions and worked examples build better connections than free-association. His worst early advice, in retrospect, was discouraging repetitive practice in favor of associative memory; he now believes practice questions should be the cornerstone of studying, with mnemonics as a supplement.

**Maturing thoughts (Ultralearning era):** A decade of self-directed learning projects (the MIT Challenge, language immersion, portrait drawing) emphasized practice, practice, practice, culminating in the 2019 book Ultralearning (chapters on Directness, Drill, and Retrieval). In retrospect, he underweighted examples and explanations relative to practice, and over-generalized from deliberate-practice research that harder learning is always more efficient — some difficulty is wasteful rather than productive, and a lack of good examples is usually more of a bottleneck than an overreliance on easy practice.

**Recent adjustments (Direct Instruction and cognitive science):** After deeper research into cognitive and educational psychology, Young became more supportive of Direct Instruction — breaking complex skills into simple concepts taught with ample examples and practice, valuable precisely because it doesn't require exceptional cleverness to work. He now distinguishes moderate difficulty (helpful for retrieval-based learning) from unnecessary difficulty (wasteful, especially for high-cognitive-load skills like math or grammar), and favors a "ramp" approach: simplified problems with heavy feedback first, then more realistic, ambiguous applications once the foundation is secure.

He notes his views have changed less dramatically over time (Ultralearning would only need footnotes to update, versus a full rewrite for his earliest work), and defends his ongoing value as focusing on the learner's perspective rather than the teacher's, which most serious educational research centers on.
