# How Exercise May Improve Mental Health by Reducing Stress and Negative Thinking

New research suggests exercise may improve symptoms across several mental health conditions by reducing perceived stress and repetitive negative thinking. A study published in Psychological Medicine analyzed data from the ImPuls trial, involving 400 physically inactive people experiencing major depressive disorder, agoraphobia, panic disorder, PTSD or insomnia.

Participants were randomly assigned either standard treatment alongside an exercise programme, or standard treatment alone. The exercise programme involved supervised group sessions and 30 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous aerobic exercise two to three times a week for four weeks, after which participants continued independently for five months.

After six and 12 months, participants in the exercise programme reported significantly lower perceived stress and repetitive negative thinking than the control group, though the programme did not significantly improve sleep quality. Reductions in perceived stress and repetitive negative thinking fully explained the relationship between the exercise programme and improvements in overall symptom severity.

"Our results suggest that changes in perceived stress and repetitive negative thinking may be key transdiagnostic mechanisms underlying the treatment effect of exercise on global symptom severity," the study authors concluded. Researchers suggest exercise may help the body better manage stress by safely exposing it to physical stress responses, and that physical activity can redirect attention away from distressing thoughts, interrupting cycles of rumination. Limitations include reliance on self-reported measures and possible expectancy effects.
