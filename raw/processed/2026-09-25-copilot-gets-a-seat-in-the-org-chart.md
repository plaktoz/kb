# Copilot Gets a Seat in the Org Chart

Microsoft announced a unified Copilot app combining chat, code, Office tools, and a persistent autonomous agent called Autopilot. The launch event in Redmond marked a consolidation of what Mike Taylor previously counted as 80 separate "Copilot" products.

## The New App Structure

Three tabs define the experience:

- **Home** — integrates chat, delegated tasks, and Office apps with shared context
- **Code** — enables non-developers to build and share apps in secure environments
- **Autopilot** — a persistent background agent assigned goals and given its own org-chart identity

## What Works (and What Didn't)

The Office integration showed genuine improvement — opening a PowerPoint inside Copilot and asking it to reformat a specific slide worked smoothly. However, when Sloan requested a cross-app task (moving a table from Word into Excel), Copilot reported insufficient permissions. Handoffs between Chat and Code were also blocked in the demo environment.

This mirrors earlier observations: enterprise IT controls keep the system safe but may prevent day-one functionality for individual users.

## Autopilot in Practice

Autopilot draws on 30 days of work history, calendar data, and org-chart position. It maintains private user preferences while remaining identifiable to colleagues — appearing in Teams under a user-assigned name like "Dot (Ryan's Autopilot)."

EVP Charles Lamanna has given his own Autopilot, named Apollo, the same Outlook access as his human executive assistant. The agent runs continuously but operates on usage-based credits and is off by default.

## The Evals Emphasis

Microsoft devoted notable attention to how customers should measure Copilot's value. Nadella called for "evals specific to your company" — tests built around actual workflows rather than generic benchmarks. Lamanna noted Microsoft runs roughly 16,000 internal evals, each built by domain experts from real usage data.

The advised customer approach: have the person who does the job daily build the eval, establish a human-performance baseline first, and treat failing tests as diagnostic rather than discouraging.

## Bottom Line

Microsoft holds a structural advantage — it already houses employees' documents, conversations, and permissions. The challenge is whether enterprise governance controls, which make Copilot trustworthy, will also prevent it from completing routine tasks. Company-specific evals are positioned as the mechanism for answering that question.
