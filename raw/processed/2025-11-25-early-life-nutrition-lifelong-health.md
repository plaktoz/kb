# Early-life nutrition: Exposures in the womb can affect lifelong health

Nutrition received from conception through early childhood likely helps lay the foundation for lifelong health. Because long-term nutritional experiments on children aren't ethical, scientists rely on natural experiments — historical events that abruptly changed diets for one group but not another — to study these effects.

An NIH-funded study led by Dr. Tadeja Gracner used UK food rationing during and after World War II as a natural experiment. Sugar rations closely matched today's U.S. dietary guidelines, and consumption nearly doubled once rationing ended in September 1953. Using UK Biobank data on roughly half a million people, the researchers found that those with the longest exposure to sugar rationing in early development had about 35% lower diabetes risk and 20% lower hypertension risk decades later than people never exposed to rationing, and were diagnosed with those conditions several years later on average.

A separate NIH study tracking diets of more than 300 pregnant people found that "empty calorie" foods (sugar, refined grains, saturated fat) eaten during pregnancy were linked to babies being larger at birth and gaining excess weight faster afterward — but even small reductions in empty-calorie intake produced a large drop in that risk, both during pregnancy and while breastfeeding.

Research on famine survivors reinforces the pattern in the opposite direction. Studies of the Dutch Hunger Winter (1944–45) found that people exposed to famine while still in the womb showed faster biological aging (measured via epigenetic clocks) decades later, with more weeks of exposure correlating with a faster aging pace, plus a higher risk of adult obesity. Similarly, people exposed in utero to the Holodomor famine had more than double the risk of developing type 2 diabetes later in life, and people exposed in the womb to the worst economic hardship of the Great Depression showed faster epigenetic aging and markers suggesting higher risk of earlier death — effects that held only for in-utero exposure, not for exposure during early childhood.

Researchers emphasize that early-life exposures influence health trajectories but don't fix them: dietary changes at any life stage produce measurable health improvements, and programs supporting nutrition for pregnant people and young children can have effects that last a lifetime.
