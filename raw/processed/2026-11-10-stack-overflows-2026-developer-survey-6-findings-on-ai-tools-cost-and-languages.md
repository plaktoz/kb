# Stack Overflow's 2026 Developer Survey: 6 findings on AI tools, cost and languages

Stack Overflow's 2026 survey shows 73% of AI coding tool users go daily, while only 20% use AI on production systems. Six findings.

Asking a machine to write, fix or explain code has become ordinary work across the software industry rather than a trial run. Developers and their employers now pay for that routine on such a scale that the coding-tool startup Cursor grew its annualized revenue from $4 million to $2 billion in 18 months, according to CNBC. Spending of that size comes from people who depend on the tools for daily work, not from people trying them out of curiosity. The open question is no longer whether developers use AI. It is how far they will let it go, and on what terms.

The limit on AI coding is increasingly the bill, not the technology. Heavy use has broken the math on both sides of the sale. CNBC reports that Jared Spataro, Microsoft $MSFT's marketing chief for AI at work, says the company realized it could no longer sell its strongest AI results for a flat monthly fee per user. Buyers have felt the cost as well, and Uber $UBER used up its annual AI budget after four months, according to remarks by its chief technology officer reported by CNBC. The seller had to rethink its pricing and the buyer ran out of money, so neither side of the deal escaped the cost. The tools developers favor, and even admire, now also depend on what someone is willing to pay for them.

Stack Overflow set out in its 2026 Developer Survey to highlight developers' AI habits, tools and technology choices, and more than 30,000 people responded. With a pool that large, the results reflect the habits of working developers broadly rather than a small group of enthusiasts. The findings show how widely developers rely on agents such as Claude Code, how Anthropic and OpenAI compare in developers' affection, and why SQL, a database language that has been in use for decades, keeps gaining ground. Ryan Donovan, a member of Stack Overflow's staff, writes that AI's spread through the software industry has left some developers frustrated. Taken together, the findings show a profession that has embraced AI while setting its own limits on where it trusts the tools and what it will spend on them.

## Daily AI use and sentiment

For most developers who have taken up AI coding assistants or agents, the tools are now part of every working day, with 73% using them daily, according to Stack Overflow. The people who use AI most also tend to like it most, and that pattern holds well beyond software. Among developers who use AI daily, 71% feel at least somewhat positive about it, and Gallup reports that 69% of Gen Z Americans who use AI daily feel curious about it. Heavy users in two very different groups lean warm, which suggests that people who use AI every day feel better about it whether or not they write code. Across the whole profession the mood is positive but cooler, with 62% of all developers surveyed feeling at least somewhat positive about AI. Some of the daily users' enthusiasm likely reflects who chose to adopt the tools in the first place. Their mood marks the high end of developers' feeling, not its typical level.

Liking a tool you use every day is different from trusting it with everything. Young Americans who use AI daily feel as anxious and angry about it as they did a year earlier, at levels statistically similar to 2025, according to Gallup. These are Gen Z Americans broadly, not developers, and they can't stand in for coders' mood. They do show that daily use and curiosity can exist alongside worries that have not eased. Developers' daily use and positive mood alone can't tell anyone whether their reservations are shrinking.

The habit is settled, and the open question is where developers who rely on AI every day still draw the line.

## Where developers work with AI

Developers who use AI now hand it the work itself as well as their questions, and most of them still keep a chat window open. Stack Overflow finds that 66% of developers who use AI work in coding agents and 63% work in chatbots. With both groups that large, many AI users must work in both, and the agent becomes an added workspace rather than a replacement for the chat window. Anthropic's Claude Code is the agent developers name most often, at 66%, and Microsoft $MSFT's GitHub Copilot follows at 59%. Those two groups overlap heavily, so many developers use the two agents side by side, and Anthropic's lead stops well short of a takeover. A Microsoft slide about Copilot displays the logos of Anthropic, OpenAI and SpaceX next to Microsoft AI, according to CNBC. Microsoft promotes Copilot as a way to work with other companies' AI as well as its own. For developers, choosing AI tools now means assembling a set of them rather than picking a single winner.

Copilot trails Claude Code even though it came out years earlier. Microsoft released GitHub Copilot in 2021, CNBC reports, and developers later moved to newer tools such as Cursor and Claude Code. An early start did not stop developers from trying what came next.

GitHub Copilot is now adding users quickly, growing from 26 million users in October 2025 to 50 million by July 2026, according to CNBC. Claude Code passes the test of being the agent developers name most often in the survey. Whether Anthropic keeps that position remains open while Copilot grows this fast.

In coding agents, an early start gave Microsoft nothing permanent, and neither company holds a safe lead.

## Model providers

Developers mostly pick their AI models from the same two American labs. Anthropic and OpenAI lead the model providers developers use, with Google $GOOGL in third, according to Stack Overflow. Developers use the two leaders at similar rates, and the difference between them shows up only in which models developers want to keep using. More developers want to stay with Anthropic's models than with OpenAI's, by a margin that Stack Overflow staffer Ryan Donovan describes as small. A lead that slim reflects a preference. It does not give Anthropic a firm hold on developers' work, and it leaves room for companies to send that work elsewhere for other reasons.

U.S. companies have moved a fast-growing share of their AI work to Chinese models. On OpenRouter, Chinese models handled just 4.5% of the tokens U.S. companies processed in the first half of 2025, but their weekly share has topped 30% ever since Feb. 8, 2026, according to CNBC. The main reason for the shift is cost. Chinese open-source models sell for far less than the leading models from Anthropic and OpenAI. "Price is doing the work here," Harpreet Arora, head of agentic infrastructure at Vercel, said, as reported by CNBC. A company that weighs price this heavily has good reason to give routine jobs to cheaper models, even while its developers favor Anthropic and OpenAI.

The cheaper option carries its own risk for the companies that rely on it. CNBC reports that Yacine Jernite, head of machine learning at Hugging Face, sees a real risk that users could be left with two options: strong U.S. models whose cost and access may shift quickly, or Chinese models as the one affordable route to cutting costs or owning their AI systems. For developers, the model they like best and the model their company pays for are becoming two different things.

Developers lean toward Anthropic and OpenAI, but companies now weigh price when they decide how much work to give those two labs.

## What AI is used for

Developers give AI the parts of their work where they can still judge the result. The most common use is generating code in areas a developer already knows, which 67% of developers do, according to Stack Overflow. In those areas, a developer knows what good code looks like and can reject a bad suggestion before accepting it. Debugging follows closely at 61%, and it is also work on code the developer is already examining. The chance to check the AI's work in both jobs may help explain why so many developers are comfortable letting it take part.

Developers mostly stop using AI once the code is live. Only 20% use it to deploy, operate and troubleshoot production systems. In that work, a mistake affects a running service rather than a draft on a developer's screen. Most developers appear to draw their line of trust at that point. They accept AI's suggestions while they write and fix code, but most keep it away from the systems their companies depend on every day.

Companies can sort their AI the same way their developers do, one job at a time, and heavy use for one task does not mean the same use for the next. "For different use cases we use different things," MongoDB $MDB CEO CJ Desai said, as reported by CNBC. At his company, as at the desk, people decide task by task where AI belongs.

AI has become standard for writing and fixing code in familiar territory. Whether developers will trust it to run live systems is still an open question.

## Language rankings

AI changed how developers write code this year, but it barely changed the languages they write it in. The clearest shift among the most-used technologies involves SQL. Developers now put SQL, the language for pulling and organizing data stored in databases, second among the technologies they use most, ahead of HTML/CSS, according to Stack Overflow. HTML and CSS are the markup and styling languages that build web pages. More developers now say they work with data in databases than with the pages people see in a browser. SQL reached second place by passing another language developers use heavily, so the change is a reshuffle among familiar tools rather than the arrival of something new.

Apart from SQL's move, the core of developers' toolkit held steady. The rest of the top 10 most-used technologies looks mostly as it did before, even in a year when developers changed the assistants, agents and models they work with. That stability matters for anyone weighing how far AI has changed the profession. A developer who switched agents this year still works mostly in the same languages as before. The tools that help write code changed faster than the languages developers depend on.

AI added new tools to developers' work without replacing the languages underneath, and the most visible change near the top was SQL passing HTML/CSS.

## Quality, cost and spending controls

The developers who write code with AI and the companies that pay for it have started to want different things. Developers still want the best output, and 75% put quality first when they choose prompts and models, according to Stack Overflow. Their employers are watching the bill instead, and 38% of developers say their organizations set usage limits. Ramp co-CEO Eric Glyman says that most finance chiefs did not plan for the steep growth in AI spending and lack good tools to manage it. A usage cap is a blunt answer to a bill finance chiefs did not budget for, and it means the model a developer would pick and the model an employer allows are starting to differ.

Some companies have taken a third path between capping AI and paying top prices for every task. Their approach is to match the model to the job, and 27% of developers report that their employers assign separate models to separate kinds of work. Google $GOOGL CEO Sundar Pichai says Gemini 3.5 Flash costs half, and sometimes about one-third, of what comparable frontier models cost. A team working under a cap has a real reason to use the cheaper model for routine work and keep the best model for the jobs that truly need it.

The AI makers now sell spending limits themselves, and that is the clearest sign the limits will last. CNBC reports that OpenAI launched enterprise spend controls in June and that Anthropic had rolled out its own spending-limit controls the previous August. Caps have become a standard product feature rather than a workaround customers build on their own. D.A. Davidson equity analyst Gil Luria expects that some of the AI makers' largest corporate clients may begin cutting back on token spending. The customers who matter most to the sellers are the ones who may push hardest to spend less.

AI makers now compete on helping customers control the bill as well as on raw capability, and developers increasingly get the AI their employer's budget allows.
