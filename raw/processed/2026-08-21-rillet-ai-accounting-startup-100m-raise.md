# How AI accounting startup Rillet raised $100M and became a unicorn in 48 hours

Rillet co-founder and CEO Nicolas Kopp raised $100 million at a $1 billion valuation in 48 hours, without actively fundraising, driven by a U.S. accountant shortage fueling growth of his AI-native accounting platform.

Rillet emerged from stealth two years ago and has raised $200 million total from investors including Iconiq, Andreessen Horowitz, and Sequoia. It has 600 customers, most looking to ditch legacy accounting systems like Oracle and NetSuite.

A few weeks ago, at a board meeting, Rillet shared growth since its $70 million Series B last summer: annualized revenue rate doubled in the last quarter alone, it added new public-company clients, and formed an alliance with EY to introduce AI tools to the auditing giant. After that meeting, informal outreach among investors led to a unicorn round closing within 48 hours.

Iconiq general partner Seth Pierrepont, who led the round and joins the board, said the deal came together fast but "wasn't a cold start" — a year of watching Rillet win against incumbents made doubling down easy. Sequoia's Julien Bek called re-investing an "easy decision," noting Rillet's initial wedge is accounting but it's ultimately reinventing the entire finance function, calling agentic finance "one of the largest application software opportunities of the AI era."

Rillet was built for AI agents, not humans, letting humans work alongside agents on bookkeeping. Customers range from laundromats to a major sports franchise: about 50% come from Intuit, 30% from NetSuite/Sage Intacct, and 20% from Oracle, SAP, Workday, or Microsoft products.

Security features include model routing (customers pick their foundational model, e.g., OpenAI or Anthropic), a harness that prevents models from training on customer data, and no cross-training between customers' data. Agents also have memory to store historical actions for their own process improvement. About three months ago, Rillet released a governance feature letting accountants audit every AI agent decision, including what numbers were pulled and how they were calculated — built only recently because agents got powerful enough to support multi-step workflows over longer periods.

Regulations currently require every AI-agent transaction at public companies to be approved by a human. Kopp doesn't expect mass job displacement in accounting — a recent Stanford report found no widespread AI job displacement yet — and points to a structural accountant shortage: accounting degree completions have declined since 2010, and 61% of finance leaders report difficulty finding talent. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects accounting-related roles will grow at least 5% (72,800 jobs) by 2034, with AI making accountants' advisory and analytical duties more prominent rather than reducing demand.
