# Observational study finds lower exercise frequency linked to increased anxiety and depression risk — Evidence Review

Published in PLOS One, by researchers from Western Norway University of Applied Sciences

A new study of over 10,000 Norwegian college students finds that exercising more frequently is linked to lower risk of anxiety and depression one year later. These results, published in PLOS One, are consistent with a large body of research showing regular physical activity supports mental health.

Multiple systematic reviews and meta-analyses indicate that frequent, moderate exercise is associated with reduced risk and symptoms of depression and anxiety, with benefits seen across different age groups and populations.

While some studies suggest exercise intensity or session duration can influence mental health outcomes, the new study puts particular emphasis on frequency, aligning with cohort data showing even low-intensity, regular activity may protect against depression.

Recent reviews highlight potential mediators—such as improved self-esteem, social support, and neuroplasticity—that may explain why routine physical activity benefits mental health, underscoring the importance of consistency over intensity alone.

## Study Overview and Key Findings

Mental health challenges are a leading concern among young adults, especially during the transition to college life—a period marked by new academic and social pressures. While previous research has linked exercise to psychological well-being, less is known about how specific exercise habits, such as frequency and intensity, prospectively affect the risk of developing anxiety or depression in students. This new Norwegian study addresses this gap by tracking the mental health and exercise patterns of over 10,000 college students over one year.

The study assessed students' exercise frequency, intensity, and total activity at baseline, and then evaluated their mental health using standardized diagnostic tools one year later. The main findings include:

- Students who exercised less frequently were more likely to meet criteria for anxiety or depression after one year.
- Exercise frequency was a more consistent predictor of mental health outcomes than intensity or duration.
- Gender differences emerged: for women, lower intensity was also linked to higher risk, while for men, fewer total hours of activity was more closely associated with anxiety and depression.
- The protective association plateaued at around 10 hours of physical activity per week.
- The study supports World Health Organization guidelines recommending at least 150 minutes of moderate-to-vigorous activity weekly.

The authors note that, as an observational study, causality cannot be established, but the results suggest promoting regular exercise may be beneficial for student mental health.

## How does exercise frequency and consistency influence risk of depression and anxiety?

Several large-scale cohort and systematic review studies support the finding that regular, consistent physical activity reduces the risk of depression and anxiety. The Norwegian study's emphasis on frequency aligns closely with evidence that even modest, habitual exercise can have a protective effect, regardless of exercise intensity.

- The HUNT cohort study found that just one hour of leisure-time exercise per week could prevent up to 12% of new depression cases, regardless of intensity, emphasizing the value of consistency.
- Meta-analyses and systematic reviews confirm frequent exercise is associated with lower odds of both depression and anxiety in the general population and in students.
- During periods of heightened stress, such as the COVID-19 pandemic or academic terms, maintaining regular exercise routines is linked with reduced mental health symptoms.

Key points:

- Regular exercise, even at low frequency, reduces risk for depression and anxiety.
- Consistency matters more than intensity for long-term mental health protection.
- Benefits are observed across age groups and in times of increased stress.

The new study's focus on frequency rather than intensity reinforces these prior findings.

## What roles do exercise intensity and duration play in mental health outcomes?

While exercise frequency stands out, related research shows that intensity and duration also have roles—though sometimes with nuanced effects. Moderate intensity is often found to be effective, and benefits may plateau beyond moderate durations or intensities.

- Physical activity interventions of moderate intensity are consistently effective for improving depression and anxiety symptoms; higher intensities may further enhance benefits, but can also increase perceived stress in some individuals.
- Short sessions (10–40 minutes), performed 3–5 times per week, yield significant improvements in mental health, with diminishing returns beyond this frequency and duration.
- Some evidence suggests that, for women, lower intensity may be less protective—a finding echoed by the Norwegian study's gender-specific results.

Key points:

- Moderate-intensity exercise is generally optimal for mental health outcomes.
- Short, regular sessions are effective; longer or more intense sessions may not always add benefit.
- Individual responses to intensity and duration can vary by gender and stress context.

The new study adds to this by showing frequency may be a stronger predictor than intensity.

## What mechanisms mediate the mental health benefits of physical activity?

Understanding why exercise benefits mental health is an active area of investigation. Multiple mediators have been identified, including psychological and physiological processes.

- Improvements in affect, self-esteem, resilience, and social support are strong mediators of the relationship between physical activity and mental health outcomes.
- Neuroplasticity and reduced inflammatory markers (such as TNF-α) may also contribute to improved mood and reduced depressive symptoms.
- The domain of physical activity (leisure vs. work vs. transport) affects its impact, with leisure-time activity most consistently linked to positive mental health outcomes.

Key points:

- Psychological and social mediators (self-esteem, social connection) play key roles.
- Biological factors such as neuroplasticity and inflammation reduction are implicated.
- Type and context of physical activity matter: leisure-time activity is most beneficial.

The Norwegian study's observational design emphasizes the need for mechanistic research.

## Are the mental health benefits of physical activity consistent across populations and contexts?

The evidence indicates that exercise benefits are generalizable, but context can influence the magnitude of effects.

- Reviews and meta-analyses report positive effects of exercise for depression and anxiety across age groups, genders, health statuses, and in various cultural settings.
- Youth and college students, in particular, can benefit from regular exercise, though more robust trials in these populations are needed.
- Stable exercise routines are especially protective during stressful periods, such as pandemics or academic challenges.

Key points:

- Physical activity's mental health benefits are robust across demographic groups.
- Young people and students show similar patterns, but high-quality, long-term studies are limited.
- Exercise may buffer against stress-related mental health declines in challenging contexts.

The current study expands the evidence base for college students, a group at elevated risk.
