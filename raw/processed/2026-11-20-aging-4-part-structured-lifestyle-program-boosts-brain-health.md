# Aging: 4-part structured lifestyle program boosts brain health

Combining physical, mental, and social habits slows brain aging in 2-year trial

- More and more research continues to find evidence of a strong link between heart health and brain health.
- New results from the U.S. POINTER study found that improving cardiometabolic risk factors may not only improve heart health, but also brain health.
- Participants who followed a structured lifestyle intervention had significantly greater reductions in cardiometabolic risk factors than those who followed a self-guided plan.

Over the last few years, researchers have provided more evidence of a strong link between heart health and brain health.

For example, past studies have found a correlation between certain cardiovascular conditions and a negative impact on the brain, including high blood pressure, heart attack, and coronary artery disease.

Other research published in July 2026 found that even minor cardiac issues could potentially raise a person's dementia risk.

Now, results recently presented from the Alzheimer's Association U.S. Study to Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (U.S. POINTER) study report that improving cardiometabolic risk factors may not only improve heart health, but also brain health.

The primary results of the study were published in JAMA.

## Structured vs self-guided cardiometabolic lifestyle intervention

For the U.S. POINTER clinical trial, researchers recruited more than 2,000 older adults with an average age of about 68. All participants were at an elevated risk for cognitive decline due to a sedentary lifestyle, followed a suboptimal diet, and had experienced cardiovascular health issues.

"We have known for some time that midlife cardiovascular risk factors are associated long-term with increased risk of cognitive impairment, Alzheimer's disease, and related dementias, but an older adult cannot do time travel and change their midlife risk factors," Rachel Whitmer, PhD, chief of the Division of Epidemiology, director of the Population Brain Health Lab, and co-director of the Alzheimer's Disease Center at the University of California, Davis, and a principal investigator of the U.S. POINTER study, told Medical News Today.

"It is critical to understand how elderly adults at increased risk for Alzheimer's and other diseases that cause dementia can improve their brain health and reduce risk." — Rachel Whitmer, PhD

Study participants were randomly selected to follow either a structured or self-guided lifestyle intervention that focused on:

- Physical activity
- Following the MIND diet
- Social and cognitive engagement
- Health monitoring

The two interventions differed by the type of support provided and participant accountability.

## Significant improvements in 24 months

At the study's conclusion, when compared to study participants following the self-guided cardiometabolic lifestyle intervention, researchers found that participants who followed the structured program experienced significantly greater reductions in:

- Body mass index (BMI)
- Diastolic blood pressure
- Hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c)
- LDL cholesterol and triglycerides
- Resting heart rate
- Total cholesterol
- Waist circumference

"In a relatively short time period of 24 months, participants in the U.S. POINTER Study structured group were able to improve a number of cardiovascular risk factors more than the participants in the self-guided group," Whitmer explained.

"This is gold standard evidence from a clinical trial that in a high-risk group you can reduce cardiovascular disease risk in a short time period through a multi-domain lifestyle intervention," she said.

## Improved risk factors linked to better cognition

Additionally, researchers found that improvements in cardiometabolic risk factors were associated with better cognition in both the self-guided and structured participant groups.

Whitmer said these findings are significant because the extent to which participants improved their cardiovascular risk factors is associated with the degree of improvement in global cognition, so this is a modifiable risk factor that elderly individuals at high risk can address to improve their brain health.

"If we see this in 24 months, what could it mean over five, 10 years?" she continued. "This is a fascinating question that we hope to learn more about and even begin to answer in the U.S. POINTER Alumni Extension study, funded by the Alzheimer's Association, which will follow U.S. POINTER study participants for four more years."

## Benefits of improved cardiovascular health extend beyond the heart

MNT spoke with Cheng-Han Chen, MD, a board certified interventional cardiologist and medical director of the Structural Heart Program at MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center in Laguna Hills, CA, about the importance of cardiometabolic risk factor improvements for brain health.

Chen, who was not involved in this study, commented that these findings are encouraging because they demonstrate that the benefits of improving cardiovascular health may extend well beyond the heart.

"The [findings] provide additional incentive for us to aggressively identify and treat modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, such as high blood pressure, elevated cholesterol, diabetes, and physical inactivity. By addressing these factors early and consistently, we may be able to improve not only heart health but also help preserve brain health and cognitive function as people age." — Cheng-Han Chen, MD

Chen said he would like to see future research evaluate how sustainable these improvements in cognitive function remain over time.

"It would be important to determine whether the cognitive benefits persist over several years and whether continued lifestyle interventions are necessary to maintain them," he said.

"I would also like to see additional research examining which specific lifestyle modifications and cardiovascular risk-factor interventions provide the greatest cognitive benefit. This could help physicians develop more targeted, effective strategies to protect both cardiovascular and cognitive health as patients get older," Chen told us.

## How does improving cardiometabolic risk factors help the brain?

MNT also spoke with Dung Trinh, MD, an internist at MemorialCare Medical Group and chief medical officer of the Healthy Brain Clinic in Irvine, CA, who was likewise not involved in this study.

When asked how improving cardiometabolic risk factors can lead to better brain health, Trinh explained that the brain is one of the most metabolically active organs in the body and depends on a healthy cardiovascular system to deliver the oxygen and nutrients necessary for optimal function.

"Conditions such as high blood pressure, diabetes, obesity, and abnormal cholesterol can contribute to blood vessel damage, impaired circulation, inflammation, and metabolic dysfunction, all of which may negatively affect brain health over time," he continued.

"By improving these risk factors through physical activity, healthier nutrition, and other lifestyle changes, we may help support healthier blood vessels, improve insulin sensitivity, and reduce some of the processes associated with cognitive decline." — Dung Trinh, MD

Trinh said that what he found particularly interesting about the U.S. POINTER study is that improvements in several cardiometabolic measures were associated with better cognitive performance, suggesting these changes may be one of the biological pathways through which lifestyle interventions benefit the brain.

"However, further research is needed to establish the precise mechanisms," he added.
