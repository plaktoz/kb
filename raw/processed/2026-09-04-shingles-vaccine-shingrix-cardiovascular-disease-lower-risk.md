# Shingles Vaccine May Reduce Cardiovascular Disease Risk by 9%

A study published in *Nature Medicine* (August 26) found that the recombinant shingles vaccine Shingrix was associated with a 9% lower cardiovascular disease burden compared to the older live vaccine Zostavax.

The study examined 72,920 adults aged 60 and over. Roughly half received Zostavax between April–September 2017, just before the U.S. transitioned to Shingrix; the other half received Shingrix during the same window in 2018.

Over 7 years, Shingrix recipients showed:
- 10% lower coronary heart disease burden
- 12% lower heart failure burden
- 12% lower ischemic stroke burden in men (no significant association in women)
- 7% lower atrial fibrillation burden

Cardiologist Kevin Shah suggested inflammation as a likely mechanism — shingles triggers inflammatory responses linked to atherosclerosis, and preventing it may reduce that risk. Preventive cardiologist Kaustubh Dabhadkar pointed to decreased IL-6, an inflammatory marker, as the "most plausible" explanation, noting the effect appeared "much more quickly than would be explained by reduced cardiac events alone."

Shah cautioned: "This study does not prove that the vaccine prevents heart disease."

Key differences between the vaccines:
- Zostavax was a live attenuated vaccine; Shingrix is recombinant (engineered viral protein)
- Shingrix offers ~90% protection; Zostavax offered only 50–60%, with waning effectiveness
- Shingrix requires two doses; Zostavax was single-dose
- Zostavax was discontinued in the U.S. in 2020

The CDC recommends Shingrix for all adults 50 and older, and for immunocompromised adults 19 and older.
