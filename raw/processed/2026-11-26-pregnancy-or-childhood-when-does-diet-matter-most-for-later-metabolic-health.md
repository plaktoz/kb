# Pregnancy or childhood: When does diet matter most for later metabolic health?

Researchers tracking European families from pregnancy through adolescence examine when healthy eating habits take hold and which stages of development may matter most for future metabolic health.

Study: Tracking healthy and sustainable diet quality in relation to cardiometabolic health markers from prenatal life to adolescence: Results from a multi-cohort study. Image Credit: Ana Sha / Shutterstock

In a recent study published in the journal Clinical Nutrition, researchers investigated associations between maternal diet quality in pregnancy and offspring cardiometabolic health and diet quality through adolescence. Childhood diet quality tracked into adolescence more strongly than maternal diet quality and was associated with a lower metabolic syndrome risk score in adolescence.

The study involved researchers from the Norwegian Institute of Public Health, the Barcelona Institute for Global Health (ISGlobal), Vytautas Magnus University, the Bradford Institute for Health Research, and other academic and public health institutions spanning seven countries.

Maternal diet plays an important role in birth outcomes and long-term offspring health. Diet quality in early life may influence the risk of diseases, such as certain cancers, type 2 diabetes, obesity, and cardiovascular disease. Adherence to healthier and sustainable diets has been associated with lower cardiometabolic risk. Evidence on how these dietary patterns change from pregnancy through adolescence remains limited.

## About the study

This longitudinal observational study included mother-child pairs from the Human Early Life Exposome (HELIX) project, which encompassed six population-based birth cohorts from France, Greece, Lithuania, Norway, Spain, and the United Kingdom. Analyses involving maternal diet quality scores were restricted to four cohorts because maternal dietary information was unavailable or incomplete in the Lithuanian and United Kingdom cohorts.

Food frequency questionnaires were administered to collect dietary intake data during pregnancy, childhood (ages 5–12 years), and adolescence (ages 12–18 years). The researchers developed the healthy and sustainable eating index (HSEI) to estimate diet quality, incorporating aspects of diet sustainability and healthiness. The maternal index was based on the intake frequencies of seven food groups: pulses, vegetables, fruits, bread and cereals, fish and seafood, meat and meat products, and dairy products. Childhood and adolescent indices included sweets as an eighth food group.

HSEI scores were based on dietary recommendations from the countries in the HELIX project, the Mediterranean diet, World Health Organization guidance on healthy diets, the EAT-Lancet Planetary Health Diet, and the Nordic Nutrition Recommendations. Scores ranged from 0 to 100, with higher scores indicating greater adherence to healthy and sustainable dietary recommendations.

Body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-height ratio were computed. Bioelectrical impedance analysis was performed to estimate fat mass percentage. Blood samples were collected to measure triglycerides, total cholesterol, insulin, low-density lipoprotein cholesterol, and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C) levels.

The metabolic syndrome (MetS) score, based on HDL-C, waist circumference, blood pressure, insulin, and triglycerides, was computed as a continuous measure of cardiometabolic risk; a higher score indicated a less favorable metabolic health profile. The score did not establish a clinical diagnosis of MetS.

The researchers compared dietary scores across HELIX cohorts and examined how eating patterns tracked from pregnancy through childhood and adolescence. They also identified groups with distinct patterns of dietary change. Statistical models adjusted for potential confounders were used to explore associations between dietary quality and offspring cardiometabolic health markers.

## Findings

The study included 1,663 mother-child pairs from the HELIX project, with 864 offspring participating in the adolescent follow-up. On average, mothers were 31 years old during pregnancy, children were eight years old, and adolescents were 15 years old.

Meat consumption was highest in adolescence (approximately ten times per week), while fruit and vegetable consumption was highest during pregnancy. Dairy intake was frequent across all periods, whereas pulses were consumed approximately once per week. Children consumed sweets more frequently than adolescents.

Among participants with available dietary scores, mothers had the highest average HSEI score (62.8 out of 100), compared with 58.0 in children and 55.2 in adolescents, indicating greater adherence to healthy and sustainable dietary recommendations during pregnancy. HSEI scores differed significantly across participating cohorts.

The maternal HSEI score was weakly associated with the child HSEI score but was not significantly associated with the adolescent HSEI score after adjustment for confounding factors. Childhood dietary scores showed a stronger relationship with adolescent scores, suggesting that eating habits established earlier in life tend to persist.

The researchers suggested that increasing dietary independence, peer influences, and changing social environments may partly explain these patterns.

Diet quality declined most steeply over time among the offspring of mothers with the lowest HSEI. Nearly a third of children and adolescents were overweight or obese.

Maternal HSEI showed a statistically non-significant inverse association with the child MetS score, but was not associated with most offspring cardiometabolic health markers. Higher maternal HSEI was also associated with lower adolescent diastolic blood pressure, though this isolated finding warrants caution because the analyses involved multiple statistical comparisons.

Better childhood dietary quality was associated with lower adolescent MetS scores, with no significant associations identified for other adolescent cardiometabolic markers. Dietary trajectory groups were also not significantly associated with adolescent cardiometabolic outcomes.

## Conclusions

The findings identify childhood as a potentially important period for establishing dietary habits that persist into adolescence, with limited evidence linking maternal nutrition during pregnancy to later cardiometabolic health.

The childhood and adolescent dietary questionnaires were unvalidated. Parents reported their children's diets over the previous year, while adolescents reported their own consumption over the preceding three months. Differences in dietary reporting methods, substantial loss to follow-up, and residual confounding limit the interpretation of these findings.

The unvalidated HSEI assessed consumption frequencies rather than food quantities and did not directly measure environmental impacts. Statistical analyses were not adjusted for multiple comparisons, and the observational design precludes causal conclusions.

Strategies to improve children's diets, including school-based nutrition policies and support for families to adopt healthier eating habits, may support better metabolic health during adolescence, while promoting healthier, more sustainable maternal diets remains important.
