Ultra-processed foods may be harming brain health in otherwise healthy adults

A study of Canadian adults suggests that those who consumed the most ultra-processed foods (UPFs) were 40% more likely to show small areas of brain damage on MRI scans than those who consumed the least.

The findings also indicate that females with the highest UPF intake were nearly twice as likely to show signs of brain damage as those with the lowest intake.

UPFs typically displaced healthier options, as those with higher intakes also ate fewer fruits, vegetables, whole grains, and other minimally processed foods.

The associated brain changes can be early indicators associated with an increased risk of stroke and future memory problems. However, the study cannot establish cause and effect, and further research is necessary.

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs), such as packaged snacks, sugary drinks, processed meats and many ready meals, often contain high levels of salt, sugar and unhealthy fats, alongside additives and ingredients designed to improve taste, texture and shelf life.

Regularly consuming large amounts of UPFs has been associated with an increased risk of obesity, type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease and other adverse health outcomes.

Now, research suggests that consuming high amounts of ultra-processed foods may be associated with signs of brain damage in otherwise healthy middle-aged adults.

The study, published in JAMA Network Open, found that participants with the highest UPF intake were 40% more likely to show certain brain changes on magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) scans than those with the lowest intake.

These changes, which may occur without noticeable symptoms, can be early indicators of damage to small blood vessels in the brain and have been associated with an increased risk of stroke and cognitive problems later in life.

While further research is still necessary, the findings raise questions about how dietary habits during middle age may influence long-term brain health.

## Higher ultra-processed food intake linked to brain changes

Researchers analyzed data from nearly 9,000 Canadian adults aged 35 to 69 who had no history of heart disease. Participants were enrolled into either the Canadian Alliance for Healthy Hearts and Minds or the Prospective Urban and Rural Epidemiology-MIND study.

Participants completed detailed dietary questionnaires and underwent MRI brain scans. Neurologists reviewed the scans without knowing the participants’ dietary habits.

The researchers classified foods according to the extent and purpose of the industrial processing used to produce them. The researchers found that participants who consumed the highest amounts of these foods were 40% more likely to have small areas of brain damage than those who consumed the least.

“The finding is concerning because the brain changes we studied are not considered benign,” Russell de Souza, ScD, RD, first author of the study and an associate scientist at the Population Health Research Institute (PHRI) of McMaster University and Hamilton Health Sciences, told Medical News Today.

“White matter hyperintensities and silent brain infarcts are established markers of cerebrovascular disease and have been associated in previous research with a higher risk of future stroke, cognitive decline, and dementia,” said de Souza.

“That said, it is important to distinguish between relative risk and absolute risk. In our study, participants with the highest ultra-processed food intake had about a 40% higher likelihood of vascular brain injury compared with those with the lowest intake. However, because these MRI findings are relatively uncommon in middle-aged adults, that does not mean 40% of people will develop brain injury,” he explained.

“Put another way, if roughly 1 in 10 people had these MRI findings, a 40% relative increase would translate to about 14 in 100 people rather than 10 in 100 people. The individual increase in risk is modest, but because ultra-processed foods are consumed by a large proportion of the population, even modest increases in risk could have important implications at a population level.”

– Russell de Souza, ScD, RD

“What makes the finding particularly noteworthy is that these associations were observed in a relatively healthy, middle-aged population before measurable differences in memory and thinking were apparent. At the same time, this was an observational study, so we cannot conclude that ultra-processed foods caused these brain changes, although our models did account for other risk factors for brain injury,” the study author added.

## Association stronger among females

The association appeared particularly strong among females. Women in the highest UPF consumption group were nearly twice as likely to show signs of brain damage as those in the lowest consumption group.

The researchers noted that the findings were unexpected because the study participants were relatively young and had no history of heart disease. The findings suggest that signs of brain damage associated with vascular health may be detectable before people develop noticeable symptoms.

“Overall, the associations appeared stronger in women, but the reasons remain uncertain,” de Souza noted to MNT. “I would view this as an intriguing signal that merits further study rather than a firm conclusion that women are more susceptible to the effects of ultra-processed foods on brain health.”

“Several explanations are possible. Women and men differ in body fat distribution, vascular biology, cardiometabolic risk, and hormonal factors, all of which can influence brain and vascular health,” he suggested.

“In particular, hormonal changes that occur during midlife and the menopausal transition may affect vulnerability to vascular risk factors. It’s also possible that women and men consume different types of ultra-processed foods, or that ultra-processed foods displace different whole foods in their diets,” de Souza detailed.

“Two people may consume similar amounts of ultra-processed foods, but obtain them from very different sources with different nutritional profiles. Finally, we cannot exclude the possibility that the finding reflects residual confounding or chance, particularly given the smaller sample sizes involved in subgroup analyses,” he continued.

## Dietary habits in midlife may impact long-term brain health

As the study analyzed data from relatively healthy adults aged 35 to 69, it highlights the potential importance of dietary choices in middle-aged adults and how it could affect their long-term brain health.

“Many of the biological processes that contribute to stroke, cognitive decline, and dementia develop gradually over decades, often before symptoms become apparent,” de Souza noted.

“In our study, we observed differences in MRI markers of vascular brain injury in a relatively healthy, middle-aged population, even though we did not observe differences in memory and thinking. This suggests that potentially important brain changes may already be detectable during midlife,” he told us.

“I view our findings as adding to growing evidence that dietary habits in midlife may have implications for long-term health, including brain health. While we cannot determine cause and effect from this study, the results support the large body of evidence that lifelong healthy dietary habits that emphasize whole and minimally processed foods, while limiting ultra-processed foods, probably promote brain health as well,” he concluded.

## How might ultra-processed foods affect brain health?

Dietary patterns high in UPFs have been associated with several health concerns, including obesity and metabolic problems. These conditions can affect blood vessels and may contribute to inflammation, potentially influencing blood flow to the brain.

Additionally, in the study, those who consumed the most UPFs also had more body fat and more deep abdominal fat than those who consumed the least. Deep abdominal fat, also known as visceral fat, surrounds internal organs and is associated with inflammation and an increased risk of cardiovascular and metabolic conditions.

Damage to small blood vessels in the brain may develop when these vessels become impaired, potentially affecting the supply of oxygen and nutrients to brain tissue. Over time, such damage can be associated with an increased risk of stroke and problems with memory and thinking.

“One possibility is that diets higher in ultra-processed foods contribute to risk factors such as excess body fat, visceral fat, high blood pressure, diabetes, and other cardiometabolic conditions that can affect blood vessels throughout the body, including those that supply the brain,” de Souza explained.

“The brain relies on a healthy network of blood vessels to deliver oxygen and nutrients. Over time, factors that damage those blood vessels may contribute to the development of vascular brain injury,” he continued.

“Other research groups are actively investigating whether characteristics of ultra-processed foods themselves, including aspects of food formulation, additives, food structure, or processing-related exposures, might influence health in ways that are not fully explained by calories or body weight alone,” added the researcher.

However, the researchers did not establish whether body fat, inflammation, or blood vessel damage explained the association between UPF intake and the MRI findings.

Additionally, as they assessed participants’ dietary habits and brain scans at a single point in time, they cannot determine whether greater consumption preceded the brain changes or whether reducing UPF intake would prevent or slow their development. Other factors may also contribute to the observed association.

Thus, the findings do not mean that everyone who eats UPFs will develop brain damage, experience memory loss, or have a stroke. Further research is still necessary to determine the biological mechanisms involved.

## Ultra-processed foods may displace healthier options

The study also found that people who ate more UPFs tended to eat fewer minimally processed foods, including fruits, vegetables, and grains.

On average, ultra-processed foods accounted for approximately 25% of participants’ daily food servings. Among the highest consumers, these foods made up 45% of daily servings, compared with 16% among those who consumed the least.

Meanwhile, whole and minimally processed foods accounted for 41% of daily servings among the highest consumers of ultra-processed foods, compared with 68% among the lowest consumers.

This suggests that UPFs may not simply be added to an otherwise balanced diet. Instead, they may replace foods that provide important nutrients and dietary fiber.

The researchers also highlighted the role of affordability, convenience, availability, and marketing in shaping dietary habits.

For people with limited time or financial resources, UPFs may be a more accessible option than fresh or minimally processed alternatives. These factors could contribute to differences in dietary quality and potentially widen existing health inequalities.

## What can people do to support brain health?

Although this study does not establish that avoiding UPFs will prevent brain damage, its findings add to the evidence supporting a balanced dietary pattern.

People looking to improve their diet may consider:

Eating more minimally processed foods: Fruits, vegetables, whole grains, beans, and other nutrient-rich foods can help provide fiber, vitamins, and minerals.

Limiting foods high in added sugar, sodium, and saturated fat: Checking nutrition labels can help people compare products and make informed choices.

Making gradual, affordable changes: Frozen vegetables, canned beans, and other convenient options can help people include more nutritious foods without relying entirely on fresh ingredients.

Focusing on overall dietary patterns: No single food determines a person’s risk of stroke or cognitive decline. Diet is one of several factors that may influence long-term health.

It is also important to remember that UPFs are not all nutritionally identical. Their ingredients and nutritional profiles vary, and the current study does not show that every food in this category carries the same risk.

“Some relatively affordable and convenient options include beans, lentils, oats, frozen vegetables, frozen fruit, eggs, milk, plain yogurt, peanut butter, and canned fish,” de Souza highlighted. “These foods often provide excellent nutritional value, require relatively little preparation, and can fit within many household budgets.”

“Practical changes might include replacing sugary drinks with water, choosing plain yogurt instead of highly processed snack foods, incorporating more beans or lentils into meals, or keeping frozen vegetables on hand for convenience. Small substitutions made consistently can add up over time,” he advised.
