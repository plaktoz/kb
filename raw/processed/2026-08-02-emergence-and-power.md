# Emergence and Power — Four 13th-Century Sisters Who Became Queens

From Farnam Street (fs.blog). The article uses the mental model of emergence to analyze an extraordinary historical case from 13th-century Provence.

## What Is Emergence?

Emergence refers to genuinely novel qualities unique to a system — separate from its individual components. A school of fish provides a clear analogy: studying one fish reveals nothing about what 10,000 together will produce. The collective behavior is not merely the sum of individual behaviors.

> "Effects are emergent if they cannot be thus calculated because they are qualitatively novel compared to the causes from which they emerge."

Emergence occurs when components self-organize to produce outcomes that are "neither obvious nor predictable" from examining individual parts alone.

## The Historical Case: Four Queens of Provence

In 1230s Provence, four sisters — Marguerite, Eleanor, Sanchia, and Beatrice — daughters of a Count, held modest prospects. Yet all four became queens through a cascade of interconnected circumstances:

- **Marguerite** married **Louis IX of France**, elevating the family's status
- **Eleanor** married **Henry III of England**, partly because he wanted what France had selected
- **Sanchia** married **Richard, Earl of Cornwall**, who later acquired the title King of Germany
- **Beatrice** inherited Provence and married Charles, eventually becoming Queen of Sicily

Each marriage built on the last — none was inevitable in isolation. The rising status of each sister made the next sister more attractive as a match.

## The Emergent Power in Action

Their collective influence meant that "almost nothing of significance" in 13th-century Western Europe was untouched by this family. A vivid example: when their uncle was captured, all four kingdoms coordinated — trade shutdowns, arrests, road closures, financial pressure — effectively imposing international sanctions until he was released.

This collective outcome was not achievable by any individual queen. The coordinated power exceeded any simple addition of individual influence — a hallmark of emergent systems. The sisters' combined influence shaped Western European politics for over 30 years.

## Why Emergence Matters

Emergence explains why reductionist analysis — breaking systems into parts and studying each part — often fails to predict system-level behavior. Complex adaptive systems routinely produce emergent properties that surprise observers who only examine components in isolation.
