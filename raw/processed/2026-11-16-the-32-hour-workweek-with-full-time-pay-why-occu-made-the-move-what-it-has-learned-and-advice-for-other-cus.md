# The 32-Hour Workweek (With Full-Time Pay): Why OCCU Made the Move, What It Has Learned and Advice for Other CUs

EUGENE, Ore.–Why would a credit union move to a 32-hour workweek while continuing to pay employees their full-time salaries? What does it take to make it really work? It’s about a lot more than just changing the schedule, according to Oregon Community Credit Union, which is sharing with CU Daily readers details around its innovative new employment policy.

As the CU Daily first reported here, OCCU is expanding its 32-hour workweek with plans to transition most of its nearly 700 employees to a four-day workweek while maintaining full wages and benefits by the end of 2027.

The credit union is not closing services for an extra day, changing business hours or relying on staffing increases to make the model work. Instead, teams align staffing and coverage with member demand before transitioning.

OCCU first introduced the 32-hour workweek in its call center in 2023 to test whether a shorter workweek could better support employees while maintaining the high standards of service members expect, it explained.

By the end of 2026, 15 teams and 188 employees across OCCU will operate on a 32-hour workweek. The expansion also includes OCCU’s first branch pilot in Roseburg, which adopted the 32-hour workweek on Oct. 1.

As the CU Daily also reported, OCCU has signed a nonbinding letter of intent to sell its 10-acre Eugene headquarters property to Costco Wholesale as the credit union rethinks its workplace strategy following six years of largely remote operations. The credit union said it currently uses about 40% of its 53,000-square-foot headquarters building, which sits on roughly 10 acres.

Below, President and CEO Greg Schumacher details why the $3.5-billion Oregon Community CU has made the move, what it has learned, and what advice it has to share with other credit unions.

The CU Daily: When the CU Daily first reported this story, it was when OCCU implemented the model with its call center, in part to make the job more attractive and help reduce turnover. What was the response after it was implemented, and did it accomplish these goals?

Schumacher: The results have been encouraging for both our employees and for our members. Before the transition, our average employee tenure in the call center was about nine months. Today, it is 3.1 years. Average daily unplanned absences have also declined from five employees to 1.6. Those are meaningful indicators of greater workforce stability.

We have also seen improvements in the member experience. Average call wait time decreased from five minutes, 47 seconds to one minute, 17 seconds, while call abandonment declined from 16.30% to 6.86%. We are also seeing fewer transfers and more member needs resolved on the first call.

The call center now serves members with 51 full-time employees, compared with a previous peak of 67. That change occurred through normal workforce movement and improved retention, not through workforce reductions, while service results continued to improve.

The CU Daily: What was learned during that initial implementation with the call center, and what changes, if any, were made?

Schumacher: The biggest lesson learned was that a shorter workweek cannot simply be placed on top of existing processes. The model works when a team first examines how work gets done, removes unnecessary steps, improves workflows, strengthens backup coverage and uses technology more effectively.

The call center pilot also showed us that we need to look at employee and member measures in tandem. We monitor attendance, retention and workload while we also look at service outcomes such as wait times, abandoned calls, transfers and first-call resolution. That combination helps us determine whether the model is creating sustainable improvements rather than moving pressure somewhere else.

Those lessons shaped our broader rollout. Teams now complete readiness work around workflows, peak demand periods, cross-training, coverage, meeting practices and performance expectations before transitioning.

The CU Daily: What changes were made in rolling out the program more broadly to the workforce? How is it being rolled out and over what schedule?

Schumacher: We are expanding deliberately, team by team, rather than setting a single transition date for the entire organization. Each team must demonstrate that it has the coverage, cross-training, workflow improvements and service measures needed to maintain or improve member experience, productivity and business results.

By the end of 2026, 15 teams and 188 employees will operate on a 32-hour workweek. Those teams represent a broad cross-section of OCCU, including business intelligence, consumer underwriting, video tellers and card services. Our goal is to transition most of our nearly 700 employees by the end of 2027.

We are letting readiness and results determine the pace. If the supporting measures are not in place, a team does not transition simply to meet a calendar date.

Q: Is there anyone or any department to whom this policy does not apply?

Schumacher: Executive team members are not included in the 32-hour workweek transition. Our broader goal is participation by the rest of OCCU’s teams and branches by the end of 2027, provided each team meets the necessary readiness, performance and service standards. Management will be eligible only after all individual contributors have transitioned.

The CU Daily: How does this policy have to be adjusted for branches, where people must be physically present? Does it require hiring any additional staff?

Schumacher: The approach itself doesn’t change for branches. Every team, whether employees work remotely or on-site, must redesign its workflows and schedules around member needs and demonstrate that it can maintain or improve service, productivity and business results before transitioning.

For branches, that requires particularly thoughtful coverage, because members must continue to have reliable access to employees throughout normal operating hours. We are not closing branches for an additional day or reducing branch hours. Instead, teams stagger schedules, cross-train employees and align coverage with member demand.

Our first branch pilot began at our Roseburg, Ore. branch on Oct. 1. It is operating with normal member volume, full-service expectations and no change to branch hours. We are tracking service levels, wait times, appointment availability, coverage and process improvements to help inform future branch transitions.

And no, this transformation does not require us to hire additional staff to make the 32-hour workweek possible. The model is about making better use of the talented employees we already have by improving schedules, cross-training, eliminating unnecessary work and strengthening processes so we can maintain the service our members expect.

The CU Daily: How much of OCCU’s workforce is remote, and how many must be on-site?

Schumacher: More than 60% of OCCU employees work remotely. Employees whose roles require an in-person presence, including many branch positions, work on-site to serve members.

Our workplace strategy is not a one-size-fits-all remote-work policy. We align work location, physical space and schedules with how each team operates and how members need to be served.

The CU Daily: What are the challenges implementing this policy that you might share with other credit unions? Difficulties scheduling meetings, staff in branches, etc.?

Schumacher: The most important advice I would share is that this cannot be treated as a scheduling exercise. The difficult work happens before a team changes schedules. Leaders must understand demand patterns, remove unnecessary work, clarify performance expectations, build backup coverage and cross-train employees.

Meeting norms are part of that work. With employees on different schedules, teams must be more disciplined about which meetings are truly necessary, who needs to attend and how information is documented for people who are not working that day. Participating teams also are reviewing peer-to-peer communication as part of their preparation.

Branches and other member-facing teams require especially careful planning. Coverage must be designed around when and how members need service, not around the assumption that everyone will have the same day off. We also monitor workload, stress, service levels and overtime so the transition does not simply shift pressure to another employee or team.

Finally, organizations should be prepared to adjust. If member service or business results begin moving in the wrong direction, leaders need reliable data and the willingness to adjust schedules or processes.

The CU Daily: Is there a policy in place around which day employees can take off? For example, it would seem most would want to take either Mondays or Fridays.

Schumacher: A 32-hour schedule does not automatically mean a three-day weekend. Employees may have Tuesday, Wednesday or Thursday off depending on member demand, team coverage and individual schedules.

Each team builds its schedule around service needs. That means maintaining coverage throughout the entire workweek, including Saturday and Sunday where applicable, rather than concentrating everyone’s day off on Monday or Friday.

The CU Daily: OCCU has said its workweek model follows a 100-80-100 approach: 100% of the work, performed in 80% of the time for 100% of the pay. The question many will have is: Was the average employee simply underutilized, or are there too many employees if the average person can get what had been five days of work done in four?

Schumacher: This is not based on the belief that employees were underutilized or that OCCU had too many people. It is based on the recognition that every organization has processes, meetings, handoffs and manual work that can be improved.

We are not asking employees to compress five days of unchanged work into four. Teams redesign the work itself by eliminating unnecessary steps, improving workflows, using technology more effectively and focusing employee time on activities that create the greatest value for members.

The call center is a good example. It now serves members with 51 full-time employees, compared with a previous peak of 67, but that change happened through normal workforce movement and improved retention. At the same time, call wait times, call abandonment, employee attendance and employee tenure improved.

The purpose is not to reduce headcount. It is to build a more sustainable operating model in which experienced employees remain with OCCU, work is designed more effectively and members receive stronger service.

The CU Daily: How does OCCU measure productivity and whether the right amount of work is getting done, and has AI played any role?

Schumacher: We do not rely on hours worked as the sole measure of productivity. Each team has operational and member-service measures appropriate to its work. Depending on the team, those may include service levels, turnaround times, accuracy, workload, wait times, appointment availability, first-contact resolution, overtime and business results.

A team transitions only after demonstrating through measurable outcomes that it can maintain or improve member experience, service, productivity and business performance. We then continue monitoring results after the transition so we can identify issues and adjust.

Technology is part of the transformation. We are using better tools to reduce manual processes, streamline decision-making, improve accuracy and give employees more capacity to focus on members.

The CU Daily: What has been the response from other employers?

Schumacher: We have received significant interest from other employers and industry peers who are curious about both the results and the operational work behind the model. The conversation is often less about the four-day schedule itself and more about how we measure readiness, protect service and redesign work.

We are happy to share what we are learning, but we are also clear that this is not a plug-and-play model. It requires strong data, operational discipline, leadership commitment and a willingness to change long-standing processes.

The CU Daily:: The obvious question: What has been the response from employees?

Schumacher: The response has been very positive, particularly from employees who have experienced the model. They describe the additional day away from work as genuine time to disconnect, take care of personal responsibilities and return more focused on their teammates and members. One participating employee described it as giving team members “mind space” so they are not trying to fit errands and responsibilities around a traditional five-day schedule.

The workforce results support that feedback. In the call center, average tenure increased from nine months to 3.1 years, while average daily unplanned absences declined from five to 1.6.

At the same time, employees understand that the transition is earned through readiness. It requires each employee to rethink workflows for their team, improve coverage and remain accountable for member service and business results.

Q: Finally, what’s next?

Schumacher: What comes next is careful expansion and continued learning. We will continue evaluating the Roseburg branch pilot, expanding to additional teams and branches, and tracking member service, employee experience, productivity and business results.

The 32-hour workweek is also only one part of our larger workforce transformation. We are continuing to invest in technology, employee development, flexible and remote work, and more intentional workplace space.

Our guiding question will remain the same: Does this help us better serve our members? If the answer is yes and the results support it, we will keep moving forward.
