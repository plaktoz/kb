# Be a Ford, Then a Sloan

In June 1896, a young engineer smashed through a brick wall with an axe — the only way to get his creation out of the shed where he'd built it. That man was Henry Ford, and the creation was the Ford Quadricycle.

Ford's core insight was viewing *the factory itself as the product.* While competitors focused on the car, Ford obsessed over production efficiency — every footstep, every minute. The result: the Model T's price dropped from $850 in 1908 to $260 by the 1920s, giving the masses access to automobiles. By the early 1920s, Ford held roughly 60% of the U.S. auto market.

But consumer preferences began shifting around 1921. People wanted variety, status, luxury — not just identical black Model Ts. Ford's son Edsel raised these concerns and was rebuffed. Ford reportedly attacked a new prototype with his bare hands when he returned from a trip to find his team had developed one without his approval. The very rigidity that built his empire now constrained it.

Alfred Sloan took over General Motors in 1923 with a different insight: *the company itself as the product.* He was a systems engineer who built adaptability into GM's structure — decentralized decisions, clear divisions of labor, data tracking, strategic financial controls. GM's market share roughly doubled by 1925. By 1927, Chevrolet outsold the Model T.

The lesson isn't simply "Ford bad, Sloan good." As Bloom frames it:

- **Ford's approach worked, until it didn't** — ruthless focus and centralized control enabled breakthrough innovation, but became a cage.
- **Sloan's approach wouldn't have worked, until it did** — organizational design and decentralization enabled adaptation at scale, but would have failed at the zero-to-one stage.

The real takeaway: *"Be a Ford, then a Sloan."*

This pattern appears across life domains:

- **Health:** Intense routines from your 20s may not survive new responsibilities in your 30s and 40s.
- **Relationships:** Deep-connection rituals require adjustment as life accelerates.
- **Money:** Frugality that builds independence can later prevent enriching experiences.

In business, most founders start as natural Fords — hands-on, controlling, executing everything. But the skills that reach a first million differ from those needed to scale further.

## Navigating the Transition

**Diagnose your current state:** Identify operational blind spots, bottlenecks, and habits that once served you but now constrain you.

**Check in regularly** with these questions:
- What single change would create the greatest impact if everything else stayed fixed?
- What decision have you been avoiding to spare someone disappointment?
- What would a world-class CEO change in the first 100 days — and what's stopping you from doing it now?

> "Adaptability is the key to life. Be a Ford, then a Sloan."
