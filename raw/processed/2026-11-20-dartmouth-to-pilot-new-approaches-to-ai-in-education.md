# Dartmouth to Pilot New Approaches to AI in Education

The initiatives bring new opportunities for faculty and students to engage with AI or to teach and learn without it.

From new approaches in research to redesigned courses and new academic programs, Dartmouth is giving faculty more ways to explore artificial intelligence and more opportunities to question its impact.

A new slate of grants, computing investments, curricular initiatives, and campus programming will support both innovation and critical inquiry alongside efforts to develop ethical guidelines for the use of AI across schools.

“This year’s AI strategy is an investment in people: our students, faculty, and staff,” says Roopika Risam, Dartmouth AI Fellow and chair of the Department of Film and Media Studies. Risam will consult with a cross-school faculty advisory committee to identify and address gaps in policies, map the landscape of AI activities at Dartmouth, facilitate community conversations to develop shared understanding and principles for generative AI, and evaluate pilot programs to identify which Dartmouth will continue to pursue.

“We’re working to ensure that everyone at Dartmouth has access to the support they need as they navigate the challenges and opportunities posed by AI,” Risam says.

Through AI Research Catalyst Grants, the Office of the President will offer seed funding for four to eight interdisciplinary projects that use AI in research or creative methods or treat AI as an object of critical inquiry.

Dartmouth has innovative AI research in medicine, engineering, and other scientific disciplines, as well as in the arts, humanities, social sciences, and business, says Risam.

“We also have a unique concentration of faculty who study AI itself, from examining the environmental impact of AI to analyzing large language models and their impact on society. I hope to see both types of research represented in our grant program, as well as projects that promote collaboration across schools,” she says.

At the same time, Dartmouth is expanding access to AI computing through remote use of high-performance graphics processing units, aka GPUs, at the Massachusetts Green High Performance Computing Center. This will provide Dartmouth faculty and student researchers specialized, energy-efficient computing power needed to develop and run advanced AI applications, positioning them to respond nimbly to emerging AI opportunities.

For teaching, a suite of grants will enable faculty to experiment with pedagogical practices:

- Course Redesign Grants offer funding for faculty who are rethinking assignments and courses, whether through AI-resilient learning, AI with purpose, or a combination of both;
- AI Curricular Design Grants offer support for faculty who are developing new degrees, majors, or minors related to AI; and
- Pedagogical Innovation Grants provide funding for faculty and student-facing staff experimenting with innovative teaching practices that can be used across disciplines and schools

Risam, Vice Provost for Research Dean Madden, and Professor of Engineering Peter Chin, who chaired last month’s AI at 70: What Must Remain Human? conference, outlined the opportunities in an email to faculty colleagues earlier this month.

“We’re looking for proposals aimed at strengthening students’ core human capacities,” says Risam. “In the case of assignments involving writing and completing problem sets, I would love to see creative approaches to helping students engage in the intellectual processes and the struggles that help them learn without using AI. I’m also curious about the ways faculty are using AI to augment students’ learning, as it’s appropriate to the learning objectives of their courses,” she says.

The teaching grants build on earlier efforts to support faculty experimentation and innovation in teaching. From AI coaches designed to improve students’ peer feedback in writing courses to assignments that required students to analyze their interactions with the tool, faculty have used Generative AI Teaching Grants offered by the Dartmouth Center for the Advancement of Learning since 2024 to integrate AI into their classrooms.

These efforts are unfolding alongside a broader conversation about the responsible use of AI. “We are responding to the swiftly moving landscape of AI by developing robust policies for AI use and disclosure in the classroom and beyond, and by building shared vocabulary and principles through community conversations,” Risam says.

Each school will develop guidelines for ethical uses of AI in teaching, research, and operations.

“As policy conversations move forward, we’re looking at ways to provide more clarity and consistency for students, even when individual course or assignment policies differ,” says Risam. “We must put human well-being at the center of everything we do, and our metric should be whether AI leads to improvement, rather than decline, in our community members’ experiences.”

Also on the agenda is campus-wide programming featuring diverse perspectives on generative AI in higher education. On March 3, Tressie McMillan Cottom will give a lecture on AI and Its Discontents, organized by the Leslie Center for the Humanities. The University Seminar, AI and Foreign Language Teaching and Learning, convened by language program directors Tania Convertini and Roberto Rey Aguado, is also underway.

“As we mark the 70th anniversary of the 1956 Dartmouth Summer Research Project on Artificial Intelligence, now is the time to make sure that Dartmouth shapes the future of AI too,” Risam says.

To this end, a central priority is on preparing students for a lifetime of learning and responsible leadership, Risam says.

“When our students leave us, they need to be able to think, write, evaluate evidence, and persuade,” Risam says. “They also need to be able to make informed decisions about their own AI use and the role it has in the organizations where they will lead.”
