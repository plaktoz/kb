# Common Rheumatoid Arthritis Drug May Support Hair Regrowth in Alopecia

## Summary

Two phase 3 clinical trials found that upadacitinib (brand name Rinvoq), a JAK inhibitor used for rheumatoid arthritis, helped people with severe alopecia areata regrow significant amounts of hair.

## How the Trials Worked

Nearly 1,400 participants aged 12–64, all with severe alopecia areata causing at least 50% scalp hair loss, were enrolled across 112–125 clinical centers in 25–28 countries. Trials ran from November 2023 to July 2025. Participants received either 15 mg daily, 30 mg daily, or a placebo, with assessments through 24 weeks and a 28-week extension period.

## Key Results

- ~50% on the 15 mg dose and ~54% on the 30 mg dose achieved ≥80% scalp coverage
- ~46% on the 30 mg dose achieved ≥90% coverage, vs. ~1% on placebo
- ~23% on the high dose achieved complete scalp regrowth, vs. nearly none on placebo
- Over half of the 30 mg group showed significant eyelash and eyebrow regrowth
- Common side effects: acne, colds, throat irritation, upper respiratory infections; serious side effects were rare

## Expert Perspectives

Spencer Kobren, founder of the American Hair Loss Association, called the results "very encouraging," noting that having another effective drug available is beneficial. However, he cautioned that these are "powerful systemic immune modulating drugs" with real and potentially serious risks.

Three JAK inhibitors are already FDA-approved for severe alopecia areata: Olumiant, Litfulo, and Leqselvi. Kobren noted that when treatment stops, hair regrowth can be lost, potentially requiring long-term use.

Stanford dermatologist Anthony Oro said upadacitinib's already-known safety profile is an advantage, and that adding another JAK inhibitor option "will hopefully bring down the cost of the therapy."

## Context

About 7 million Americans have alopecia areata. Hair loss can cause significant emotional distress, including anxiety, depression, and social withdrawal. Kobren emphasized that decisions about accepting treatment risks are "intensely personal" and should be made with a physician who understands both the disease and the medication.

The findings were published in *JAMA Dermatology* on August 12.
