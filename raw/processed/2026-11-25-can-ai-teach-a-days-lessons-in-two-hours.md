# Can AI teach a day's lessons in two hours?

Imagine a teacher that remembers exactly how each student answered a question six months ago, knows exactly where the gaps are in their knowledge, what they need to study to catch up, and makes them return again and again until they have mastered the material.

Now suppose that teacher isn't human at all but an AI tutor.

On top of that, suppose each student could spend just two hours a day in concentrated study and learn twice as fast as students spending a full day in a normal classroom, leaving the rest of the time for extracurricular activities.

That's the vision of Alpha School, a network of 46 private schools across the United States that believes AI supported learning is opening a new frontier in K-12 education.

Proponents of artificial intelligence in the classroom believe they may have cracked two of the thorniest problems in childhood education: providing personalized tutoring at scale and tailoring the pace of learning to allow individual students to master a subject before moving on.

But competing educational visions, worries over standards, concerns about time spent on screens, inconclusive research, and a huge addressable market for technology providers are making for a neuralgic and often polarized debate.

At the same time, surveys show worrying declines in attainment and increasing public dissatisfaction with the provision of K-12 education.

For Carl Hendrick, chief learning scientist at Alpha School, one of the major benefits of AI tutoring is it can follow the performance of individual students over months, replacing the haphazard testing and patchy learning that can occur in classes of 20 or 30 students.

"We're getting hundreds of data points," Hendrick told Newsweek. "What is the latency? Did they pause on a question? Did they just try to guess their way through five multiple choice questions? And then the real thing is that the system can get a composite gathering of all of that data over weeks and months."

Alpha is quick to stress that its model is not about students spending all their time in front of screens. It claims screen use is about in line with the national average. Alpha uses what it calls "guides" to motivate students instead of traditional teachers.

"One of the misconceptions is that we are always on computers," Chauncey Nartey, a reading guide at Alpha School in New York, told Newsweek during a visit on September 30.

## Rising Dissatisfaction With K-12 Education

Increasing the time that teachers spend one-on-one with pupils has long been the holy grail for education. The problem has always been cost.

Those like Hendrick believe that the prize is now within reach with the latest wave of generative AI offering personalized tutoring, study plans, and testing.

The debate around children's use of technology and its effect on learning has been raging for years. The rapid pace of AI adoption adds an additional layer of complexity to that debate and comes at time of much hand wringing about the state of education in general.

A broad educational survey by the OECD last year showed that performance in math, science and reading across 760,000 school pupils in 91 countries continued to decline sharply. The OECD described last year's drop in reading scores as "huge."

On top of that, a Gallup poll in September showed Americans' satisfaction with the provision of K-12 education hit its lowest level since the series began 27 years ago, explaining the openness for experimental alternatives.

Generative AI has sparked a new wave of development in educational technology, or edtech, building on previous innovation waves from the massive open online courses, or MOOCs, of the noughties through the widespread adoption of online teaching and video learning platforms during the Covid pandemic.

Alexandre Glaser, a partner at EduCapital, a 200 million-euro European venture capital fund that specializes in edtech investments, told Newsweek that a structural shift is underway across the entire sector.

"AI will enable personalization of education to happen at scale. So it affects every segment within our market, from early age learning to life-long learning, corporate training, and higher education, and affects them quite significantly," he said.

The size of the edtech market is huge. Exact numbers are hard to come by, but Glaser estimates that education globally accounts for 4-6 percent of gross domestic product, or $6 trillion to $8 trillion.

Edtech represents about 8-10 percent of the market, or up to $800 billion. Glaser believes that the AI share of edtech will grow to $90 billion or more by 2030, and possibly a lot more.

Joe Liemandt, principal of Alpha School and billionaire tech investor, and his co-founder MacKenzie Price, hope to scale Alpha's model through its AI system TimeBack, which is being used in Unbound Academy, an online publicly funded charter school in Arizona, and piloted in public schools in Texas.

"We're using artificial intelligence to raise human intelligence," Price told Newsweek at Alpha's New York school.

"From an academic perspective, we're able to do that by leveraging AI to provide personalized lesson plans and assessments, not chatbots, and then we're able to give them more time to be able to have that human connection and develop life skills."

## Studies on Effects of AI Learning Are Mixed

Studies on the affect of AI on learning outcomes show a mixed picture. A recent study of over 27,000 Chinese high school students found that for students using AI, time spent on homework dropped and homework results improved. However, results on monthly tests and exams fell sharply. The largest declines were among students whose homework patterns suggested they were relying on AI to do the work.

The declining results were concentrated in about 80 percent of students, meaning a fifth of students were benefiting from AI use, suggesting there could be optimal ways to use the technology.

Libby Hills, who advises the British government on educational policy around AI as chief executive officer of EdTechnical, an edtech and AI research firm, told Newsweek that the debate around AI in the classroom is "polarized" as people draw on conflicting data to claim that AI either helps or hinders childhood learning.

For many, digital devices, screens, and now AI in the classroom are deeply troubling.

"We're seeing declines in this generation over prior," Kristen Mansharamani, founder of Torit Montessori School in Boston told Newsweek. "Concentration is the biggest piece that's been lost. The ability to concentrate and work steadily through a problem or a piece of work."

For Mansharamani, AI tutoring's main claim, that it can offer personalized tutoring at scale, is not unique to AI.

"The individualization of education doesn't require that students learn via AI," she said. "We can help teachers create more time to individualize…leaving intact all of what we know about the science of learning and brain development."

On the surface, Alpha School's results look impressive. Alpha claims they are "statistically exceptional," placing it in the top 1 percent of U.S. schools for academic achievement.

However, the numbers have not been independently verified and could also reflect a selection bias given the school's hefty fees that reach up to $75,000.

"The numbers look impressive on the surface, but they're self-reported and cover part of a school year, and the comparisons only adjust for pupils' scores at the start of the autumn term," said Hills.

"I do think there's something interesting in what Alpha is trying, but the evidence hasn't caught up with the claims yet."
