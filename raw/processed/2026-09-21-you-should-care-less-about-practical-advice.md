# You Should Care Less About Practical Advice

Young argues that despite readers gravitating toward practical tips, focusing on **fundamental principles** is more efficient than consuming actionable advice.

## Core Argument: The Tree of Knowledge

He uses a tree metaphor:

- **Roots/trunk** = fundamental principles (broadly applicable, context-independent)
- **Branches** = domain-specific subdivisions
- **Leaves** = specific tactical advice (narrowly applicable)

Since "there are far more leaves than trunks," learning at the leaf level requires absorbing enormous amounts of advice to cover many situations. Principles, by contrast, cover vast territory with less learning.

## Examples

| Domain | Leaves | Trunk |
|---|---|---|
| Fitness | A specific workout plan | Energy metabolism, muscle physiology |
| Business | "Buy Facebook ads" | Supply/demand, product-market fit |
| Learning | Exam-specific tips | Spacing effect, retrieval practice |

## Objections Addressed

1. **Principles don't constrain decisions enough** — True, but they narrow the search space significantly
2. **Principles may not describe reality** — Valid in fuzzy domains; supplement with situational knowledge
3. **Deriving answers from principles is hard** — Fair, but breadth still makes them efficient

## Practical Takeaway

For a single book, a practical guide may suffice. But for ongoing learning, prioritize foundational texts — textbooks, unbiased primers — over tactical self-help. Build trunk knowledge first; leaf-level details become easier to acquire when needed.
