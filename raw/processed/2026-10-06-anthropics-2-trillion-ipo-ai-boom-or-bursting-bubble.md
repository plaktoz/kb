# Anthropic's $2 Trillion IPO: AI Boom or Bursting Bubble?

Fears of an AI bubble are again stirring amid reports that Anthropic is preparing to enter the market at a $2 trillion valuation.

The company behind Claude is set to tell investors its total addressable market is worth more than $30 trillion, a higher TAM than SpaceX identified, a valuation that has reportedly drawn scepticism on Wall Street.

Some observers believe Anthropic could be preparing to plough ahead with its IPO within weeks, despite rising doubts around the putative valuation. Analysis of leaked financials seen by Morningstar shows that Anthropic's revenue is dangerously exposed to customer spending decisions, with just two customers accounting for nearly 25 per cent of 2025's revenue.

As the outlet notes, a $2 trillion valuation would value the company at 435 times last year's total revenue, and while it is observed that the company is putting on rapid growth, it does not, in the view of the analysts, do enough to justify what would be the biggest IPO ever seen.

It looks increasingly clear that AI, as a technology, is here to stay. The recent White House AI safety summit, and the Trump administration's subsequent appointment of its national intelligence director to oversee the technology, demonstrates that both industry leaders and political leaders believe the technology could be a danger – which is perhaps the best evidence an AI bull could point to in support of the technology's potential.

But it does not follow that every exuberant valuation can survive in the absence of fundamentals, or that every exciting new project will take off without providing real, tangible results. There's every possibility that AI as a technology proves to be a revolution, but that not all the pioneers see it through to the end.

This week, the CEO of the deVere Group, Nigel Green, urged investors to show caution around the Anthropic IPO, noting that the enormous valuation comes in spite of the fact that the firm's compute spend is now "wiping out revenue, and they're burning billions."

The finance chief said the plans provoked comparison with the dotcom bubble, and the run-up to the financial crisis, warning investors that "Historically, we have seen that when there are record IPOs, the market often falls afterwards.

"That's because it can be a sign that the market is overpricing. Now, if you're behind the IPO, you might want to go when there is an overprice, but there are risks for Anthropic too.

"Many companies have pulled their planned IPOs because US government debt is now paying 5.3 per cent, meaning capital is increasingly being drawn toward what is increasingly being seen as a secure but steady return, rather than a speculative investment.

"Would you rather have a fixed guaranteed return, or take on the additional risk? Right now, many investors are opting for the former."

## Big Short Investor Michael Burry Says AI Bubble Could Burst Sooner Rather Than Later

Michael Bury, the investor of Big Short fame who was memorably played in the 2015 film by Christian Bale, has doubled down on his AI bearishness, claiming that the 'bubble' may boost sooner than he previously thought.

While Bury remains on the bearish fringe of opinion – he is famed for being well ahead of the 2007 financial crisis, and even is his doomsday scenario doesn't come to fruition, he has made keen observations about the structural shortcomings in the sector and the stability of some aspects of its growth.

In his most recent investment newsletter, Bury said he was moving his timeline forward for a bubble event, writing that "[T]he bubble in AI may burst sooner rather than later…As such, I want more leverage in my short positions. Better timelines make leverage more palatable. Nothing says leverage like options, in this case put options, which are relatively cheap due to exceptionally tight volatility measures such as the VIX."

Bury said that AI firms were arriving at valuations on the basis of unproven and precarious revenue streams, and that a single disappointing revenue event could undermine capital expenditure plans – plans which help to secure growth and underwrite forward projections.

The warning comes just days after the Bank of England governor, Andrew Bailey, gave a not-so-oblique signal that he believes there will be some major losers in the AI arms race, and that the fallout from failure could rebound on markets, telling the BBC that "not everybody always wins".

Asked about the potential for an AI bubble event, the bank boss told the outlet that "You could see some correction of asset prices at some point," adding:

"There is a large, very large, amount of investment going into this sector now, and of course that's natural because it's a major area of growth…"And of course you see that the asset prices of the companies that are developing it have gone up a lot, and that reflects the fact that there are high expectations of what it can deliver.

"Everybody is currently priced to be a winner, [but] you look back at the past, not everybody is a winner".

While Bailey said that AI has "great potential" for economic growth, he said it posed risks, not just in terms of cybersecurity, but to the security and stability of markets in the event that AI cap-ex fails to generate the expected revenues.

## AI Faces Regulatory Risk

Right now, AI is steaming ahead, continuing to advance at a pace that reportedly beats the best expectations of the labs from where it hails. The recent White House AI summit showed no signs of slowing that progress – despite some disquiet from some sector bosses who fear what the technology might produce without proper oversight and scrutiny.

But should government posture change, it has the potential to slow development, delay advancement, and potentially materially alter the trajectory not just of the technology but of the companies pioneering it. We can see that in the EU, where AI faces an enormous regulatory burden, the technology hardly exists. AI labs have flocked to America, in search of a more lax regime, so that they can develop their products with a greater degree of freedom. However, the freedom they are afforded can change subject not only to the outcomes of elections, but on the whims of incumbents.

Governments, particularly large governments, can also pose a risk by the manner in which they engage, or decline to engage, with various AI models and their respective parent companies. Anthropic, which is now reportedly eyeing a $2 trillion IPO, has complained that US government attitudes toward it could have broad implications for its business.

The warning, contained within Anthropic's IPO prospectus, which was seen by Reuters, states that "The company may experience material revenue losses or business disruptions" attributable to events such as President Trump's order that the DOD refrain from using Anthropic models, back in February, after the company was designated as a supply-chain risk.

## Are AI Companies Overpriced?

For all the warning signs, the AI bulls can point to strong numbers and rapid growth. Anthropic's revenue grew 14-fold year on year to $11.5 billion in the second quarter, when it also turned an adjusted operating profit, and it has told shareholders to expect another in the third quarter, the Financial Times reported.

SemiAnalysis's Joey Brookhart told the paper that investors expect annualised revenue to reach $120 billion by the end of the year. Measured against that run-rate, a $2 trillion valuation works out at around 17 times revenue, a fraction of the 435 times implied by last year's accounts. "I think there's going to be a very strong appetite and demand for Anthropic," CFRA Research analyst Angelo Zino told CNN.

That said, the IPO prospectus shows a net loss of $42 billion for 2025, most of it a non-cash accounting charge, alongside an operating loss of more than $8 billion and $518 billion of future computing and infrastructure commitments. The Bank of England's Financial Policy Committee warned last week that AI valuations remain vulnerable to a "sharper correction" than July's, noting that growth prospects and public finances now lean partly on AI delivering productivity gains, according to Bloomberg.

The last record-breaking listing offers a mixed precedent. SpaceX shares fell more than 50 per cent from their post-IPO high within seven weeks of June's debut, CNBC reported, though Reuters noted last week that they have since recovered above their $135 offer price. But there are some suggestions the party might be nearing its end for headline grabbing pricey IPOs in this cycle.

The Federal Reserve raised rates last month for the first time since 2023 to tackle inflation fuelled by the war with Iran, and the 10-year Treasury yield hit its highest level since 2002 last week. Invesco's Paul Jackson told Reuters that yields above 5 per cent allow investors to lock in the best returns on US government debt since 2007, and that he has been trimming stocks and switching some money into bonds and bond funds are drawing fresh cash at the fastest rate in years.

Anthropic could start marketing its shares as soon as the week of 9 November, with trading pencilled in before Thanksgiving, Bloomberg reported last week. Its reception will show how much investors are prepared to pay for AI's promise while US government debt is paying them more than 5 per cent to wait.
