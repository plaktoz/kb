# Learning never retires

source_url: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1137549

---

University of California, Riverside — Feature Story, July 24, 2026

Rachel Wu, a UC Riverside psychology professor and developmental cognitive neuroscientist, is learning to ice skate alongside her oldest daughter — part of a lifelong commitment to becoming a beginner again and again that shapes both her personal life and her research.

Wu studies how people learn and pay attention across their lifespan, exploring how lessons from child development can help older adults continue learning and improve cognitive health and independence as they age. She has found that when older adults are placed in supportive learning environments — the kind children often experience — they can significantly improve cognitive abilities while mastering practical skills such as using new technology.

Every few years she deliberately learns something new: drawing, painting, singing, piano, German, swimming, crochet, weightlifting, sourdough baking, and now ice skating, with hula dancing next. Each skill has had a purpose — she learned weightlifting to safely carry future children, swimming to swim with them, crochet to make baby clothes.

Her research grew partly from her own experience: during graduate school at Birkbeck, University of London, she took art classes and was told she was wasting time that should go to her doctoral studies; when she improved, people attributed it to natural talent rather than practice. The same happened later with piano and singing. "They didn't want to believe it was the effort," she said.

Wu's honors include an NSF CAREER Award, the Presidential Early Career Award for Scientists and Engineers (PECASE), and fellowship in the American Psychological Association.

Two of adults' biggest misconceptions about learning, per Wu: that it should happen quickly ("we give babies one year to say one word... adults expect miracles"), and that mistakes signal failure — in reality, "the people who make the most mistakes are often the ones who learn the fastest."

Older adults who continue learning new skills tend to report better mental health, greater resilience, and lower loneliness and depression than those who stop learning, with benefits accumulating over time as people become more adaptable and confident. "Perhaps the biggest obstacle isn't age at all — it's believing that it's too late to learn," Wu said. That confidence extends beyond hobbies to adapting to new technology, changing healthcare systems, and everyday challenges requiring continual learning to remain independent.
