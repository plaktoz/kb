# Nvidia's Risky Business

On January 1, 1870, Jay Cooke, hailed as an American hero for his role in financing the Union effort in the Civil War, signed a contract that would, if you squint, lead to world war. Northern Pacific Railway had struggled to secure financing until it offered Cooke a commission of 12 percent on every bond, and $200 of Northern Pacific stock for every $1,000 in bonds he sold. Cooke leaned on appeals to patriotism, control of the media, and promises of railroad fortunes to sell bonds to retail investors at industrial scale — until, in September 1873, credit tightened worldwide and he could find no more buyers. The bankruptcy of Jay Cooke & Company triggered the Panic of 1873, culminating in railroad bankruptcies across the country and a multi-year depression.

## Blowing Through Debt

Liaquat Ahamed's new book *1873* draws a link between the collapse of the railroad buildout and the current AI moment: the $500 million that went into U.S. railway bonds annually during the boom years of the early 1870s would today be the equivalent of $600 billion, roughly what is projected to be invested by major tech companies in 2026.

Microsoft CEO Satya Nadella cited *1873* as "the book to be read" on the company's recent earnings call. Microsoft, alone amongst the hyperscalers, still boasts substantial free cash flow — $19.6 billion last quarter — and is the one hyperscaler still funding its capex without debt.

That was, believe it or not, a defense that could be used for nearly all of Big Tech a year ago; then, between September and November, Oracle, Meta, Alphabet, and Amazon issued a combined $80 billion in debt for building out infrastructure. After raising a combined $108 billion in all of 2025, these four companies have, as of July 7, already raised $194 billion this year. Spreads are rising, and 86% of the bonds issued this year are already trading at higher yields than at issuance. Cover for recent issuance has fallen to less than 2x, from 5x in February.

The real shock came at the beginning of June, when Google announced it would raise $85 billion in equity, including a special $10 billion issuance to Berkshire Hathaway.

## DeepMind Drama

Google right now is no one's bet, at least in terms of the AI frontier. After the departure of DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis (technically promoted to chairman) and Gemini co-lead and former Chief Scientist Jeff Dean, along with a host of other prominent researchers, SemiAnalysis declared that Gemini is Cooked, arguing DeepMind is no longer a frontier lab and that the biggest beneficiary is neither Anthropic nor OpenAI, but Google Cloud.

## Google's Infrastructure Bet

Google Cloud grew 82% year-over-year last quarter (compared to 63% the quarter before, and 32% a year ago), with 36% margins. More than 20% of total TPU shipments from 3Q26 to 4Q27 are being sold directly to Anthropic, excluding the hundreds of thousands of TPUs GCP already rents to Anthropic today. Whether or not Google is competing for the AI frontier, it is absolutely competing to dominate AI infrastructure — and in a world where intelligence is a commodity, TPUs in particular are a big deal.

## Nvidia's Investable Asset Class

Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang announced partnerships with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to establish independent financing platforms designed to mobilize over $500 billion of third-party capital to support the buildout of AI infrastructure over time. Huang argues Nvidia-based AI factories are fungible, productive infrastructure assets that can be financed with repeatable platforms and long-term institutional capital.

The company is backstopping opportunities with up to 25% residual-value based financing — in effect a price cut, reducing the cost of capital for entities building data centers with Nvidia chips by putting Nvidia's own profits on the line. That guarantee is downstream of Google's (and soon Amazon's) aggressiveness: why build a data center with Nvidia chips if you can buy TPUs or Trainiums instead. Nvidia's bigger problem: Anthropic has not been dependent on CUDA for years, and OpenAI is moving in that direction too, at least for inference.

## Risky Business

This might not cost Nvidia anything in the end: if AI revenues truly take off, the debt markets will open back up, and companies will go back to funding infrastructure investment through free cash flows. Right now, however, is the danger zone, as hyperscalers blow through the debt markets and Google starts to tap equity. To the extent Nvidia competes through novel funding mechanisms that draw on things like insurance floats and pension funds — the bread and butter of the asset managers the company is partnering with — the risk, unmarked unlike equity, is considerably higher. It's one thing to spend all of your free cash flow; it's another to tap the debt markets; and it's a completely new, nerve-racking thing to bring safety-seeking assets to bear. AI better deliver before it's too late.
