# Poor sleep can cost over-50s nine months of career, study finds

Sleep that is extremely disrupted or that goes on too long could mean a loss of nine months from working life after the age of 50, a study has found. It also found that women with no sleep disturbances had a longer working life from 50 to 68 than their male equivalents.

Compared with participants who had no sleep disturbances, those with moderate sleep disturbances could expect to work nearly five fewer months within that age range, and those with severe sleep disturbances eight fewer months, regardless of gender or job type. The research, conducted by a team at the University of Turku in Finland, used data from the Finnish Public Sector study and the Health and Social Support study, surveying participants' sleeping habits after age 50.

Nightly sleep length was categorized as short (under 7 hours), mid-range (7-8.5 hours), or long (9+ hours); sleep disturbances were defined as difficulty falling or staying asleep, waking too early, and nonrestorative sleep in the prior four weeks. Short and mid-range sleep length were both associated with a working life expectancy of 13 years 2 months after 50, while long sleep length was associated with 12 years 5 months. The longest working life expectancy, almost 14 years, was among professional women with no sleep disturbances; the shortest, nearly 12.5 years, was among men in routine jobs with severe sleep disturbances.

Lead investigator Dr. Saana Myllyntausta of the University of Turku said it was surprising to find no difference between short and mid-range (optimal, 7-9 hour) sleep duration. Long sleep duration was linked to shorter working life expectancy and has also been linked to poorer health outcomes, which might explain the shorter working career. The findings highlight the importance of supporting sleep health and treating sleep problems, particularly among those with lower socioeconomic status, during middle age — including cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia or workplace educational interventions. The researchers noted they could not account for other influential factors such as physical/mental health, underlying conditions, risky health behaviors, or shift work.
