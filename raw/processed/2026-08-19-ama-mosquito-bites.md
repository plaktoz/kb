# What Doctors Want Patients to Know About Mosquito Bites

Mosquito bites are among the most universal human experiences, but the itch and bump they leave behind are the result of an immune system response. Donald Lei, MD, MS, a dermatologist at Rush University System for Health, explains what patients should know.

Bites itch because of an inflammatory response to proteins in mosquito saliva — the immune system recognizing something foreign and reacting to it, partly via the histamine pathway. Only female mosquitoes bite humans, feeding on blood protein for egg development; males feed on plant nectar. Mosquitoes breed in even small amounts of standing water, such as flowerpots, which is why they persist in dense cities.

Reactions range from a small raised bump (a hive or "wheal" appearing within 30 minutes) to Skeeter syndrome — large areas of swelling, warmth and redness, sometimes with mild fever. Rarer still is severe mosquito bite allergy (SMBA), which can cause high fever, fatigue, swollen lymph nodes, and bites that turn dusky or black, blister, or scab.

Most bites need no treatment, but cold compresses, over-the-counter anti-itch creams, and nonsedating antihistamines (cetirizine, fexofenadine) can help. The most effective repellents contain DEET, picaridin or permethrin; DEET and picaridin are for those over 2 months old, while infants should be protected only with physical barriers like long clothing and netting. For 2026, West Nile virus season started early, with the highest infection count at this point in the year since 2004, per the CDC.

Seek medical evaluation for spreading redness or warmth after 24-48 hours, pus, duskiness or black crusting, blisters, widespread rash, swollen lymph nodes, fever, headaches, confusion, or muscle/joint pain — and seek immediate care for hives away from the bite site, difficulty breathing or swallowing, or swelling of the face, lips or tongue.
