# New endometriosis study finds 27 fresh clues about the painful disease affecting 1 in 10 women

A massive new study has provided researchers with new data that could pave the way for better treatments for endometriosis, a painful condition affecting 1 in 10 women.

## The Brief

A large international study of over 1 million people from diverse racial backgrounds identified 58 DNA segments linked to endometriosis, including 27 newly discovered clues.

Researchers and National Institutes of Health officials noted that these findings could help guide future efforts to create better diagnostic tools, risk assessments, and treatments.

Affecting roughly 1 in 10 women, an estimated 190 million reproductive-age women worldwide, the chronic condition has no cure and causes tissue similar to the uterine lining to grow outside the uterus, leading to symptoms like severe pain, heavy bleeding, and infertility.

A large international study of over 1 million people is giving researchers new data that could help lead to better treatments of endometriosis, a condition that can lead to severe pain and fertility changes.

## Big picture view

The study, supported by the National Institutes of Health, looked at a wide variety of cases and possible genetic associations and risk factors that other diseases share. They also looked for possible genes associated with the condition.

The researchers detected 58 DNA segments linked to endometriosis, including 27 that had not been found before. That is in part because of the diversity of the study participants across multiple racial backgrounds.

The research detected that endometriosis shares genetic links with 135 other health conditions or traits and identified 314 genes that may be associated with it.

"Together, these findings offer a bigger picture of endometriosis than was previously available and could lead to studies looking at the function of the mechanisms driving the disease," a release from the National Institutes of Health states.

## What they're saying

Dr. John Gaitanis, the director of NIH's Eunice Kennedy Shriver National Institute of Child Health and Human Development, said the findings of the study are providing a "wealth of information on the genetic underpinnings" of the disease.

"A greater understanding will help us take steps toward developing diagnostic tools and treatments to address this devastating disease," Gaitanis said.

"By studying many types of biological data across people with different ancestries, we were able to increase our understanding of how endometriosis develops and affects the body," Shefali Setia-Verma, Ph.D., the senior author of the study, said.

Setia-Verma, who also serves as an assistant professor of Pathology and Laboratory Medicine at the University of Pennsylvania Perelman School of Medicine, said the findings "will help guide future research that could eventually lead to better diagnosis, personalized risk assessment, and treatments that target the disease."

## Why you should care

Endometriosis affects an estimated 190 million reproductive age women worldwide, the World Health Organization says. It is a chronic disease with symptoms that include infertility, severe pain during menstruation; heavy menstrual bleeding; chronic pelvic pain that does not go away when the menstrual cycle ends, and abdominal bloating and nausea.

In patients with endometriosis, tissue that is similar to the inner lining of the uterus grows outside the uterus, according to the Mayo Clinic. It often affects the ovaries, fallopian tubes and the tissue lining the pelvis. The growths can sometimes be found beyond the pelvic organs.

While there is currently no cure, health experts say access to early diagnosis and effective treatment of endometriosis is limited in many settings, something researchers hope the new data will help them be able to address.
