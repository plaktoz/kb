# The Dark Side of the Jevons Paradox

Newport opens by noting the resurgence of interest in the **Jevons Paradox**, an 1865 economic theory from William Stanley Jevons, who argued in his book on England's coal supply that making an energy source more efficient tends to increase overall consumption rather than reduce it, since new uses emerge. He notes this prediction proved true historically for coal use.

He explains why this idea has become relevant to AI discourse: many worry AI-driven efficiency will shrink the job market, but the Jevons Paradox suggests the opposite could happen — cheaper, faster output could expand overall demand for services like software development, potentially growing rather than shrinking employment in some fields. He points readers to a podcast interview (Derek Thompson and Alex Imas) for more on this economic angle.

Newport then pivots to what he calls the "darker side" of the paradox: efficiency gains can produce unintended negative consequences. He cites two historical examples:

1. Steam engine efficiency contributing to pollution and the origins of industrial climate impact.
2. Digital communication tools (email, Slack) making messaging so efficient that it fueled constant interruptions — citing a statistic that knowledge workers are now interrupted roughly every two minutes, according to Microsoft research he references.

He closes by expressing hope that the positive version of the Jevons Paradox holds for AI's effect on labor, while cautioning that efficiency improvements often carry unexpected costs, urging watchfulness rather than blind optimism.
