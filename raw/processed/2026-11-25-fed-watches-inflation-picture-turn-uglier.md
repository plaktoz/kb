# Fed Watches Inflation Picture Turn Uglier

In late October, Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh said policymakers wanted more information before agreeing to raise interest rates again. At least some of what they've seen hasn't been pretty.

Yes, the rise in the Consumer Price Index (CPI) decelerated from 0.6% month over month in September to 0.4% in October. But energy accounted for 58% of September's rise, while in October it accounted for only 15%, making energy a relatively minor factor in the latest reading. Still, October's gain works out to annualized inflation of about 4.3%.

Zooming out also reveals a less-than-attractive picture. Over the three months through October, CPI rose an annualized 5.7%, while the wholesale Producer Price Index (PPI) for final demand rose an annualized 9%. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, rose at a 3.7% annualized rate during the same period. That's more than double the 1.6% pace it set in the three months through July.

Personal Consumption Expenditures (PCE) price data for October is due November 25. PCE is the Fed's preferred inflation gauge. But Fed Governor Christopher J. Waller didn't wait for it. Hours before the CPI release on November 10, he said that if October CPI rose 0.3% or more, the Fed should consider raising rates by a half percentage point at its December meeting to maintain its inflation-fighting credibility. Waller, who voted to hold rates steady in October, noted particular concern about the breadth of price increases.

## Inflation spreads?

Inflation appears to be spreading. In October, 105 of 177 CPI expenditure categories (59%) rose faster than a 3% annualized rate. That equals the highest level since August 2022, up from 49% in September and 46% in August. In all, 27 categories rose the most in at least a year, including household furnishings and supplies (up 0.7% month over month), motor vehicle insurance (up 0.7%), and club memberships and sports fees (up 1.2%).

Several regional Fed gauges that track the underlying inflation trend have turned up again, reaching the highest levels since May. The Federal Reserve Bank of Atlanta's core sticky-price index, which excludes food and energy and tracks slower-moving items, rose at a one-month annualized rate of 4.7% in October.

(The Dallas Fed's trimmed-mean PCE hasn't been updated for October because the PCE data hasn't been released. The 12-month rate ticked up to 2.3% in September, while its one-month annualized rate surged to 2.8%, the highest since May.)

## Energy "look-through"

While energy was a relatively minor factor in October, it may pose a lingering threat.

Fed policymakers often try to "look through" energy prices, which are volatile and typically have a minimal, short-lived effect on longer-term inflation trends. But in a November 10 speech, Waller cited Fed staff research indicating that energy prices may drive goods and services prices for longer than they did in the pre-pandemic era, possibly well into 2027, especially if oil prices remain elevated.

Oil and gasoline prices have fallen somewhat, and that'll help. But global refinery constraints have caused diesel prices to diverge somewhat from oil prices, and diesel feeds into inflation via more channels than gasoline does. Diesel powers the trucks that deliver goods and the farm machinery used to grow food. As of November 16, diesel prices were down only 9% from their record high set in September, compared to a 22% decline for WTI crude oil from its October high.

October's PPI data showed that truck freight rates rose 3.9% from a month earlier and 25% from a year earlier, while wholesale food prices rose 1.3% from a month earlier, the fastest increase since February.

## Bottom line

Fed policymakers should have plenty to discuss at the December 8–9 meeting. But as of November 18, the most recent round of data has the market pricing in a 100% chance of a rate hike, including a 9.6% chance of a half-percentage-point increase, according to the CME FedWatch Tool. PCE data due November 25 could shed new light on price gains, but most forecasts call for core PCE to rise about 0.3% month over month. Any upside surprise could add pressure on the Fed to take more aggressive action, potentially rattling markets.
