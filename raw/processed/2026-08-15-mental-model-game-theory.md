# Mental Model: Game Theory

From Morton Davis's *Game Theory*: The theory of games is a theory of decision making — how one should make decisions and, to a lesser extent, how one does. When your options and their consequences are known, the solution is easy (e.g., picking a floor button in an elevator). Game theory applies to more complex situations where "decision makers are trying to manipulate their environment, [while] their environment is trying to manipulate them" — e.g., a store owner who lowers prices must anticipate competitors reacting in kind. Because everyone's strategy affects the outcome, each player must worry about what everyone else does, knowing everyone else is worrying about them too.

**What is a game?** From *Game Theory and Strategy*: a situation of conflict/cooperation where (1) there are at least two players (individuals, companies, nations, even species), (2) each has a number of possible strategies, (3) the strategies chosen determine the outcome, and (4) each outcome carries a numerical payoff for each player. Game theory studies how players should rationally play to maximize their own payoff.

**Strategic situations**: Per Greg Mankiw's economics textbook, game theory applies when each person, in deciding actions, must consider how others will respond. It's essential for understanding oligopolies (few sellers) where each firm's profit depends on rivals' output — but unnecessary for pure competition (too many small firms to matter) or monopoly (no rivals). The classic example is the prisoners' dilemma. In oligopoly, firms are collectively best off cooperating like a monopolist, but individual self-interest creates strong incentives to defect from that cooperation.

**Interdependent choices**: Avinash Dixit and Barry Nalebuff (*Thinking Strategically*): "Everyone's best choice depends on what others are going to do, whether it's going to war or maneuvering in a traffic jam" — such situations require looking at the whole system of interaction rather than simple summation.

**Business application**: Michael Mauboussin relates game theory to firm interaction and sustainable value creation — it forces managers to view competitive situations from rivals' perspectives rather than only their own, and to consider how firms co-evolve, not just compete head-on.
