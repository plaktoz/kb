# AI Schools: What Public Funding Should Reveal

Texas payments to an AI-centered sports academy raise questions about how families can verify learning claims, teaching responsibilities, and student support.

AI schools receiving public funds should show how students learn, who provides academic help, and what happens when the model falls short.

AI schools moved deeper into Texas’s school-choice debate when The Texas Tribune reported on November 21 that Texas Sports Academy, connected to Alpha School, had received $6.5 million through the state’s education savings account program as of October 30. Citing the state comptroller’s dashboard, the Tribune identified the academy as the largest school recipient at that point.

The amount reflects payments through that date, rather than a full school year. Even so, it brings a practical question into focus: What can families and taxpayers verify about the education those dollars support?

For a family drawn to a school that promises to complete core academics in two hours, the appeal could be more time for athletics, projects, or individual interests. The enrollment decision still depends on what happens during those academic hours—and when a child needs something different.

edCircuit’s guide to school vouchers examines how private-school choice programs work. AI-centered instruction adds another layer to that decision: whether a school’s evidence supports its promises.

## What Public Funding Confirms About AI Schools

Texas Education Freedom Accounts, known as TEFA, help eligible families pay approved educational expenses. The program’s school guidance lists accreditation and operating-history requirements. Participating private schools must also administer an annual nationally norm-referenced assessment to participating students in grades 3–12.

Approval establishes eligibility to participate. It does not establish that a particular use of AI improves learning.

Texas does require information about results. Under the law establishing TEFA, assessment results must reach parents and the program’s certified educational assistance organizations. The law also calls for an annual longitudinal report covering assessment results, participant satisfaction, and other measures, with methodology disclosed subject to student privacy protections and review by the state auditor.

Those obligations provide a basis for scrutiny. Families evaluating a school’s claim of faster learning still need to see how that claim was calculated and whether the findings apply to the campus their child would attend.

## Who Helps When the Software Cannot?

The term “AI schools” says little about the division of work between software and adults. A program might use technology to select practice questions, explain material, generate feedback, or decide when a student advances. Each arrangement gives adults different responsibilities.

In Alpha’s description of its model, students attend a full school day and spend about two hours on software-based core academics. The remaining time includes collaborative activities, physical activity, and life skills. Adults called guides concentrate on mentoring, motivation, and coaching.

That description explains the intended structure. A closer look should reveal how academic help works within it.

Consider a student who repeatedly answers fraction questions incorrectly. More practice might help, or the student might need someone to identify a misunderstanding and explain the concept another way. The school should be able to identify who makes that judgment and what expertise that person brings.

A guide’s ability to encourage a frustrated child matters. So does access to someone who can diagnose an instructional problem. Staffing information becomes useful when it connects qualifications to actual responsibilities, including reading intervention and subject-specific help.

An observed academic session and an anonymized intervention example would give prospective families more to work with than a job title alone.

The daily schedule should show where students discuss ideas, receive feedback on writing, and work through open-ended problems with adults. For an online student, the same explanation should specify whether help is live, how it is requested, and how long a student may wait. These details make the staffing model easier to assess.

## How AI Schools Should Explain Learning Gains

The 2 Hour Learning results page promotes high national assessment rankings and identifies Alpha School MAP results from spring 2023 and fall 2024 as a basis for its presentation. Understanding those figures requires knowing which students, campuses, and subjects they cover.

High achievement and rapid growth describe different things. A student can enter a school already performing well above national averages. A student beginning further behind might make substantial progress while still scoring below those averages. Neither result, by itself, establishes how much improvement the school’s model caused.

NWEA’s explanation of academic growth cautions against treating a “year’s growth” as a fixed amount. Typical growth varies with age, starting achievement, and the time between assessments.

A claim of accelerated learning needs enough detail to follow the calculation: starting and ending scores, testing dates, the number of students included, and the benchmark used. A test developed by an outside organization can provide valuable information. Independent review of the school’s analysis is a separate step.

The students included in that analysis matter just as much as the assessment. Results limited to children who completed a full year describe the experience of those who stayed. They leave open what happened to students who withdrew, transferred, or missed testing.

For that reason, an evaluation should account for everyone who enrolled and explain missing results. It should also distinguish established campuses from newer locations. Evidence from one school can inform expectations elsewhere, but readers need to know where it originated.

A credible comparison group would further strengthen the analysis, particularly if it accounts for differences in students’ starting points. Without that context, strong results can warrant further investigation while leaving the model’s contribution uncertain.

Families can also look beyond test scores. Essays written without automated assistance, mathematical explanations, and science investigations offer opportunities to examine whether students can apply their learning when software hints are removed.

## Student Support Must Be Clear Before Enrollment

The limits of an instructional model become especially important when a child struggles. A school’s support plan should explain how staff respond to stalled progress, anxiety, attention difficulties, or a need for disability-related services.

Promises of personalized learning need practical detail. That includes available specialists, the frequency of services, additional charges, and how staff decide that a student needs more academic time than the advertised schedule allows.

For students with disabilities, private enrollment also changes the legal setting. Federal regulations state that children placed in private schools by their parents have no individual right to the special education and related services they would receive in public school. Some may receive services through a services plan. Families need to verify those arrangements rather than assume an existing individualized education program will transfer intact.

Written commitments allow parents to compare the school’s offer with their child’s needs. The enrollment agreement should also make remaining tuition, required technology fees, withdrawal deadlines, and refund terms understandable.

If the placement does not work, families need access to records and a clear path to another setting. Their experience belongs in any serious examination of the model, alongside testimonials from students who thrive.

## Public Dollars Deserve Evidence Families Can Use

The Texas comptroller’s dashboard page describes the released information as a first-year snapshot and explains how small student counts are masked, rounded, or combined to protect privacy.

Public reporting can respect those protections while making financial records and aggregate outcomes understandable. Clear reporting dates and definitions help readers interpret the figures. State officials should also distinguish findings about the choice program overall from evidence about a specific instructional approach.

School boards and families following these developments need to know where required reports will appear and how to raise concerns when advertised services differ from what students receive.

A statewide average will offer limited guidance to a parent choosing between individual schools. Public reports should explain their level of detail and the questions their data can reasonably answer.

Schools seeking public support for AI-centered instruction should make their strongest claims checkable. Families deserve an explanation of how progress is measured, whose results are included, and who takes responsibility when a child falls behind.
