# AI Careers Weekly: What Six New Reports Say About Hiring

The dominant careers signal last week came from two institutions that rarely weigh in together: the IMF and the US Federal Reserve both reported that AI is already reshaping where employers are hiring. The IMF’s new Regional Economic Outlook for Asia and Pacific found demand falling in the region’s more AI-exposed occupations, while the Fed’s October Beige Book collected district-level accounts of AI softening hiring even as employment kept growing.

A KPMG survey of chief executives pointed the other way, with most still planning to add workers. Oyster’s annual talent report showed AI skill requirements tripling in job postings since 2022, and a Selby Jennings survey found a quarter of financial firms now tying pay and promotions to AI proficiency.

ManpowerGroup’s 2026 Talent Shortage research named AI skills the hardest-to-find talent category worldwide for the first time. Quick Hits cover a Wisconsin training program, TCS’s hiring plans, and a Microsoft warning on AI immigration policy, followed by the two ODSC events.

## Surveys and research

## IMF finds demand falling in Asia’s more AI-exposed jobs

What happened: The International Monetary Fund released its Regional Economic Outlook for Asia and Pacific on October 15 at the IMF and World Bank annual meetings in Bangkok, with a chapter on AI’s effect on the region’s labor markets. Drawing on roughly 40 million online job postings across 19 Asian economies, the analysis found that employer demand fell by nearly 10 percent in more AI-exposed occupations relative to less-exposed ones, an effect that appears to have stabilized even as AI capabilities have advanced. The report warned that AI could complicate job creation in younger economies while helping aging ones offset labor shortages, per the IMF report.

Why it matters: A measured drop in demand concentrated in exposed occupations, built from tens of millions of real postings rather than a sentiment survey, is one of the firmer pieces of evidence that AI is already changing hiring rather than only promising to. The finding that the effect has leveled off suggests the first wave of adjustment may have largely worked through, not that it is accelerating. For workers in the region, exposure is becoming a question of which specific tasks a role still owns.

## KPMG survey finds most CEOs still planning to add workers

What happened: KPMG published its 2026 US CEO Outlook on October 15, surveying more than 1,300 chief executives at large companies worldwide, including 400 in the United States. Eighty-nine percent said they expect to increase headcount over the next three years, 79 percent said they are hiring for AI and technical skills, and 86 percent said they are redesigning roles for human and AI collaboration, even as 53 percent anticipated workforce reductions in some areas over the longer term and 75 percent said AI has changed the skills they require for entry-level roles, according to the survey release.

Why it matters: Executives describing AI as a reason to hire rather than shed workers is a counterweight to the demand softening the IMF and the Federal Reserve recorded, and the gap between the two may come down to timing and to who is being asked. The same survey’s finding that three-quarters of CEOs have changed entry-level skill requirements is the more concrete signal for job seekers, since it reshapes what a first job asks for. The headcount optimism sits alongside a quiet reset of the bottom rung.

## Worker sentiment and workplace policy

## The Federal Reserve’s Beige Book records AI damping hiring in several districts

What happened: The Federal Reserve released its October Beige Book on October 14, and business contacts in several of its 12 districts tied slower hiring to AI. Accounts from the Dallas, Cleveland, and Minneapolis districts described firms delaying entry-level hiring, trimming staffing needs through automation, and slowing new job creation, while the Philadelphia district linked some manufacturing growth to data center and AI construction. Overall district employment still rose modestly, and the Beige Book draws on anecdotal reports from business contacts rather than a count of affected jobs, per the October 2026 Beige Book (summarized by 24/7 Wall St.).

Why it matters: The Beige Book is qualitative, but it captures what employers are telling the central bank in close to real time, and this edition is among the first to carry AI as a recurring explanation for hiring decisions across multiple districts. The pattern it describes, slower hiring rather than outright cuts, matches the IMF’s demand finding and fits awkwardly with the cleaner story that AI either destroys jobs or does not. For US practitioners, it is a sign that the hiring slowdown in exposed roles is being named out loud by the firms doing it.

## Selby Jennings finds financial firms tying pay to AI proficiency

What happened: Recruiting firm Selby Jennings released its AI Talent Pulse Report for financial services around October 14, based on a survey of 500 HR professionals in US finance conducted in late August. A quarter of respondents said their organization has tied raises, promotions, and bonuses to AI proficiency, and 30 percent said they have created new roles or departments dedicated to proving AI’s return on investment, with AI fluency rising above traditional credentials in hiring decisions. The findings appear in the AI Talent Pulse Report and were first reported by Reuters.

Why it matters: Formal compensation tied to AI use moves the skills premium from the hiring stage into the annual review, where it reaches people already in their jobs rather than only new candidates. The concentration at fintechs and smaller firms, ahead of the largest banks, suggests the practice is spreading from the edges inward. For finance workers, demonstrated AI use is starting to carry the weight a credential or pedigree once did.

## Skills demand and the wage premium

## Oyster reports AI skill requirements tripling in job postings

What happened: Oyster, a global employment platform, published its 2026 Global AI Talent Report on October 14, drawing on about 15,000 job descriptions posted through its platform between 2022 and mid-2026 plus a survey of more than 1,000 HR and people leaders across the US, UK, Canada, and Europe. The share of those postings explicitly requiring AI skills rose from about 5 percent in 2022 to 15 percent in the first half of 2026, roughly tripling, and the report found that 95 percent of HR leaders say employees are already doing AI work their formal job descriptions never named. Companies reported building AI capability mostly through internal training, at 53 percent, rather than external hiring, at 14 percent, per Oyster’s research.

Why it matters: A tripling of explicit AI requirements in four years, measured against the same platform’s own postings, lines up with the demand shifts the IMF and others recorded and puts a number on how fast the baseline is moving. The gap between formal job descriptions and the AI work people are actually doing means much of the reskilling is happening informally, off the org chart. The tilt toward internal training over hiring suggests employers are trying to grow capability they cannot reliably buy.

## ManpowerGroup names AI skills the hardest-to-find talent category worldwide

What happened: ManpowerGroup‘s 2026 Talent Shortage research, covering employers across dozens of countries, found AI skills displacing IT skills at the top of the hardest-to-fill list for the first time. This appeared in the October 18 daily sweep under the same “Agent Fluency” careers story that carried the Robert Half and Oyster wage-premium findings, but the ManpowerGroup figure itself was not in the body of last week’s draft or its exclusion notes.

Why it matters: This is a structural complement to the demand-side findings above: it is one thing for employers to say they want AI skills, another for a global staffing firm to find that most of them cannot reliably evaluate candidates on those skills yet. That gap is an opening for candidates who can demonstrate agent-level competence concretely, since the bar many evaluators are applying is not calibrated to the skill they’re screening for.

## Quick hits

## Reskilling and upskilling

Wisconsin committed $6.6 million to train more than 3,800 workers in advanced manufacturing and AI through its WisTRAIN grant program, announced by Governor Tony Evers and the state Department of Workforce Development on October 14.

## Roles and hiring

Tata Consultancy Services said it expects 80 percent of its new hires to carry digital and AI skills, with its chief operating officer noting that 25 to 30 percent of the quarter’s hires were specialized AI talent, in comments around the firm’s second-quarter results on October 9.

## Worker policy

Microsoft President Brad Smith warned that tighter US immigration rules could push AI talent abroad, citing green card backlogs and H-1B restrictions as a threat to the country’s ability to attract top AI workers.
