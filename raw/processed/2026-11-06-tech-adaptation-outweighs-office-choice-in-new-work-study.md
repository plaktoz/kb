# Tech Adaptation Outweighs Office Choice in New Work Study

AI is driving individual efficiency across the workforce, yet it is failing to translate into meaningful gains for collective organisational performance.

According to findings from the 2026 Workstyle Monitor, sponsored by audio and video equipment provider Jabra, technology-related factors currently explain 13.5% of the variation in individual productivity, compared with just 8.9% in team productivity.

Furthermore, AI adoption is reshaping workflows rather than drastically lifting output: AI use shows a small positive correlation with workers finding better ways of working (r=.23), while its relationship with individual productivity (r=.17) and team productivity (r=.16) remains markedly smaller.

Efficiency gains alone are not enough. While AI tools expand worker capacity, the research reveals that business benefits depend entirely on giving employees the autonomy to decide where that saved capacity goes – such as into deeper thinking, learning or problem-solving – rather than using it merely to churn through higher volumes of administrative work.

## Inside the Workstyle Monitor

The Workstyle Monitor is a multi-year longitudinal research programme delivered by Workstyle CIC and King's College London.

Designed to investigate ways of working with a direct focus on autonomy, trust and technology, the study evaluates their collective impact on wellbeing, productivity, and workplace inclusion, aiming to answer "how we ensure organisations benefit rather than fall behind".

The 2026 data reflects findings from 1,058 respondents, predominantly based in the UK and weighted toward professional, managerial and experienced knowledge workers across various sectors. This first wave specifically tracks "the growing nature of AI-enabled individual capability and how connective capability can be used to harness this for the benefit of organisational capability".

"We find ourselves at the dawn of another period of significant change, with AI redefining the way people work once more," write Alex Hirst and Lizzie Penny, Founders of Workstyle CIC.

"Over the years ahead, we will track how technology, autonomy, trust and the ways peoples shape their work impact their wellbeing, productivity and feelings of inclusion. With so much change on the horizon, we are fascinated to see what the data reveals."

Professor Mike Clinton of King's College London adds: "This research has been designed around a model of working life that brings together factors that are too often considered separately: how people shape their jobs, their use of technology, their experience of autonomy, the trust and inclusion they experience at work, their wellbeing and their individual and team performance."

## The enterprise architecture gap: Moving beyond the 'super-individual'

The deployment of generative tooling has fundamentally altered employee workflows, compressing cross-functional collaboration into individual human-AI loops.

"We are moving towards a world in which individuals can increasingly work with AI as a coach, a colleague or an extension of their own expertise," explains Prithwiraj Choudhury, Professor at the London School of Economics.

"Someone can use AI to interrogate an idea, access knowledge outside their domain, analyse information, create alternatives and receive immediate feedback. Work that previously required several specialists – and multiple handovers between them – can increasingly be progressed by one person working alongside AI."

Prithwiraj warns, however, that technology leaders must rethink broader organisational architecture: "The organisations that thrive with AI may be those that achieve giving individuals greater power to act, while redesigning work so that this greater capability compounds when people come together."

Bridging this gap requires addressing what Jabra calls the loss of shared context across digital collaboration stacks.

"Individual capability is growing quickly thanks to AI," says Daniella Bell, Future of Work Research Lead at Jabra. "The harder question is what happens to that capability when people come together. At Jabra, we think one answer lies in something organisations rarely treat as a shared resource: context. Every team has it. The reasoning behind a decision. The caveat someone raised in a meeting. The customer insight that changed the direction of a project. The expertise sitting with one person that another colleague will need three weeks from now.

"Much of that context is created through conversation, in meetings, phone calls and instant messages… then disappears the moment the interaction ends. That creates a problem as AI becomes more embedded in work. After all, AI can only build on the context it has access to. If the important parts of a conversation stay in someone's memory, inbox or private notes, the next person, team or AI agent has to reconstruct them from scratch. The result is a ceiling on how much the gains AI creates for individuals can translate into gains for the team."

Daniella notes that tech ecosystems must move away from isolated surveillance and toward frictionless context capture: "Our view is that better teams start the moment we treat every conversation as an opportunity to create shared context. That means giving AI access to the context that usually gets lost: why a decision was made, what was ruled out, what still needs to happen and who knows what. It means creating records that travel with the work, rather than relying on the people who happened to be in the room. And it means designing meetings and digital interactions so that what matters can be captured clearly, accurately and effortlessly. The goal is not to record or monitor conversations, but rather to preserve the things that help someone else understand, decide or act."

## Collaboration and trust in the workplace stack

For IT buyers evaluating communications infrastructure and return-to-office policies, the research shows that physical location has "very little" impact on whether collaboration succeeds. Crucially, the balance between digital and face-to-face interactions demonstrates no relationship with reported team productivity.

Instead, software and tool reliability are closely tied to team culture: trust in workplace technology shows a moderate positive correlation with trust in teammates (r=.40).

Even after controlling for factors like team inclusion, relatedness and relational job crafting, confidence in workplace technology accounts for an additional 2.5% of the variation explained in overall team trust.

## Autonomy over location flexibility

When isolating factors driving employee vitality, the study found that autonomy stands apart. When computer-use intensity, location flexibility, desk-based working, asynchronous working, and autonomy are evaluated together, autonomy is the only factor that remains significantly associated with worker vitality.

Notably for hybrid workplace policies, simply getting to pick where you work does not drive an employee's sense of autonomy; the true driver is having control over how tasks are shaped and executed.

## Disparities across demographics

The report also reveals that aggregate employee sentiment hides critical variations across different demographic groups.

Neurodivergent and disabled workers maintain individual productivity levels almost identical to their neurotypical and non-disabled peers, but they report lower autonomy, inclusion, connection and trust in workplace technology. They also report lower vitality scores:

Disabled workers: 4.21 vs. 4.89 (non-disabled)

Neurodivergent workers: 4.12 vs. 4.82 (non-neurodivergent)

Women: 4.67 vs. 4.87 (men)

These figures indicate that comparable output is frequently achieved inside a significantly less supportive working environment.

Rather than designing tools for an assumed "average worker", the findings point to an opportunity for IT and HR leaders to leverage AI's adaptive capabilities – offering workers customised choices in how they ingest information, develop ideas and interact.

## Strategic priorities for leaders

The Workstyle Monitor outlines five strategic directives for leadership teams managing workplace transformation:

- Expand autonomy alongside AI capability
- Design work around deliverable and interdependency
- Protect the moments that improve collective judgement
- Equip managers to build connective capability
- Measure the links as well as the nodes

For technology and enterprise leaders, the message is unmistakable: unlocking the true return on AI investments will require shifting focus away from raw individual tool adoption and toward deliberate system design that pairs personal autonomy with shared connective capability.

## Research partners and methodology

The findings in this report stem from a cross-sector collaboration between academic researchers, future-of-work specialists, and enterprise technology providers:

Workstyle CIC: A Community Interest Company co-founded by authors and workplace researchers Alex Hirst and Lizzie Penny. Workstyle CIC focuses on evidence-based job redesign, autonomous working practices and organisational development.

King's College London: Led by Professor Mike Clinton, King's College London serves as the primary academic research partner, developing the underlying analytical model to examine how job crafting, technology adoption, autonomy, and trust intersect to shape employee wellbeing and performance.

Jabra: Part of the GN Group, the audio and enterprise video communications provider sponsored the multi-year study. Jabra's Future of Work research team contributed domain insight into enterprise collaboration architecture, audio-visual meeting dynamics and the role of shared conversational context in modern digital stacks.

Academic contributor: Professor Prithwiraj Choudhury of the London School of Economics (LSE) contributed analysis regarding the evolving relationship between individual AI capabilities, cross-functional specialisation and workflow architecture.
