# New study may change how we think about GLP-1s

For decades, obesity medications produced only modest weight loss. But Ozempic and related GLP-1 therapies have transformed the field, enabling sustained weight loss of 10 to 15% or more. Despite their success, scientists have not fully understood how these drugs work in the brain.

In a new study, Yale researchers identified an unexpected mechanism that challenges a long-held assumption about the brain's hunger circuitry: that agouti-related peptide (AgRP) neurons, known as drivers of hunger, function solely to oppose weight loss. The new study shows that GLP-1 therapies like Ozempic instead recruit these neurons to help sustain fat loss.

## Missing biology

Semaglutide, the active ingredient in GLP-1 drugs like Ozempic, has become one of the most effective medications ever developed for obesity, yet why it works so well wasn't fully understood. Previous generations of weight-loss drugs suppress appetite almost as effectively as semaglutide, but none produce the same degree of sustained weight loss — suggesting semaglutide does something beyond simply reducing appetite.

One prevailing view held that GLP-1 drugs promote weight loss by reducing the activity of AgRP neurons that drive hunger. Although widely discussed, this hypothesis had not been directly tested in vivo during chronic GLP-1 treatment until this study.

Researchers combined several approaches in a mouse model, monitoring body weight, food intake, metabolism, and energy expenditure during semaglutide treatment, and used genetic methods to selectively remove or silence AgRP hunger neurons to test whether those neurons were necessary for the drug's effects. When treating mice genetically modified to lack AgRP neurons, GLP-1 drugs could no longer sustain weight loss. Further experiments using electron microscopy, molecular biology, and electrophysiology showed these AgRP neurons were being activated rather than inhibited by semaglutide.

## Layer of complexity

The findings suggest the brain adapts to the calorie deficit created by GLP-1 treatment by increasing the activity of AgRP hunger neurons, which also coordinate fat loss — similar to what normally happens under calorie deficiency. This reveals a previously unrecognized layer of complexity in how GLP-1 therapies work.

"This completely changes how we think about the mechanism involved in these medications and provides new insight into the biology underlying their long-term effects, opening an avenue for the development of more efficient drugs," said Mateus d'Ávila, a Ph.D. candidate in neuroscience at Yale School of Medicine and first author of the study, published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (PNAS).

Because the study was performed in mice, additional research is needed before these findings can be translated to people. "By identifying a previously unrecognized neural mechanism involved in sustaining weight loss, our work provides new biological insights that could eventually help researchers design therapies that are even more effective or have fewer side effects," d'Ávila said.
