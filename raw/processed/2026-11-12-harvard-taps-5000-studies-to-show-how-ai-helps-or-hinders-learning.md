# Harvard Taps 5,000 Studies to Show How AI Helps or Hinders Learning

Note: this is a condensed paraphrase of the Education Week article, not a verbatim copy.

The Harvard Graduate School of Education (HGSE) released the AI Guidance by Age Matrix, a resource for educators, parents, students, and policymakers on how AI may help or hinder human development. It draws on more than 5,000 pieces of research across 81 developmental areas and shows that AI's effects on learning differ by a learner's age and the skills being developed.

The tool is part of Compass Collaborative, an initiative HGSE launched on Nov. 10, which aims to close the gap between developmental science on how people learn and the AI decisions made by educators, policymakers, and technology developers.

Context: research on AI's effects on learning is still emerging. Stanford University's AI Hub for Education found mixed results that depend on design and use, and a separate audit of meta-analyses found the existing studies flawed and unreliable as guides for decisionmakers.

Jeff Curley (chief of strategic initiatives in the dean's office at HGSE, co-director of Compass Collaborative) said schools are handling AI very differently, often with little evidence or research, and that the resource was meant to be research-based yet clear and actionable at the point of decision.

Tessa Forshaw (HGSE faculty chair of the workforce and workplace learning concentration, co-director of Compass Collaborative) said the "fundamental truths" of learning have not changed: proximity to human relationships with more experienced others, and opportunities to practice developing skills. If an AI use moves toward practice or human relationships, it is generally in good territory; if it moves away from them, it deserves a second thought.

## How the resource works

Users choose their role (teacher, parent, researcher, etc.), a developmental domain (such as cognitive foundations, creativity, or relationships), and an age range (early childhood to adulthood). The result page gives a quick take on the research, Compass Collaborative's recommendations on how technology can support those skills, key developmental processes and milestones for the age range, and how AI might help or hinder development.

Example given: for a K-12 educator looking at cognitive foundations for ages 9 to 11, children are still strengthening working memory, sustained attention, and self-monitoring of thinking. Possible help: adaptive platforms can keep difficulty in a range that supports meaningful practice, and evaluating AI output can build critical thinking that passive AI use bypasses. Possible harm: AI homework and answer tools can short-circuit the retrieval that builds memory and leads to learning.

## Early reactions

An elementary special education teacher in Grand Rapids, Mich., called it a helpful tool. An assistant principal at Mojave High School in North Las Vegas found the audience/age/domain breakdown interesting but the tool pretty basic, and said he would not recommend it yet, preferring ChatGPT for ideas on building skills.

Curley said the guidance is a "living resource" that will be updated as new research appears, and that HGSE chose not to wait because educators and parents are making these decisions now.
