# Always-On Work Rarely Pays Off. Just 8 Percent Link It to a Promotion

Survey data shows that despite 71 percent of workers feeling pressured to answer work calls or email after hours or on vacation, only 8 percent were promoted for doing so, and just 12 percent got raises.

Always-on accessibility and responsiveness to job demands after the workday ends almost always generates annoying, even stressful invasions of employees' private lives. Still, most workers regard the habit as a necessary sacrifice in seeking manager approval, and along with that pay raises and promotions.

Cue the gameshow "wrong answer" buzzer. New survey data shows only about 10 percent of always-on workers say their off-the-clock accessibility has ever paid off—except by raising burnout rates and decreasing work-life balance.

According to the 1,006 respondents to a survey by computing resource platform Software Finder, only "8 percent of workers say being highly responsive outside work hours directly contributed to a promotion." That "open all hours" attitude wasn't much more efficient in putting extra moolah into employees' pockets, with just 12 percent of participants saying it eventually led to a salary raise or bonus payment.

Overall, nearly 70 percent of adepts of always-on habits reported that "being available outside normal work hours has not meaningfully improved their career."

## Most employees always-on despite no payout for it

So why do people who answer calls, join remote meetings, or check and quickly respond to email after hours or on vacation continue that beyond-the-call grind? "Fear" is a fairly good explanation.

Fully 71 percent of responding employees said they felt "pressure to be 'always on,' checking work communications after hours," the survey said. That rate rose to 81 percent among managers. People keen to protect their work flexibility were also prone to all-hours responsiveness, with 76 percent of hybrid workers copping to the habit, compared to 71 percent of fully in-person staffers.

By contrast, true to their outlier workplace status, Gen Zers reported the least inclination to handle after-hours work queries at 65 percent, compared to 72 percent of both millennials and Gen X workers who do so. Still, the broader prevalence of always-on habits winds up seriously eating into the private lives of employees practicing them.

"The average worker spends 1.7 hours per week checking work communications after hours," Software Finder said, noting that compounding time equals "more than two full workweeks a year spent on unpaid availability."
