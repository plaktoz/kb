# AI Users Work 90 Min Longer Daily, Study Finds [2026]

AI users put in longer workdays than colleagues who skip the tools, and the gap reaches 90 minutes a day. That is the headline from a new ActivTrak Productivity Lab study, covered by TechRadar this week. The research landed on October 29, 2026, and it cuts against the pitch that AI hands employees their afternoons back.

The finding is an association, not a verdict. ActivTrak did not prove that AI causes people to work longer, and the report does not name the tools involved. What it does give leaders is a sharp reason to question adoption dashboards. Other recent coverage on this site, such as Gartner’s forecast that 30% of AI-driven layoffs get reversed, points the same way: the real cost and benefit of workplace AI is still unsettled.

This analysis walks through the numbers, separates what is confirmed from what is not, compares the study with other ways of measuring AI at work, and lays out what employers should do before the next quarterly review of their AI spend.

## What the ActivTrak Productivity Lab Report Found

The ActivTrak Productivity Lab released its research on October 29, 2026. According to the ActivTrak announcement, the team analyzed behavioral work data from 9,140 knowledge workers across 467 enterprises. The data window ran from April 1 through September 25, 2026, which is roughly 25 weeks of activity.

The central result is simple to state. Employees who use AI had workdays spanning 8.42 to 9.08 hours. Employees who do not use AI averaged 7.58 hours. The difference reaches up to approximately 90 minutes per day.

Beyond the headline gap, the lab reported that heavy AI users showed more application switching and shorter focus sessions. They also spent less of their active time in meetings. Gabriela Mauch, Chief Customer Officer and Head of the ActivTrak Productivity Lab, summed up the pattern in the release. She said AI may help employees finish tasks faster, but the research shows it contributes to longer workdays, more application switching and shorter focus sessions.

Note what the study measures. ActivTrak tracks digital work activity, so the data reflects what happened on screen across a workday, not what employees recalled in a survey. That makes the sample large and the signal hard to dismiss, but it also limits what the numbers can say about quality, effort or output.

Two pieces of arithmetic help put the range in context. The low end of the AI-user range, 8.42 hours, sits about 50 minutes above the non-user figure of 7.58 hours. The high end, 9.08 hours, sits exactly 1.5 hours above it. The 90-minute headline therefore describes the top of the range, not an average across every AI user.

The sample also works out to about 20 workers per enterprise on average, again simple division of 9,140 by 467. That suggests a broad spread across many employers rather than a few large companies driving the result, though the report excerpts do not break the sample down by industry or company size.

## How ActivTrak Defined Heavy AI Users

Definitions decide what a study can claim. ActivTrak labeled employees as heavy AI users when they spent more than 45 minutes with AI tools across at least five sessions per day. That is a behavioral threshold, not a job title or a license count, and it separates people who open an assistant once from people who work with it repeatedly.

## The session threshold

Five sessions a day means AI is woven into the rhythm of the job. A person who drafts, checks, re-prompts and revises in short bursts would cross the line quickly. This matters for interpretation, because the pattern the lab describes, more application switching and shorter focus blocks, matches how a session-heavy workflow looks in activity logs.

## The after-hours finding

Heavy AI users worked 18 more minutes per day after hours than non-users. Their after-hours work made up 32.3% of the workday, against 31.9% for non-users. The shares sit only 0.4 percentage points apart. One reading is that the extra time spreads across the whole day instead of piling up late at night. The report excerpts do not test that reading, so treat it as a question for follow-up work.

## Why Association Does Not Prove AI Causes Longer Days

The reported findings show a link between AI use and longer workdays. They do not show that AI use causes those longer days. Any claim that the tools themselves stretched the schedule remains unconfirmed.

Several alternative explanations fit the same data. Employees with heavier workloads may reach for AI because they are overloaded. Ambitious employees may adopt new tools early and also put in more hours. Teams under deadline pressure may use AI to survive a crunch. In each case the AI would travel with long days without driving them.

The opposite story is possible too. AI could create new work, such as reviewing machine-written drafts, correcting errors and managing more parallel tasks, and that extra work could push days longer. TechRadar’s coverage pointed toward that kind of implication, noting that AI usage alone should not be the goal. The study’s design cannot choose between these stories. Only a controlled comparison of the same workers before and after adoption could.

That gap is the single most important caveat for anyone quoting the 90-minute number. Repeat it as a pattern in the data, and keep the cause open.

## Fewer Meetings, More Switching: The Focus Trade-Off

One detail complicates a purely negative reading. Heavy AI users spent 3% of active work time in meetings, compared with 5% for non-AI users. That is a real reduction, and it is the kind of change a productivity pitch would celebrate. Meetings are among the most common complaints in knowledge work.

The same group, however, showed more application switching and shorter focus sessions, according to the lab. So the day shifts shape. Time leaves the conference room and goes into fragmented individual work across more tools. Whether that trade helps or hurts depends on the output, and the report excerpts do not measure output.

Fragmentation carries its own cost. Researchers who study attention have long argued that switching between tasks drains time and quality, and managers know the feeling of a day spent in thirty small windows. If AI turns deep work into rapid prompt-and-review loops, the clock may show a busy day while the number of finished deliverables stays flat. That is a hypothesis, not a finding, and a good target for the next round of research.

## What Mauch Told Leaders, and Why Usage Is the Wrong Target

The most quotable line in the release is aimed at executives. Mauch said: “For leaders, the goal can’t simply be AI usage; they need to treat AI usage as a means to measurable gains in productivity, efficiency and business performance.” The ActivTrak announcement carries the full statement.

That sentence captures a common failure in corporate rollouts. Many organizations track seats activated, prompts sent and weekly active users, because those numbers are easy to pull. They say little about whether the business got faster, cheaper or better. When a company rewards usage, employees use the tools more. When it rewards results, they use the tools where they help.

The startup world has started to build around that gap. A company like Marble, which raised $3.75M to train AI coworkers, sells the idea of AI that carries defined work, not AI that merely sits open in a tab. The ActivTrak data is a reminder that the definition of “defined work” needs a measurable outcome attached.

A fair reading of Mauch’s statement is not anti-AI. She frames usage as a means, and she asks leaders to connect it to gains they can count. The lab did not report any specific productivity gain, cost saving or business-performance improvement from AI in the available excerpts, so those gains remain unconfirmed in this dataset.

## Historical Context: From the Productivity Paradox to Generative AI

Economists have seen this movie before. In 1987 Robert Solow observed that computers showed up everywhere except in the productivity statistics, a remark that became known as the productivity paradox. Businesses spent heavily on information technology through the 1980s, and measurable gains arrived years later, after processes changed around the machines.

Email and smartphones followed a similar arc in the workplace. Each promised to speed up communication, and each also extended the reach of work into evenings and weekends. Faster tools raised expectations about response time and volume, so the saved time got absorbed. Economists describe a related idea under the name Jevons paradox, in which efficiency gains can raise total consumption of a resource instead of lowering it.

Applied to AI, that pattern gives one plausible reading of the ActivTrak numbers. If a model cuts the cost of producing a draft, a team may ask for more drafts. The day grows to fit the new capacity. This is interpretation, not a finding from the report, but it shows why a longer day and a real speedup can coexist.

## Market Impact for Employers and AI Vendors

For employers, the study raises a budgeting question. AI subscriptions and enterprise licenses now sit in many IT budgets, and finance teams want proof of return. A report that ties heavy usage to longer days, with no confirmed productivity gain attached, hands skeptics an argument and gives champions a homework assignment.

For vendors, the pressure runs toward outcome reporting. AI companies are spending heavily on compute, a theme we covered when Anthropic’s Q3 growth slowed to 47% against $895B of commitments. Revenue that depends on seat counts is only safe if customers keep believing the seats pay for themselves. Studies that question time savings make renewal conversations harder, and vendors that can show measured results will have the advantage.

Workforce analytics firms like ActivTrak occupy an interesting spot. They sell visibility into how people spend their day, and a headline study about AI and workday length advertises exactly that capability. Readers should weigh the finding on its merits while remembering that the publisher has a commercial interest in measurement.

The report excerpts do not quantify any market effect, and this article does not invent one. What can be said is that the burden of proof is shifting. Boards that once asked whether the company was using AI are starting to ask what the company got for it.

## Four Predictions for the Next 12 Months

These are analyst judgments based on the pattern above, not facts from the report.

Outcome metrics replace adoption dashboards. Expect more enterprises to report results such as cycle time or error rates next to usage numbers, driven by finance teams that want a return figure.

Follow-up studies chase causation. Researchers will try before-and-after comparisons of the same workers, because the open question here is cause, and the first credible answer will get wide coverage.

Workday span becomes a wellbeing metric. HR teams and employee representatives will start asking for workday-length data by AI-usage tier, especially where after-hours work is rising.

Vendors add focus and time-saved reporting. AI platforms will bundle analytics that show where saved minutes go, since a vendor that proves net savings can defend its price.

None of these require the 90-minute figure to hold up in every setting. They only require leaders to accept that usage by itself proves nothing.

## What Teams Can Do Now

Leaders do not need to wait for a perfect study. A few steps follow directly from the findings and from Mauch’s advice to tie usage to measurable gains.

Pick one outcome per AI deployment, such as turnaround time on support tickets, and measure it before and after.

Watch workday span and after-hours minutes by team, and treat a rising trend as a signal to review workload, not as proof of dedication.

Protect focus blocks. If heavy AI users switch apps more and focus for shorter stretches, set aside meeting-free windows for deep work.

Cap automated rewrites where they create review burden. Our walkthrough on capping AI rewrites with Cursor hooks shows one way to keep machine-generated changes reviewable.

Ask employees. Telemetry shows hours, and only people can say whether the extra time is review work, new tasks or pressure.

Individual workers can apply a version of the same advice. Decide what the tool is for, track whether it shortens the task, and stop when it does not. Using AI more is not the same as working better.
