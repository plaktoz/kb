# Why I Became a Student Again in My 40s

Subtitle: "Personal Perspective: There are flashcards on my nightstand like it's 2004."

After 14 years as a practicing anesthesiologist, the author decided to pursue a new board certification in obesity medicine, driven by patient demand for evidence-based guidance on topics like GLP-1 medications and nutrition that weren't covered in her original medical training.

She contrasts her current motivation with her younger self, noting that in her 20s she "memorized to survive," whereas now she studies out of genuine curiosity and a desire to immediately apply new knowledge with patients.

Drawing on Malcolm Knowles' adult learning theory, she explains that adults absorb material better when it connects to lived experience. She then shares three evidence-based study strategies she's adopted:

1. **Self-testing over rereading** — citing Roediger and Karpicke's research showing recall practice outperforms simple review.
2. **Structured breaks** — using a modified Pomodoro technique (50 minutes on, 10 minutes off) and prioritizing sleep for memory consolidation.
3. **Studying with a partner** — describing a "study-and-spa weekend" in San Diego with a friend also preparing for the exam.

The piece's emotional core addresses the discomfort of "being a beginner again," referencing Carol Dweck's growth mindset research to reframe wrong answers as data rather than identity threats. She closes by encouraging readers to pursue a curiosity of their own, framing lifelong learning as a source of joy and a pathway to reinvention.
