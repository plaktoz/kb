# How do institutions embed AI literacy in higher education?

It's no secret that students are using AI.

Research by the Higher Education Policy Institute reveals that AI use is now 'near universal' among undergraduates, with its survey of United Kingdom students finding that 95% report using AI in at least one way.

Meanwhile, the Digital Education Council's most recent research on AI in higher education shows that global AI literacy remains at an early stage, for students and faculty alike. The survey revealed that there are also early signs of dependence on AI for certain skills: 21% of respondents say they find it harder to work through problems without AI, while 20% say they depend on AI to produce better work.

Although students are actively using AI in parts of their education, there is an emerging disconnect between the rate of adoption and understanding how to use it responsibly.

As AI becomes ubiquitous in higher education settings and beyond, how can we help students to grow the skills they need in their studies and the workplace, while also dealing with the potential setbacks that overreliance on AI can bring?

## Understanding how students are using AI

It's easy to understand the reasons why students are turning to AI to assist them in their studies. It offers time-saving solutions at a time when we know that students are under increasing time pressure and often have other responsibilities on top of their studies.

For example, in the United States, data from 2025 suggests that 67% of undergraduates work for pay alongside their studies, while an estimated one fifth of undergraduate students are parents with caregiving responsibilities.

In the UK, nearly nine in 10 incoming students expect to work during term time, with less than a quarter believing they will have enough money to cover their living costs. Under these conditions, it is only natural they would seek out solutions that can make their studying more efficient.

While concerns about AI often focus on students using it to write assignments, the reality appears to be more nuanced. Students are using AI for a wide range of academic tasks, including researching unfamiliar topics, generating ideas, organising their thinking, refining drafts, and receiving feedback on their work.

While experimentation with the variety of ways in which AI can be used is valuable in its own right, it is also important for instructors and students to know what kinds of AI use actually enhance their studies.

Do they know what would constitute inappropriate use? Do they understand and consider the risks to their own studies, such as overreliance on AI tools and the erosion of their critical thinking faculties? Do they think about the longer-term impacts of irresponsible AI use on their academic development?

Given the large proportion of students who say they use AI in their studies, it is arguably even more important that they understand the benefits and drawbacks of using AI, and that there is sufficient provision and resources to give them this context at higher education institutions.

As a large university press with a global reach, we at Oxford University Press (OUP) want to support a more personalised learning experience for every student, helping them to harness the benefits of AI to achieve optimal learning outcomes.

It's why we are taking a conscientious approach to embedding AI in our higher education resources.

For example, alongside our AI study assistants in our Business, Law, Politics, and Science Trove platforms, we have developed an AI literacy module that supports instructors in supplying students with the skills to evaluate generative AI content and usage, which they can then apply within their courses, throughout their education, and into their future careers.

The module consists of interactive video and assessments, covering AI fundamentals in six parts, including:

- What is generative AI: Explaining the distinction between generative AI and other AI systems, some examples of common tools, their capabilities, and basic ethical considerations.
- AI and academic integrity: Covering appropriate decision-making, accurate documentation, and awareness of institutional AI policies, with guidance on distinguishing between AI use that supports learning and use that undermines it.
- Advantages and disadvantages: Addressing risks such as misinformation and hallucinations, and acknowledging benefits such as creativity and efficiency.
- Critical thinking: Applying techniques for identifying errors, verifying information, and analysing outputs for bias, relevance and accuracy.
- Guidance on prompting: Explaining prompt engineering, constructing effective prompts, refining through iteration, and applying techniques ethically in academic contexts.
- Generative AI activities: A section where students are then able to apply these concepts to a course-specific AI activity, authored by an expert in the course's field.

The authors of this foundational module, Dr Anand Rao, director of the Center for AI and the Liberal Arts at the University of Mary Washington, and Stefan Bauschard, course instructor at Union College and expert in AI and education, provided expert knowledge and guidance, and it is thanks to their hard work that we were able to shape this timely course, developed with insights from survey respondents, focus groups and reviewers.

## Supporting instructors to cover key topics

By introducing this module, we hope to equip students with skills that extend beyond education and can be used in their careers. But we also know that, while AI use is undoubtedly an important topic to raise with students, some course instructors may not feel prepared to lead discussion on these topics themselves.

In The AI Challenge report from the American Association of Colleges and Universities and Elon University, 62% of faculty respondents said they believed their graduates were not prepared in their overall understanding and use of generative AI (GenAI) tools, while 68% also said their institutions had not prepared them for using GenAI for teaching and mentoring students.

Our module offers instructors a flexible, ready-to-use resource they can choose to assign to their students on Oxford Insight, Oxford Learning Link, and Oxford Trove, providing an effective and efficient way to cover important AI literacy basics.

We have also hosted webinars for faculty on AI usage and literacy, offering further guidance and background that we hope will be beneficial to them.

Research by the National Association of Colleges and Employers found that, while employers increasingly value AI skills in recent college graduates, 51% of new graduates said they are not actively building those skills for the future.

Our module acts as a starting point in closing this skills gap. Being AI literate is valuable, regardless of discipline and what direction your future career may take you.

This is why we have made our AI literacy module available for instructors to assign to students, encouraging and boosting engagement with a crucial topic at a crucial point in their education, while alleviating pressure on instructors who need a helping hand in this evolving space.

It is likely that, with each passing week, studies and surveys will continue to focus on students and AI: for example, there was the recent news that OpenAI has launched a new student ambassador programme to champion the use of AI tools on campus.

What is already clear from a growing body of research is that responsible AI usage, key critical evaluation techniques, and AI literacy skills are set to be very valuable for young people. It's up to all of us to give them the skills and the opportunities to succeed in this ever-changing landscape.
