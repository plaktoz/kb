# Study reveals why DNA damage from smoking, UV rays and other factors may cause cancer in some people but not others

source_url: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1137422

---

University of Cambridge, News Release — July 22, 2026

Scientists have found the first direct evidence of the powerful role genetic makeup plays in influencing cancer risk, with inherited genes interacting with acquired mutations to shape how tumors evolve. The findings, published in *Nature* (Aitken, SJ et al., "Genetic background sets the trajectory of experimental cancer evolution," DOI: 10.1038/s41586-026-10821-z) and carried out in mice, suggest responses to DNA-damaging cancer treatments could differ by patient genetics, strengthening the case for personalized treatment.

Tumors arise when DNA accumulates mutations, causing cells to grow faster and ignore signals that would otherwise trigger cell death. Environmental exposures (cigarette smoke, sunlight) influence how much DNA damage occurs, and inherited genetic alterations affect how many mutations accumulate — but not everyone exposed to the same risk factors develops cancer (most smokers don't get lung cancer; some non-smokers do). Proving that inherited genetics explains this has been difficult in human cohort studies because lifestyle, environment, and exposure history vary so much between people.

Researchers from Cambridge, Edinburgh, and collaborators across Europe and the US — co-led by Professor Duncan Odom, Dr. Sarah Aitken, and Professor Martin Taylor — bred four mouse strains spanning genetic diversity comparable to human populations, then exposed all of them to a single controlled dose of the liver carcinogen diethylnitrosamine (DEN, found in tobacco smoke and some processed foods) at 15 days of age, eliminating the environmental variation that confounds human studies. They sequenced the genomes of almost 600 tumors, analyzed gene activity, and compared spontaneous tumor formation across strains to reconstruct how each tumor evolved from its original mutation.

Across all strains, cancers nearly always acquired a driver mutation activating the same MAPK signaling pathway — but depending on the mouse's inherited genetics, the specific driver mutation altered other cancer-associated pathways differently and caused varying rates of whole-genome duplication (doubling of the entire chromosome set).

"Cancer does not arise entirely by chance. Although tumours often reach the same biological endpoint, the path to that endpoint is determined by an individual's genetic background," said senior author Duncan Odom (now at DKFZ, German Cancer Research Centre). First author Sarah Aitken (Yale School of Medicine) said the findings mean "future cancer prevention and screening strategies will need to take into account inherited genetics and population diversity," and that drug response is likely to differ by inherited genetics too. Cancer Research UK's Dr. Sam Godfrey called it "a fascinating hint" requiring more research before translating to humans, but one that "could change our understanding of how cancer starts."
