# Is Taste the New Moat? AI Moats Explained (2026 Evidence)

Bodiam Castle has stood in East Sussex since 1385. Its walls are thin. A serious siege would have broken them. What made the castle hard to take was the water around it, and that picture gave business strategy its favorite word. In 1986, Warren Buffett described GEICO's cost advantage as "a kind of moat that protects a valuable and much-sought-after business castle."

Forty years later, AI drained a lot of moats. Code that took a team six months now takes one person a weekend. So the industry went looking for a new one, and in early 2026 it settled on a single word: taste.

This guide tests that claim against the evidence. The short version: taste matters more than it ever did, and it is still not a moat. It is the thing you use to build one.

TL;DR: Taste is a filter, not a wall. App releases rose 60% year over year in Q1 2026 while studies show AI-assisted output converging, so judgment is scarce. But judgment alone can be copied. The moats that hold in 2026 are accumulated context, workflow depth, trust, owned distribution and taste written into process.

## Quick Answer: Is Taste a Moat?

Taste is not a moat on its own, because a moat must survive a competitor who can see your product and copy it. Taste decides what you build and what you refuse to build. It becomes defensible only after you encode it in process and compound it with private context. Until then it is an edge with a half-life.

## What Does "Taste Is the New Moat" Mean?

"Taste is the new moat" is the claim that when AI can produce any artifact on request, the lasting advantage belongs to people who know which artifact is worth producing. The phrase spread through tech in the first quarter of 2026, and it drew a counter-argument within days. Both sides are worth reading in their own words.

In February 2026, Fortune reported Sam Altman's view that good taste may get you a job in the AI era. By May, a video essay by Hak titled "Is taste really the only moat left?" gave the argument its sharpest image: software just got fast fashion. This guide builds on that essay's three layers of taste and its three reps, and adds the strategy test and the data that the debate was missing.

Notice who is on the "against" side. One of the strongest sceptics worked at Linear when he posted it, the product that everyone on the "for" side holds up as proof. That is a hint that the two camps are describing different things. One camp describes a skill. The other describes a business defense. A skill can be valuable and still fail the test for a moat.

## Why Is Everyone Talking About Taste in 2026?

Because supply exploded. In the first quarter of 2026, new app releases rose 60% year over year across both app stores and 80% on iOS, according to Appfigures data reported by TechCrunch. When the cost of making software falls toward zero, the scarce resource stops being the ability to make it.

Carter's line explains the shift in one sentence. When building software took scarce engineers and long timelines, knowing how to build was the work. Time did the filtering for you: anything that took six months had to earn those six months. Now the first 80% of a product arrives in an afternoon, so the filter has to come from the builder. We covered the supply side of this in the state of vibe coding and the casualties in the vibe coding graveyard.

The result is familiar to anyone who has opened a launch feed this year. Onboarding flows that ask everything and explain nothing. Settings pages that look like spreadsheets. Newsletters you can identify as machine-written three sentences in. We have a name for the output, and a full guide to it: AI slop.

## What Is a Moat, Exactly?

A moat is a structural advantage that lets a business keep its customers and its margins even when competitors can see what it does. The test is simple and harsh: if a well-funded rival copied your product tomorrow, what would still stop your customers from leaving? Whatever survives that question is the moat.

Read the table again and look for taste. It is not one of the seven. A skill is not a power until it is built into the company. That is the gap in most of the writing on this topic: it argues about whether taste is valuable, when the useful question is which power it feeds.

## What Is Taste? Three Layers Most Definitions Blur

Taste is the ability to recognize that something is right, or wrong, before you can explain why. It is a real form of knowledge, and it is not the same as preference. Most arguments about taste go in circles because the word covers three different layers, and people defend one while attacking another.

Layer 1 is recognition. A piano tuner hears a flat note before measuring it. A chef knows a sauce needs something before knowing what. Designer Dan Alldis calls this "the ability to feel the delta between almost and actually." In AI work, it is the moment you look at a wall of generated options and realize the prompt was wrong.

Layer 2 is plumbing. Many products copied the surface of the tools they admire: dark mode, keyboard-first navigation, the same typography. Few copied the discipline underneath. Linear co-founder Karri Saarinen puts the difference this way: "For quality, you need a team that views the spec as the baseline, not the finish line." Dieter Rams compressed the same idea into three words in 1995: Weniger, aber besser. Less, but better.

Layer 3 is restraint. This is the uncomfortable one. Builders used to ask "can I ship this?" The answer is now always yes, so the only useful question left is "should this exist?" Paul Graham made the constraint concrete back in 2002: "When you're forced to be simple, you're forced to face the real problem."

A note on a famous line. You will often read that Michelangelo said the statue was already in the marble and he only carved away what was not David. Quote Investigator traces that story to an 1858 book review and to later jokes credited to Ruskin and others, with no evidence that Michelangelo said it. It is a good image and a false attribution, which is a small lesson in taste by itself: check the plumbing.

## Is Taste Actually a Moat? The Case For and Against

No. Taste passes the scarcity test and fails the durability test. It is scarce because few people exercise it. It is not durable by itself because its output is public, and anything public can be studied, copied and learned by people and by models. The honest verdict takes both sides seriously.

Shankar's word for it is the right one: alpha. In investing, alpha is an edge over the market that shrinks as others discover it. Taste works the same way. A fresh interaction pattern is alpha on the day you ship it and table stakes two quarters later.

The companies that look like they have a "taste moat" did something more specific. They converted taste into things that do not decay at the same rate.

There are two exits in that diagram. Taste that stays in one person's head exits through "Copied." Taste that is written down, enforced and recorded exits through compounding context. Apple is the classic example: the taste of a few people became a review culture, then a brand, then a habit for a billion customers. We traced that arc in the history of Apple and in Bill Atkinson's HyperCard, where a single designer's standards became a tool that millions of non-programmers used to build.

## Does AI Make Everything Look the Same?

Yes, at the group level, and the evidence is consistent. AI assistance raises the quality of an average individual output and lowers the variety across outputs. That combination is exactly the condition under which judgment becomes valuable: everyone gets the same strong first draft, so the difference is made after the draft.

The meta-analysis is the number to remember. A positive effect of 0.27 on creative performance, and a negative effect of 0.86 on diversity. The floor went up. The spread collapsed.

This is why so many 2026 products feel alike when you first open them. They come from the same factories, trained on the same patterns. It is also why the editorial habit matters more than the generating habit. The working rule from practitioners is short: what the model gives you is the starting line, not the finish line.

## How Did Software Get Fast Fashion?

Software got fast fashion when production cost fell below the cost of judgment. Clothing went through the same change: production got cheap, patterns got copied, fabric got thinner. Shein lists "more than 6,000 new fashion, beauty and lifestyle products daily" by its own account, and up to 10,000 by NPR's reporting. Much of it does not outlast the season.

The comparison is useful for what it does not say. Fast fashion did not kill clothing brands. It split the market. One part competes on price and volume. The other part competes on fit, material and trust, and it charges more than before. Software is heading for the same split, and the middle is where it hurts.

A fair word for the tools: cheap production is a real gift. Ideas that used to die in the gap between thinking and shipping now get built. The problem is not speed. The problem is shipping the prototype as if it were the product. A first draft is supposed to be rough. The failure is calling it finished and walking away.

## What Do monday.com and Perplexity Teach About Rented Advantage?

They teach that an advantage you rent can be repriced by the landlord. monday.com rented its distribution from ad and search platforms. Perplexity rents its intelligence from model vendors. Both are real businesses with real revenue, and both spent 2026 showing where the exposure sits.

### monday.com: rented distribution

monday.com is one of the great growth stories in software. It also built that growth on paid acquisition. Its IPO filing shows sales and marketing at $191.4 million in 2020, which was 119% of revenue. The plan worked: the company passed $1 billion in annual recurring revenue in August 2024.

Then the channel changed. On the Q2 2025 earnings call, management told analysts it saw "some softness within the down market due to the changes in the Google algorithm." The stock fell 29.8% in a day. In February 2026 the company guided to 2026 revenue of $1.452 billion to $1.462 billion and stopped discussing its 2027 targets, and the stock fell another 21%. In July 2026 it announced a cut of about 20% of its staff. The full sourced timeline is in our history of monday.com.

### Perplexity: rented intelligence

Perplexity built a product people love on top of models it does not own. The Information reported that in 2024 the company took in about $34 million in revenue and spent at least $57 million on AI models and web services (summary). From 2025, the response was predictable: caps on heavy features, a $200 a month Max tier, and a credit system for its agent product.

The story is not a collapse. By August 2026, annualized revenue had passed $750 million, even as its share of AI web traffic fell. The lesson is narrower and more useful: when your cost of goods is someone else's price list, your margin is someone else's decision. See the history of Perplexity for the full ledger.

This is the same force behind the February 2026 software selloff and the move away from pure seat pricing. We covered both in the SaaSpocalypse explained and the great SaaS unbundling.

## What Moats Still Work in the Age of AI?

Five moats hold up in the 2026 evidence: accumulated context, workflow depth, earned trust, owned distribution and taste encoded as process. Each one shares a property. A competitor cannot buy it with a bigger model budget, because it is produced by time spent with real customers.

The Abundance Moat Stack (2026), from top to bottom:

5. Encoded taste: written standards, review gates
4. Owned distribution: community, marketplace, habit
3. Earned trust: brand, reliability, security
2. Workflow depth: the product runs the work
1. Accumulated context: memory the product earned

Below the stack sits the rented layer: models, compute, ad channels. Everyone can buy this, so it defends nothing.

### Is context really a moat if memory is portable?

Context at the model layer is not a moat, and the early evidence points one way. In March 2026 Anthropic shipped a tool that imports your memories from other assistants. A 2026 paper surveyed 388 AI chat users. The 170 active users in it used 2.83 AI platforms on average, and only 17.6% used a single one. Chat memory moves.

Context at the workflow layer is different. A list of facts about you is portable. A working system is not: the client records, the agents trained on your documents, the automations wired to your tools, and six months of approve and reject decisions that taught the system how you work. Agency builder Luuk Alleman put it in a single sentence: "The switching cost is the memory." For the long version, see the history of agent memory and context engineering.

## Who Are You Building For: Humans or Agents?

Both, and they want opposite things. An AI agent reading your product does not care how it looks. It wants structure, clean interfaces and predictable patterns. A human in a world full of generated sameness cares more about feel than before. The temptation is to serve the agent and ship slop to the human. That is backwards.

This is why "agent-readable" and "delightful" are not a trade-off. The best products in 2026 expose a plain, structured surface for agents and keep a crafted one for people. Our own site does this with Markdown copies of documentation pages for agents and designed pages for readers. The bar for human-facing software went up, not down. ## Is the MVP Dead? MVP vs Minimum Lovable Product

The MVP is not dead, but it stopped being a goal and became a starting condition. A minimum viable product proves that something works. When anyone can produce a working version by Sunday night, "it works" no longer separates you from 100 other versions of the same idea. The bar moved to the minimum lovable product.

Brian de Haaff of Aha! has written that he first wrote about the minimum lovable product in 2013. The idea waited a decade for its moment. Speed is no longer a reason to skip the lovable part, because speed is no longer scarce.

## How Do You Turn Taste Into a Moat?

Turn taste into a moat in five moves: write it down, gate on it, record every decision, feed the record back, and cut on a schedule. The goal is to move judgment out of one person's head and into a system that every teammate and every AI agent applies the same way, and that gets better with use.

Step 1 is where most teams stop, and it is the cheapest step. A taste spec does not need to be clever. It needs to exist.

Taste spec (one page, read by people and agents):

- We always: lead with the answer, then the detail; show an empty state that teaches the next action; name the human who approved the output.
- We never: ship a first draft as final; add a setting when a good default will do; use a number we cannot source.
- Examples of right (5 links).
- Examples of almost (5 links, with one line on what is off).
- Kill list this week: 1 item, removed, not deprioritized.

The "examples of almost" line carries the most weight. Models and new teammates learn a standard fastest from near misses, because near misses show where the line is.

That loop is the whole mechanism. The human stays in control of every decision. The system keeps the reasons. After six months, the record is something no competitor has and no model vendor can ship, because it is made of your decisions. This is the approve, edit or reject pattern that agent frameworks document as human-in-the-loop, and it is the practical bridge from taste to switching costs.

## How Do You Develop Taste? Three Reps

You develop taste the way you develop any judgment: repetition, feedback, and exposure to people who see differently. There is no shortcut, and it cannot be outsourced to a model. Three habits do most of the work, and each takes less than an hour a week.

On the second rep, Carter leans on Hannah Arendt: "For Arendt, taste is the exercise of judgment in the presence of others." Comedians do not write jokes only in their heads. They go up at an open mic and learn in real time which lines die. Your version is the friend who says the empty state feels sterile, or the user who says the AI feature feels bolted on.

On the third rep: expect to hate it. The thing you are cutting has time, thought and tokens invested in it. That resistance is the feeling of taste forming.

One more model helps here, from a talk by Joel Torana on mapping ideas. Your experience is the macro view, the era you live in is the meso view, and the specific thing you make is the micro view where the two meet. Authority comes from that intersection, which is why two people with the same tools produce different work. If you like thinking in levels, our guide to systems thinking goes deeper.
