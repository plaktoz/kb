# Language: Why We Hear More Than Words

Human language isn't a simple code. Unlike animal communication—where signals have rigid one-to-one meanings—human language is deeply **inferential**, meaning we interpret far more than the literal definitions of words.

Wilson and Sperber's *Meaning and Relevance* illustrates this with a striking example: the sentence "Peter got angry and Mary left" carries entirely different implications than "Mary left and Peter got angry"—despite using identical words. Order, tone, and context reshape meaning dramatically.

## Beyond Literal Meaning

Animals rely on fixed coded signals; any mutation disrupts communication. Humans are different. Our inferential capacity means we absorb context, emotion, and implication alongside words. This is why a traveler can navigate a foreign hotel without sharing a language—tone and context fill the gaps.

## Irony as Efficiency

Irony demonstrates how much meaning can be compressed into few words. As Wilson and Sperber explain, irony communicates "an attitude to [a] proposition and to those who might hold or have held it"—not merely the opposite of what's said.

When someone says "That was fun" after a dull party, listeners instantly decode disappointment, social distance, and cultural commentary—all from three words.

## The Takeaway

Two cognitive abilities drive human language's power: linguistic capacity and the ability to **attribute mental states to others**. We instinctively seek context and infer intent.

The practical lesson: resist pedantry. Taking language purely at face value misses most of what's actually being communicated.
