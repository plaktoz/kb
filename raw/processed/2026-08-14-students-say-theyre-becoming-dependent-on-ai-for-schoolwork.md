# Students say they're becoming 'dependent' on AI for schoolwork

The education watchdog has called for a national framework to navigate artificial intelligence in schools, with a recent report finding more than half of teachers think it's a barrier for students learning to think for themselves.

The Education Review Office (ERO) report outlined how New Zealand schools and teachers were engaging with AI and called for an urgent national framework: "There is an urgent need for a more coherent system-wide approach to help schools use AI safely, consistently, and effectively, to enhance teaching and learning." Nearly 60% of teachers said AI is having a negative impact on students' critical thinking. Students and teachers also said AI had positive impacts when used as a tool for problem solving and checking work instead of doing the work for them.

Year 11 students at Rongotai College in Wellington said they were using AI tools in the classroom more and more. "Since AI, it's made me rely on it more, so I haven't had the chance to think for myself," one said. "Whenever I am trying to think of questions about everyday life, school, schoolwork, I've become really dependent on asking ChatGPT for help," another said.

Teacher Sonya McAteer said students are allowed to use AI to get definitions the same way they'd use a dictionary, but beyond that teachers question what the AI told them and where it came from. She said up to two hours on top of marking was spent checking assessments for AI plagiarism, though "very few of them intentionally use it to cheat." Some departments allow AI for research; others, like the English department, make students sign a waiver saying they must not use it.

## Thinking becomes 'much more homogenised' — neuroscientist

Adam Green, a neuroscientist at Georgetown University, told 1News there is strong evidence AI limits creativity. "When we come up with ideas without AI, we travel through the history of our own lives. So, we build these semantic networks in our brains," he said. "When we generate ideas with AI, instead of traveling through our own life story, our own semantic networks, we're traveling through this massively shared common network of ideas — and if we let those ideas replace our own, then that journey becomes much more homogenised."

AUT psychotherapist and AI ethics advisor Brigitte Viljoen said the government needs stricter AI policies for schools: "The Government's taken a soft touch approach to AI regulation in New Zealand, more guidelines than actual regulation, and this is concerning because it's not providing guardrails, giving transparency, and also accountability to AI companies."

Education Minister Erica Stanford said she welcomed the report and that the Ministry of Education broadly accepts its recommendations to strengthen responsible AI use: "AI must not get in the way of students developing and demonstrating their own knowledge and skills."
