# The AI Moat Investors Should Avoid Counting Twice

AI investors have become fluent in the language of flywheels. More users generate more data. More data improves the model. A better model attracts more users. Repeat.

The logic is sound. The terminology often is not.

A proprietary data advantage and a network effect can reinforce each other, but they are economically different sources of defensibility. Treating them as interchangeable leads founders to overestimate their moat and investors to double-count the same advantage. At my technology investment firm, we evaluate hundreds of AI investments each year. We test these advantages separately because they behave very differently when a competitor enters the market.

The distinction starts with a simple question: Does another participant make the product more valuable to users, or does that participant merely give the company more information with which to improve the product? Those are not the same thing.

## Two Different Engines

A conventional network effect exists when additional participants increase the value of the product to other participants. A car service marketplace illustrates the point. More riders can attract more drivers, while more drivers improve availability and reduce wait times for riders. The value comes from participation itself; the network is part of the product.

A data or learning advantage works differently. An AI application that learns from every customer's interactions may see which recommendations users accept, which outputs they reject and where the system fails. If the company can use those signals to improve the product, each new customer makes the underlying system better.

Customers may benefit from that learning even if they never interact with one another. The improvement comes from what the company learns, not simply from having more users. That distinction is easy to state and easy to blur in a pitch deck.

Uber is an example of both mechanisms. Its rider-driver marketplace runs on what the company itself called a "liquidity network effect" in its S-1: More drivers can shorten wait times, which can attract more riders, which can draw more drivers. Separately, Uber uses marketplace and historical data to improve matching, pricing and forecasting. The two reinforce each other, but only one requires other users present for the value to appear.

The difference becomes even more important in enterprise AI. Consider a company whose software analyzes highly proprietary customer data. If each customer remains isolated and the vendor cannot pool that information across accounts, the company may still have an important data advantage. It does not automatically have a network effect.

Palantir is instructive here. Its platform helps customers make use of their own data, with access controls and governance built in. Palantir's "AIP security and privacy" webpage says its AI platform does not use customer data to retrain third-party models, though customers can retrain private models. That can be a powerful product and trust strategy. Economically, though, it is different from a system in which every new participant improves the experience for everyone else.

## Why This Matters To Investors

The two advantages compound differently, with direct underwriting implications. A network effect tends to become harder to dislodge as participation grows; a competitor must recreate not merely the product but the audience around it. A data advantage is often more transient, eroding as a category matures, unless something structural keeps the data from converging across competitors.

In my view, the relevant diligence questions are: Is the data genuinely proprietary? Does additional data keep improving the system? How expensive is it to acquire or replicate? Is the resulting improvement large enough for customers to notice?

That last point matters greatly in AI. A company can sit on billions of observations and still have no real data moat if a competitor can obtain comparable data, license it, generate synthetic examples or simply catch up through better engineering.

Conversely, a modest dataset can be strategically valuable if it captures something competitors cannot easily reproduce, such as proprietary outcomes, specialized workflows, hard-to-label edge cases and high-frequency feedback tightly coupled to the product. Volume alone is not the moat. The learning curve is.

## Where The Two Combine

I consider the strongest companies to be those that run both engines at once. Picture an AI platform connecting buyers and suppliers: More participants widen the number and quality of possible transactions, and every transaction generates information about pricing, reliability, demand, fraud and fulfillment that sharpens matching and prediction.

Over time, the two advantages can feed each other. More participants create more interactions, more interactions create more proprietary intelligence, and better intelligence makes each interaction more valuable, which draws in more participants. That compounding loop is the pattern we look hardest for in enterprise AI networks.

## Four Questions That Separate A Network Effect From A Data Story

Before we underwrite either kind of moat, we ask a few key questions that I recommend other investors consider:

1. What does a new participant actually contribute? Is it another node in a network, proprietary information or both?

2. Who benefits from that contribution? Do existing users benefit from participation itself, from improvements driven by other customers' data or both?

3. Is the data genuinely proprietary? Could a well-capitalized competitor acquire comparable data, generate it synthetically, license it or reproduce the underlying signal another way?

4. Does incremental data still move the needle? Can the company show that additional usage measurably improves accuracy, conversion, retention, speed or another outcome customers care about?

If new participants make the product more valuable to existing users, and their activity generates learnings that competitors can't easily match, the company may have both advantages. But if the honest answer is simply "more customers give us more data," founders should stop calling that a network effect, and investors should stop paying for one.

The distinction is more than semantic. In AI, where technical advantages can diffuse quickly, knowing which part of the moat comes from the model, which comes from proprietary learning and which comes from the network itself is the difference between underwriting a durable business and underwriting a head start.
