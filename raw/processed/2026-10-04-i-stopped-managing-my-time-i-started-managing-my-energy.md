# I stopped managing my time. I started managing my energy.

## There are still only 24 hours in a day. The difference is how I choose to spend them.

Looking back, I spent years trying to solve the wrong problem. I thought I needed to manage my time better, when what I really needed was to understand my energy.

I bought planners, colour-coded my calendar and searched for better productivity systems because I assumed I simply wasn’t organising my days well enough. If I could just schedule everything perfectly, surely I’d finally stop feeling behind.

But no matter how carefully I planned my time, I still had days where everything felt difficult. I would stare at my to-do list, knowing exactly what I was supposed to do, but not having the mental energy to begin.

Eventually, I realised I’d been trying to manage the wrong thing.

Time is the same every day. My energy isn’t.

Once I started paying attention to how I felt rather than just what time it was, my days became much easier to navigate.

## I stopped expecting every hour to be equally productive.

One of the biggest mistakes I made was assuming that 2 p.m. on Tuesday should feel exactly the same as 9 a.m. on Saturday.

In reality, my energy rises and falls throughout the day, the week and even the year. Some mornings I wake up ready to write for hours. Other days, replying to a few emails feels like enough.

I used to see those lower-energy days as failures. Now I see them as information.

Not every hour is meant for deep work. Some are meant for admin, errands or simply slowing down. Once I stopped fighting my natural rhythm, I found myself getting more done with much less frustration.

## I started matching the task to the energy.

Instead of asking, “What should I do next?”, I now ask, “What do I have the energy for?”

If I’m feeling focused, that’s when I write, study or tackle work that requires real concentration.

If my brain feels tired, I’ll answer emails, tidy the house, go for a walk or do something more repetitive. These tasks still move life forward, but they don’t demand the same level of attention.

I’ve realised that productivity isn’t just about managing your schedule. It’s about pairing the right work with the right version of yourself.

## I stopped feeling guilty for protecting my energy.

There was a time when I thought saying no meant I wasn’t working hard enough.

Now I understand that every commitment has a cost, even if it’s something enjoyable. Every meeting, social event or extra project asks for a little of your attention, your time and your emotional capacity.

Protecting my energy has become less about doing less and more about choosing carefully.

I still say yes to plenty of things. I’m simply more aware that every yes shapes what I’ll have left for everything else.

## I accepted that rest is productive.

This has probably been the hardest lesson for me to learn.

When I was younger, I treated rest as something that interrupted productivity. It felt like time I should have been spending on something more useful.

Ironically, the more exhausted I became, the less capable I was of doing meaningful work.

Now I think about rest differently. Going for a walk, reading for half an hour, getting to bed earlier or spending an evening doing very little aren’t distractions from a productive life. They’re part of what makes one possible.

Energy has to come from somewhere.

## I stopped planning for my best days.

For a long time, every weekly plan I made assumed I’d wake up each morning feeling motivated, focused and full of energy.

Life has never worked like that.

Now I build my weeks around the assumption that there will be busy days, low-energy afternoons and moments when I simply won’t have the capacity I’d hoped for.

Rather than seeing that as pessimistic, I’ve found it incredibly freeing. My plans have become more realistic, which means I’m much more likely to stick to them.

It’s easier to be consistent when your routines expect you to be human.

## I started asking a different question.

Whenever I feel overwhelmed now, I no longer ask:

“How can I fit everything into today?”

Instead, I ask:

“Given the energy I have today, what’s the kindest and most useful way to spend it?”

The answer is rarely perfect.

Sometimes it’s finishing an important piece of work.

Sometimes it’s cancelling plans and going to bed early.

Sometimes it’s accepting that today is a maintenance day rather than a progress day.

I’ve found that asking this question leads to much better decisions than trying to squeeze every possible minute out of the day.

I still use a calendar. I still make to-do lists. I still plan my weeks. But I’ve stopped believing that managing my life is simply about managing my time.

The older I get, the more convinced I am that the real skill is learning how to work with your energy instead of constantly demanding more from it.

Because no planner can give you more hours.

But learning to spend your energy wisely can make the hours you already have feel very different.
