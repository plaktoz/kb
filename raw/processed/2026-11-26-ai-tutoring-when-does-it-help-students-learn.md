# AI Tutoring: When Does It Help Students Learn?

Hints, feedback, and teacher oversight can give AI a useful role in practice. Schools need to examine how that help affects learning.

AI tutoring can support student learning when feedback, guided practice, and teacher oversight help students build skills they can use independently.

AI [tutoring](https://edcircuit.com/tutoring/) should help students make progress on a problem while building the skills to tackle the next one independently.

For a student stuck on an algebra equation, that help might begin with a question about the operation they need to undo. A follow-up could address a mistaken step or offer a worked example. The student then needs a chance to try again.

The quality of that exchange matters. A tool's responses should fit the student's difficulty, the learning goal, and the methods being taught.

Schools also need to be clear about what they are evaluating. AI tutoring may involve a student working directly with a conversational system, a structured practice tool, or AI suggestions delivered to a human tutor. Those arrangements place different responsibilities on students and adults. Evidence about one should not automatically be applied to another.

## What the Research Shows

A [2025 study published in PNAS](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122) examined nearly 1,000 high school mathematics students in Turkey. Researchers compared a general-purpose GPT-4 interface, a version designed with teacher input to guide learning, and a group without generative [AI access](https://edcircuit.com/ai-access-is-creating-a-new-digital-divide/).

Both AI groups performed better during assisted practice. On a later exam without AI, however, students who had used the general-purpose interface performed worse than those who had practiced without it.

The guided version largely avoided that negative effect, but did not show a clear improvement over the comparison group on the exam. Its design reduced the learning problem seen with unrestricted assistance; it did not establish an advantage in independent performance.

A [Stanford-led trial of Tutor CoPilot](https://scale.stanford.edu/publications/tutor-copilot-human-ai-approach-scaling-real-time-expertise) tested a different approach: AI suggested responses to human tutors during sessions. Students whose tutors used the system were four percentage points more likely to master mathematics topics than students whose tutors did not.

That result applies to AI support for human instruction. It does not establish that students would receive the same benefit from working alone with a chatbot.

## Test AI Tutors With Classroom Problems

A tool that asks questions can still leave a student stuck. Teachers should examine whether its feedback addresses the actual difficulty.

Consider the equation **2x + 5 = 17**. A student subtracts five from each side, reaches **2x = 12**, and stops. A targeted prompt could ask which operation would undo multiplying x by two. After the student answers, a related problem can show whether the idea carries over.

The same prompt would be poorly matched to a student who does not yet understand why subtracting five preserves the equality. That learner may need an explanation or a visual representation of keeping both sides balanced.

If a student repeatedly makes an error with negative numbers, another prompt to think carefully offers little direction. Feedback could identify the relevant step, explain the sign change, and give the student a similar example to attempt.

Worked examples can also be useful. Teachers need to see whether students can use the method afterward. Copying a completed solution reveals little about whether it makes sense to the learner.

Before adopting a tool, schools can test it with familiar situations: an incorrect first step, a partly correct explanation, a vague request for help, and repeated confusion.

Review the accuracy of the responses, their suitability for the student's level, and their consistency with classroom instruction. Check what happens when a student asks directly for an answer or challenges the system with an incorrect claim.

Where confusion continues, students need a clear route to a teacher or tutor. Repeating prompts indefinitely should not become the default response to a learning gap.

## Keep Teachers Connected to the Learning

A teacher might use an approved tool for targeted practice after introducing a concept, then review a short task before deciding whether students are ready to move on.

In reading, a guided activity could ask students to identify a claim and support it with a passage from the text. A follow-up discussion gives the teacher a chance to hear how students connect the two.

The [National Student Support Accelerator's review of emerging AI tutoring approaches](https://nssa.stanford.edu/news/research-notes-two-emerging-strategies-using-ai-tutoring) describes models in which human tutors review AI responses or use AI suggestions to guide their teaching. Adults remain involved in deciding what help reaches students.

Schools considering direct student use should establish how teachers will review progress and respond to problems. Completed tasks and platform scores can inform that review, alongside students' explanations and subsequent work.

A useful report should help a teacher identify an instructional next step. If several students need repeated help with the same concept, the response might be a small-group lesson. If a student succeeds only after receiving extensive assistance, the teacher may need to check the skill more closely before moving ahead.

Staff also need time to examine confusing or inaccurate responses and agree on how to address them. That work belongs in implementation planning.

Families should know which tools are approved, what they support, and how to contact the school when the help is confusing. For homework, directions can explain whether students should attempt a problem first, request a hint, or stop and bring a question to class.

## Match the Support to Student Needs

A response can be mathematically correct and still be difficult for a student to use.

Long explanations, unfamiliar vocabulary, and inaccessible notation may interfere with the task. Teachers should examine whether students can read or hear the response, navigate the interface, and communicate what they have already tried.

[CAST's Universal Design for Learning Guidelines](https://udlguidelines.cast.org/) emphasize multiple ways to access information and express learning, including accessible materials and assistive technologies. Those considerations belong in decisions about AI tutors as well.

Students may also need instruction in asking for useful help. A teacher can model how to identify the confusing step, describe an attempted solution, and check a response against class notes. A vague request for help should not be assumed to reveal the underlying misconception.

Access and privacy also belong in the decision. Schools should review age requirements, accessibility, student information practices, and whether all participating students can use the intended features without purchasing a subscription.

These checks should include the students who will actually use the tool. An adult demonstration cannot reveal every difficulty a learner may encounter.

## Evaluate Learning Before Expanding Access

A pilot can begin with one subject, a defined learning goal, and an agreed period of use.

Identify the outcome before starting. Students might need to solve a related equation, explain vocabulary in context, or apply a concept to an unfamiliar example. Collect a starting measure, then check progress after practice and again later where feasible.

The follow-up should include new examples that require the same skill. Repeating an identical question may tell a teacher less about whether students can apply the underlying idea.

Include teacher observations and student feedback about confusing responses, technical barriers, and the amount of help needed. These details may help explain differences among learners and identify where additional support is required.

Be precise about what the pilot can establish. A simple before-and-after comparison cannot show that the tool caused an improvement. Students may also have received additional instruction or practice. A well-designed randomized trial can provide stronger evidence of cause and effect.

Even a modest pilot can reveal practical problems and help schools decide what to investigate further. Review the learning results alongside the staff time, training, and support required.

Costs should include the work of setting up accounts, helping students use the system, reviewing reports, and responding when the technology fails. A low subscription price alone does not describe the resources a school will need.

If students can explain a method and apply it to new work, teachers have a stronger basis for continuing the approach. If the same misconception keeps returning, the next step is to revisit the feedback and instruction before adding more time in the platform.
