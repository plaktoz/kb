# Can Food Solve Sleep Issues? New Research Reveals the Clues

Research published in Nature Health indicates that diets rich in fiber and a variety of whole plant foods are associated with improved sleep quality. While higher fiber intake correlates with more restorative sleep, caution is advised in interpreting the findings as definitive treatment solutions for sleep disorders.

## Impact of High Fiber Diets

A study involving 3,598 predominantly healthy adults analyzed 4,793 person-nights of dietary and sleep data derived from wearable technology. The results demonstrated that increased dietary fiber density was associated with a 0.59 percentage-point increase in deep sleep, a 0.76 percentage-point increase in REM sleep, a 1.35 percentage-point reduction in light sleep, and a 1.14 beats-per-minute decrease in average nighttime heart rate.

Based on these findings, physicians could recommend fiber-rich foods to their patients. Suitable options include:

- Beans and lentils
- Oatmeal and whole grains like barley and quinoa
- Chickpeas and black beans
- Berries, apples, and pears
- Vegetables, particularly those with skins
- Nuts and seeds

As noted by study author Hagai Rossman, these dietary choices significantly influence sleep architecture, making it easier for individuals to adopt a higher-fiber, plant-rich eating pattern that is associated with improved sleep.

What your patients eat directly influences their sleep architecture—making fiber-rich, plant-based diets a powerful, practical recommendation for better rest.

## Diversity of Plant Foods and Sleep

The research further highlighted a beneficial connection between a diverse intake of plant foods and lower nighttime heart rates. A wider variety of whole-plant foods was associated with better sleep measures. The study linked a higher Dietary Phytochemical Index to increased REM sleep and decreased light sleep, indicating that consuming a range of fruits, vegetables, legumes, whole grains, nuts, and seeds may be advantageous.

Marie-Pierre St-Onge, PhD, suggested that individuals with plant-forward diets may have distinct gut microbiomes that enhance the production of short-chain fatty acids, potentially affecting neurotransmitter signaling involved in sleep regulation.

## Timing of Food Intake and Sleep Quality

Interestingly, the study noted that participants who consumed a larger proportion of their daily calories in the six hours preceding sleep experienced a marginal increase in sleep duration—approximately 7.7 minutes—while their nighttime heart rate was elevated by about 0.73 beats per minute. This finding raises important considerations, as additional sleep does not necessarily equate to improved sleep quality.

Furthermore, research in 2026 suggested that finishing meals at least three hours before bedtime may offer benefits beyond mere sleep duration, although the effects on sleep quality were not the study's primary focus.

THE CNP TAKEAWAY

Addressing sleep issues may go beyond nighttime habits. Encouraging a diet abundant in fiber-rich, minimally processed plant foods could benefit sleep quality, offering a practical dietary approach for patients.
