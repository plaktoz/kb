# What Is The Current Evidence Into AI Tutoring And The Impact On Learners In School?

source_url: https://thirdspacelearning.com/blog/ai-tutoring-evidence

---

By Tom Hooper | Third Space Learning

Is AI tutoring a transformative way to scale up one-to-one support in school? The UK government thinks so — the Department for Education (DfE) announced plans in 2025 to trial AI tutoring tools with up to 450,000 disadvantaged students by 2027, one of the most significant EdTech commitments any government has made. Up until recently there has been little evidence specifically around AI tutoring, though research is now under way into the efficacy of different models.

## What makes traditional tutoring effective

The EEF identifies one-to-one and small-group tutoring as one of the most effective interventions available to schools, with an average impact of five additional months' progress. A 2026 review by Vanacore, Baker et al. examined what makes human tutoring so effective: the quality of the conversation. Effective tutors scaffold reasoning rather than explain answers, sustain motivation through dialogue, adjust in real time, and build trust that keeps students willing to struggle productively. Human tutoring cannot scale without quality dropping — finding, training and retaining enough high-quality human teachers is the binding constraint.

## What effective AI tutoring needs to look like

Adaptive learning platforms have personalised content pathways for years but are fundamentally non-conversational — they lack the reasoning and feedback loop that makes human tutoring effective. On-demand chat tools can converse but require students to self-regulate; research shows large language models tend toward over-helpfulness, reducing the metacognitive effort that produces actual learning. Effective AI tutoring systems need to be conversational, structured, curriculum-aligned, and built around human expertise — scaffolding reasoning rather than supplying answers.

## Where AI in education falls short

Bastani et al. (2025) conducted one of the first well-designed RCTs in this area, randomly assigning students to use ChatGPT to study (banned in the final exam to mirror real-world conditions). Students who used ChatGPT during study performed ~50% better in practice sessions, but ~17% worse in the exam — the most common student prompt was "Give me the answer." A qualitative evaluation of Khanmigo, Khan Academy's AI tutor, found it did not tailor tasks to individual students' needs and did not support metacognitive learning skills (Vanacore et al., 2026).

## Where carefully designed AI tutoring systems show promise

Kestin et al. (2025) published one of the strongest RCTs to date, comparing a carefully designed AI tutor with active learning in an introductory college physics course. The AI tutor produced median learning gains more than double those of the active learning control group, with effect sizes ranging from 0.73 to 1.3 standard deviations — among the largest ever recorded in higher education research. The context was higher education, not primary/secondary schools, so whether results transfer to younger students remains to be seen.

## Cognitive outsourcing and the case for purposeful design

Oakley and Sejnowski's Memory Paradox (2025) identifies "cognitive outsourcing" — when AI thinks for students, no learning takes place. The Eedi and Google DeepMind RCT (November 2025) tested LearnLM, an AI tutor using a Socratic approach that prompts students to identify their own mistakes; human tutors supervised every AI response, approving 82.3% of suggestions with no or minimal edits — a level of oversight that is not scalable.

## Evidence of within-session learning gains: Educate Ventures Research evaluation of Skye

An independent evaluation from Professor Rose Luckin's Educate Ventures Research analyzed 9,320 student sessions of Third Space Learning's AI maths tutor Skye. Students improved from 34% accuracy on diagnostic check-in questions to 92% on check-out assessments within a single session. Confidence data reinforced this: 63.8% of students' confidence outcomes ended higher than they began, with only 11.1% showing a decrease. This is not yet an estimate of standardised attainment impact — that would require a controlled design — but demonstrates strong, consistent within-session learning gains. Struggling and anxious/quieter students benefited particularly, though high-achieving learners also showed improved confidence.

## What the DfE expects from AI tutoring

The DfE's Generative AI: Product Safety Expectations and Generative AI in Education policy paper (both updated June 2025) require that student-facing generative AI tools must not provide direct answers or full solutions by default — progressive disclosure and hints that require students to attempt the problem first are the standard expectation. Human oversight remains a compliance requirement, and safeguarding (detecting signs of student distress) is a statutory duty under KCSIE 2025.

## The verdict

According to the DfE, only 25.6% of disadvantaged students achieved a strong pass in English and maths at GCSE in 2024–25, compared to 52.8% of non-disadvantaged peers. AI tutoring could help close that gap — but only if the systems schools adopt are built on evidence, not promise. Where AI tutoring is built to guide students, manage cognitive load and keep human expertise at the centre, the emerging evidence shows real improvements in learning outcomes. Where it hasn't, the evidence is equally clear — students do worse.
