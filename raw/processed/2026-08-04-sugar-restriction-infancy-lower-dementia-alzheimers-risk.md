# Less sugar during early life linked to 46% lower Alzheimer's risk

A new study published in *npj Aging* found that infants exposed to less sugar in their first 1,000 days of life had lower risks of developing Alzheimer's disease and all-cause dementia as adults.

Researchers analyzed health data from more than 60,000 UK Biobank participants born between 1951 and 1956 to study how the end of UK sugar rationing in 1953 affected later health — a natural experiment where people conceived during rationing consumed far less sugar in early life than those born shortly after, minimizing confounding from socioeconomic or behavioral factors that typically affect nutrition studies.

Adults who experienced sugar restriction during their first 1,000 days had a 46% lower Alzheimer's risk and 27% lower all-cause dementia risk. They also had 11% lower depression risk and 20% lower anxiety risk. Study author Bing Zhang, PhD, called it "the first human evidence that limiting sugar exposure in very early life is associated with substantial long-term protection against both neurodegenerative and psychiatric disorders."

Brain imaging showed that participants exposed to sugar rationing had brains that appeared, on average, 0.39 years younger than their chronological age, with larger volumes in regions including the hippocampus and thalamus — areas critical for memory and among the earliest affected by Alzheimer's.

Outside experts urged caution against over-interpreting the findings: the 46% figure was based on a relatively small number of Alzheimer's cases, and participants aren't yet at the age when Alzheimer's is most common; the depression and anxiety findings appeared somewhat stronger. The study also can't say whether restricting sugar in adulthood produces similar benefits, though reducing added sugar later in life may still support brain health indirectly by lowering obesity, insulin resistance, diabetes, and cardiovascular risk. One expert noted the goal shouldn't be eliminating all sugar — including from nutritious whole foods — but limiting excessive added sugars as part of a broader healthy lifestyle.
