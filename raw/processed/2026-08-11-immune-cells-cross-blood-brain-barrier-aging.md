# Immune cells cross blood-brain barrier during human aging

The brain contains specialized immune cells called microglia, whose origins in humans have been unclear. In mice, microglia precursors settle in the brain during embryonic development and are replenished from inside the brain thereafter. But recent evidence suggested aging human brains contain microglia that originate in bone marrow — implying microglia or their precursors can cross the blood-brain barrier, though how common this is wasn't clear.

An NIH-funded team from Stanford University (Drs. Julia Belk, Howard Chang, Siddhartha Jaiswal) investigated microglia origins using clonal hematopoiesis: bone marrow stem cells acquire unique mutation sets over a lifetime, passed to the cells they produce, so shared mutations between brain and blood cells indicate a bone-marrow origin. Published in Nature, July 30, 2026.

Examining postmortem brain tissue from 20 older adults, researchers found each individual had brain cells sharing mutations with their blood cells — the first clear evidence that bone-marrow-born cells often enter the brain in older adults. These bone-marrow-derived cells resembled microglia in key ways and made up a large share of brain microglia. Older individuals' brain tissue had more marrow-derived microglia than younger individuals', suggesting migration accumulates over a lifetime.

Analysis of a bone marrow transplant recipient confirmed the finding: their brain tissue contained microglia with the same mutations as their transplanted marrow cells.

The researchers also examined links between clonal hematopoiesis and Alzheimer's disease risk in tens of thousands of people, finding most types of clonal hematopoiesis were associated with reduced Alzheimer's risk.

Since the blood-brain barrier blocks many therapeutic molecules, stem cell transplant could offer a route to deliver treatments into the brain via engineered peripheral immune cells. "We have discovered a massive influx of immune cells from the blood into the human brain during aging," Belk said. "Our findings suggest new opportunities to engineer peripheral immune cells to treat or prevent neurological diseases."
