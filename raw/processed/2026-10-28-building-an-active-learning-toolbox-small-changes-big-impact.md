# Building an Active Learning Toolbox: Small Changes, Big Impact

Small instructional changes create the greatest impact. Learn how active learning strategies drive deeper student understanding and success.

When faculty hear the phrase "active learning," many assume it requires a complete redesign of their course. In reality, some of the most effective active learning strategies are also the simplest to implement.

Throughout my teaching career, I have learned that engagement increases when students are asked to do something with information rather than simply receive it. Whether students are discussing ideas, solving problems, teaching one another, or reflecting on concepts, they are more likely to retain knowledge and develop deeper understanding.

One strategy I frequently use is Think-Pair-Share. Students first consider a question independently, then discuss their ideas with a partner before sharing with the larger group. This simple structure increases participation because students have time to process their thinking before contributing.

Another favorite strategy is Jigsaw. In this activity, students become "experts" on one portion of a topic and then teach it to their peers. Not only does this encourage accountability, but it also promotes collaboration and deeper learning. Students often learn best when they are responsible for helping others understand a concept.

I am also a strong believer in micro-lectures. Rather than lecturing for an extended period, I break content into smaller segments and incorporate engagement checkpoints throughout. These pauses provide opportunities for discussion, reflection, problem-solving, or application of concepts before moving on.

What makes active learning so effective is that it shifts the focus from teaching to learning. Instead of asking, "How can I cover this material?" I ask, "What can students do with this information?" That subtle shift changes everything.

For instructors interested in incorporating more active learning, my advice is not to overhaul an entire course overnight. Instead, identify one lesson and add one active learning strategy. Start with a discussion prompt, a collaborative activity, or a reflective exercise. Observe how students respond and build from there.

The beauty of active learning is that it works across disciplines, modalities, and class sizes. Whether teaching healthcare, business, psychology, or communications, students benefit from opportunities to engage, collaborate, and apply their knowledge.

The most meaningful learning occurs when students are active participants rather than passive observers. Often, small instructional changes create the greatest impact on student engagement and success.
