# Mental Illness Is Clearly Influenced by Personality. But How?

Psychologists and psychiatrists have long considered the role of personality in understanding mental illness and treatment. Now, science is bridging the gap.

Where does personality end and mental illness begin?

While laypeople may understand personality as inherently subjective, described by a riot of adjectives that capture each person's idiosyncrasies, psychologists typically understand it through five broad traits supported by decades of research. These tendencies, known as the Big Five, are openness, conscientiousness, extroversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. All of them exist on a continuum, vary among individuals and tend to remain fairly stable throughout adulthood.

While the idea that personality and mental health conditions are closely intertwined may give some people pause, mental health professionals have long recognized that two patients with the same diagnosis may require very different treatment approaches depending on their personalities. Some practitioners even view personality assessments as integral to how they evaluate and treat patients. Now, science is offering more support. An accumulating body of research suggests that these ordinary differences in how we think, feel, behave and relate to others are closely connected to our vulnerability to mental illness.

One of the largest studies to date, a report published this year in the journal Nature, analyzed genetic data from about 1 million people and found more than 1,200 genetic markers associated with the Big Five personality traits. The results also found that all five types share genetic influences with mental illness.

Different extremes of personality traits carry different risk profiles for mental disorders. Neuroticism — the tendency to experience negative emotions and difficulty coping with stress — shows a strong genetic relationship with certain mental health conditions, most notably depression and anxiety. Genetic influences associated with conscientiousness, a trait linked to organization and self-discipline, appear to be associated with a lower risk of attention deficit hyperactivity disorder, or A.D.H.D., but an increased risk of obsessive-compulsive disorder and anorexia.

All of these links are far from deterministic, and the relationship between personality and mental illness is itself caught up in a complicated interplay of genes and environment. Even so, the connection increasingly informs how researchers understand mental disorders.

One example is the Hierarchical Taxonomy of Psychopathology, or HiTOP, a newer framework that organizes mental disorders based on patterns in how symptoms and traits occur together. Some of these dimensions closely resemble personality traits: a cluster of symptoms involving social withdrawal and detachment, for example, corresponds to low extroversion.
