# Oral Nutrition Supplement Interventions Show Short-Term Benefits in Patient Outcomes at Low Cost for Older Adults

A review of 12 economic studies finds predominantly favorable economic results, but longer-term evidence is needed

Lawrenceville, NJ, USA—November 25, 2026—Value in Health, the official journal of ISPOR—The Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research, announced today the publication of an ISPOR Special Interest Group report examining the health and economic effects of oral nutrition supplements in older adults, most with or at risk of malnutrition. The systematic review found that oral nutrition supplements use, often as part of broader nutrition interventions, was generally associated with modest short-term improvements in health outcomes, and was cost-effective or cost-saving, with economic benefits largely driven by reductions in healthcare resource utilization, such as fewer hospitalizations.

The article, "Systematic Review of Health Economic Evaluations of Oral Nutritional Supplement Interventions in Older Adults: An ISPOR Special Interest Group Report," by Amarsinh Desai, PhD, Olivia M. Dong, PhD, and colleagues in the ISPOR Nutrition Economics Key Project Working Group, appears in the November 2026 issue of Value in Health.

Older adults face an increased risk of malnutrition because of factors such as the accumulation of diseases and impairments over time, age-related physiological changes, and social factors, like isolation, that can impact dietary intake. Oral nutrition supplements, including drinks, powders, and other formats that provide specific nutrients, may be used under medical supervision when someone can eat by mouth but cannot meet nutritional needs through diet alone.

Earlier reviews of the economics of oral nutritional supplements included adults across a broad age range, focused on a narrow group of older adults, or drew on only one cost-effectiveness study.

The new ISPOR report brings together several types of economic evaluations across care settings to examine costs and health outcomes predominantly in older adults. By focusing on studies published from 2014 through 2024, the review reflects more recent approaches to diagnosing and managing malnutrition.

Seven studies reported per-patient cost savings ranging from $127 to $1988 in 2024 US dollars, primarily associated with reduced use of healthcare services. Five studies reported per-patient cost increases ranging from $356 to $672 in 2024 US dollars, mainly associated with the costs of oral nutrition supplements and other intervention components, including nutrition-related services. "Overall, studies reported favorable economic conclusions (cost-effective or cost-saving), which were largely driven by utilization-related cost offsets," wrote author Amarsinh Desai, PhD, Nestlé Health Science in Bridgewater, NJ, USA.

"Subgroup patterns suggest oral nutrition supplement interventions may offer the greatest economic value when targeted to higher-risk patients and implemented across care transitions, in which modest clinical improvements translate into reductions in hospital utilization," wrote author Olivia M. Dong, PhD, RTI Health Solutions, Durham, NC, USA. Nine studies reported favorable findings for their main health outcomes and measures of healthcare use, while 3 reported mixed results across several main outcomes. Outcomes examined across the studies varied and included hospital admissions, length of stay, nutritional status, clinical outcomes (such as pressure ulcer healing), life-years gained, and quality of life. The reported health gains were generally modest and assessed over short periods.

The studies in the ISPOR review covered the United States, United Kingdom, Australia, Italy, France, Spain, and Colombia, with 11 of the 12 conducted in high-income countries. Mean participant ages ranged from 63 to 88 years.

The authors cautioned that the long-term value of oral nutrition supplements and the intervention programs they were part of remains uncertain. Most studies assessed outcomes over 6 months or less. In 8 studies, oral nutrition supplements were part of broader intervention programs that included other components, such as dietary counseling, making it difficult to isolate the effect of oral nutrition supplements. Differences in study design, settings, and outcomes made it difficult to make comparisons across studies. The authors also noted gaps in how some studies reported their methods, and that most were industry-funded, which underscores the need for transparent reporting of data sources and uncertainty. Because the evidence comes largely from high-income countries, findings may not apply to other settings. Longer-term studies with more consistent reporting of key study methods could give healthcare decision makers a clearer picture of the lasting value of oral nutrition supplements.

Overall, the findings support the targeted consideration of oral nutrition supplements as a potentially valuable strategy for improving outcomes in older adults at low or no net cost to healthcare systems, while highlighting the need for longer-term, methodologically robust studies that take a societal perspective, capturing costs such as caregiver time and out-of-pocket expenses, and that include a wider range of settings, such as low- and middle-income countries.
