# Battle Royale: Five AI Teacher Tools Try to Make the Same Worksheet

## Trying to understand Gallup’s finding that teachers still aren’t using generative AI in meaningful numbers.

At the end of the last school year, Gallup asked K-12 educators, effectively, “Did you use any artificial intelligence last year?” 59% said yes, which means 41% of surveyed teachers didn’t use any AI at all.

We are one year out from edtech insider Alex Sarlin’s claim that, “it’s going to be hard to find an educator who doesn’t use generative AI on a weekly basis.” We are two years out from Sal Khan’s claim that “generative AI tools will cut 90% of teachers’ admin tasks.” Four years out from the release of ChatGPT, why is there such a mismatch between the expectations of technologists and the reality of teacher needs?

## This teacher needs help.

I was working in a fifth-grade class recently. We were subtracting mixed numbers, which is a fantastic blend of procedural and conceptual knowledge.

Kids need to understand what a fraction is, whether it’s more or less than 1, whether it’s more or less than another fraction. They’ll pull in ideas about decomposing and regrouping from other subtraction problems. They’ll multiply, add, and subtract a lot.

The teacher had a mini-lesson worksheet ready for students who needed more work with the operations. It’s a great little supportive worksheet, but you can see parts that were clearly challenging—namely layout and math typesetting.

I hope we’d all agree that teachers shouldn’t have to do this kind of supplementary work. Ideally they’d be able to pull that kind of mini-lesson out of a core curriculum like the one I work on, Amplify Desmos Math. Look at this beautiful baby with its faded scaffolds.

## Can AI help this teacher?

Let’s say our teacher doesn’t have that kind of curriculum, though. Let’s say she turns to AI tools like Magic School, Brisk, Diffit, or frontier models from Anthropic and OpenAI.

She might describe her worksheet above and say, “Make it for me.” I sent the same prompts into each of those systems and received these worksheets.

I scrubbed their identifiers and shared them on a couple of social networks, asking people, “Which of these would you assign?” and “Which of these do you think is best?”

The results are interesting, but even more interesting are the reasons people gave for their preferences:

Diffit: “No space for work. I would assign if more space was given”

ChatGPT: “I don’t like the slanted fractions. They should be flat bar fractions.”

Brisk Teaching: “The order of the fraction changes from horizontal to vertical.”

I asked the LLM in Brisk Teaching to “make the fractions appear on the same line, in the same order as they appear in the original expression.” The result? Horizontal fractions that extended beyond the width of the printable page.

## Why More Teachers Aren’t Using These Tools

Why does generative AI still lack the teacher usage enjoyed by email software, attendance software, digital projectors, or document cameras—technologies that have been in heavy use in 100% of the classes I visited in the 25-26 school year.

My hypothesis: most teachers would prefer materials that are a predictable B- to a probabilistic A+.

I don’t think many teachers have the time or inclination to fiddle—to translate a handout from their imagination into words and then fiddle with whatever an LLM produces from those words.

Fiddling is fun for a lot of people. You enter a slightly different prompt and get a result that is slightly closer to what you want. For a lot of people—especially people who work in technology—that process is a happy one, even addictive, like a slot machine. For teachers—overworked and underresourced; class starting in an hour—I suspect that generative AI’s fiddly nature releases more cortisol than dopamine, more stress than relief.

## Odds & Ends

¶ My respect only grows for math practice platform Eedi and Bibi Groot, their Chief Impact Officer. They do rigorous research and they’re transparent about their results, even when those results dump cold water on prevailing wisdom about artificial intelligence in education. In their recent report, they found that their AI tutor did as well as their human tutors on near-term measures of learning (nice, given AI is often more accessible than humans) but on longer-term measures neither AI or human tutors outperformed the 2010s-era technology of videos and pre-constructed hints. That lines up with the finding out of Tennessee that the Khanmigo AI added negligible value to the standard Khan Academy experience.

¶ In another study with nice controls, the addition of a chatbot to the learning management system at the University of Maryland resulted in decreases to student final grades and participation with other course resources—e.g. discussions and online assignments. “Of the 3,460 requests students sent to the [virtual study assistant], 73.8% were classified as direct-answer requests.” Instructors added course materials—syllabi, notes, etc—into the chatbot’s RAG pipeline, which is about as advanced as you’d want. Chatbot usage increased right before exams but doesn’t seem to have improved exam performance.

¶ The term “cognitive offloading” is well-understood. You also see “political offloading,” where people @grok on Twitter to give them an AI-generated final word on profoundly uncertain questions of values, definitions, and policy. In a similar spirit, I appreciated this mom who called a teacher out for what we might call “imaginative offloading,” telling kids to create AI-generated images depicting themselves in hypothetical careers. It seems like malpractice to limit a kid’s imagination to the biases and probabilities of an LLM.

I don’t want my daughter to see AI as something magical before teaching her what it does (or ever). I don’t want to outsource her own imagination.
