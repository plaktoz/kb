# Online learning motivation among secondary vocational students in China through an integrating technology acceptance and Community of Inquiry framework

## Abstract

This study examines the relationships among technology-related beliefs, Community of Inquiry (CoI) dimensions, and online learning motivation (OLM) among secondary vocational students in China by integrating the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and CoI framework. Survey data from 962 students were analysed using partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) to investigate how self-efficacy (SE), perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (EU), and CoI dimensions relate to students’ motivation for online learning. The findings indicate that SE was the strongest factor associated with OLM, followed by PU and EU. Consistent with TAM, EU was positively associated with PU. Within the CoI framework, teaching presence (TP) showed a significant relationship with OLM and demonstrated significant indirect effects in the relationships between SE/EU and OLM. In contrast, social presence (SP) and cognitive presence (CP) did not demonstrate significant mediating effects. This study contributes to TAM and CoI research by providing context-specific evidence that teaching presence may represent an important motivational pathway in secondary vocational online learning environments. The findings suggest that effective online learning design should integrate accessible technologies with structured instructional support and confidence-building strategies.

## 1 Introduction

Vocational education in China stands at a pivotal moment of transformation, as the nation strives to cultivate a highly skilled and digitally literate workforce aligned with the demands of the modern economy [1, 2]. At the heart of this shift is the strategic integration of online learning, propelled by national initiatives such as the Education Informatisation 2.0 Action Plan and the Modern Vocational Education System Construction Plan [3]. These national policies have accelerated the integration of online platforms, highlighting the need for adaptive, accessible, and skill-oriented learning models ([4]; World Economic Forum, [5]). In secondary vocational education, incorporating real-world applications and practical simulations in online modules has been shown to strengthen students’ perceptions of the learning activity’s value [6, 7].

Motivation remains a key determinant of student engagement and success in online learning environments [8]. However, vocational learners often face unique challenges, including varied digital literacy, limited infrastructure in rural areas, and a need for hands-on training, which may influence their perceptions and motivation toward online learning [4, 9, 10]. These disparities raise critical questions about how online learning can be effectively implemented and sustained in vocational contexts.

Research on student motivation in online learning has mainly relied on the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM), which highlights perceived usefulness (PU), ease of use (EU), and self-efficacy (SE) as important factors influencing technology adoption [11,12,13]. At the same time, the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework, including teaching presence (TP), social presence (SP), and cognitive presence (CP), provides a complementary view centred on the learning environment [14, 15]. Despite their relevance, few studies have integrated TAM and CoI to explain online learning motivation in vocational education. Prior research often treats these models in isolation, overlooks mediation effects [16, 17], or excludes vocational learners altogether [18, 19]. This creates a significant gap in understanding how technology acceptance and learning presence interact to shape motivation. To address this, the present study develops and tests an integrated TAM-CoI model to explain online learning motivation among secondary vocational students in China. Specifically, it investigates:

The relationship between self-efficacy, ease of use, and perceived usefulness in vocational education contexts.

To examine the effects of self-efficacy, ease of use, and perceived usefulness on online learning motivation.

To investigate the mediating effects of teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence on the relationships between TAM factors and online learning motivation.

This study contributes to the literature by integrating the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM; [20]) and Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework [14] to develop a more comprehensive understanding of online learning motivation in vocational education. Specifically, the study investigates the mediating effects of CoI dimensions and examines the relationships between self-efficacy, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and online learning motivation. Additionally, the study provides context-specific insights for vocational educators by highlighting the importance of user-friendly technologies and confidence-building interventions in designing effective online learning environments.

## 2 Literature review and hypotheses

## 2.1 Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Community of Inquiry (CoI)

This study integrates the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework to examine online learning motivation among secondary vocational students. TAM, proposed by Davis [21], explains users’ acceptance of technology based on perceived usefulness (PU) and ease of use (EU). In educational settings, self-efficacy (SE) has been identified as an influential addition to these constructs, significantly shaping students’ beliefs and behaviours regarding technology use [22, 23]. Research also indicates that online learning engagement behaviour is primarily influenced by perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, learning self-efficacy, and other factors [24].

The CoI framework developed by Garrison et al., [14] complements TAM by conceptualising online learning through teaching presence (TP), social presence (SP), and cognitive presence (CP). These three presences form a dynamic learning environment that supports knowledge construction, interpersonal interaction, and instructional guidance. Prior studies confirm the positive impact of TP, SP, and CP on student engagement, satisfaction, and cognitive effort in online education [25, 26]. This study positions TP, SP, and CP as mediators between TAM factors and online learning motivation (OLM) while also examining their direct contributions to students’ motivational outcomes. Whereas engagement is typically associated with behavioural participation [27], motivation reflects internal drivers such as goal orientation, interest, and value perception [28]. Motivation influences students’ willingness to begin, persist in, and regulate their online learning. Meaningful and contextually relevant learning activities significantly enhance student motivation and participation [29]. Similarly, higher levels of motivation in online settings lead to improved academic outcomes [30].

## 2.2 Online learning motivation

Online learning motivation refers to students’ internal psychological processes that influence their willingness, effort, and persistence in participating in online learning activities. Unlike behavioural engagement, which reflects observable participation such as attendance and interaction, motivation concerns the underlying reasons that initiate and sustain learning behaviours [28]. Within online learning environments, motivation represents students’ perceived value of learning activities, willingness to invest effort, and persistence when encountering learning challenges. This distinction is important because students may have access to online technologies and participate in learning activities without necessarily possessing the motivational orientation required for sustained learning. From the perspective of self-determination theory, motivation can involve both intrinsic and extrinsic orientations, where learners may engage in online learning because they find learning activities meaningful or because they perceive external benefits such as academic achievement and career development [28]. Although intrinsic and extrinsic motivation represent distinct motivational orientations, both reflect students’ reasons for engaging in learning activities and can influence persistence and effort in online environments [28]. In vocational education contexts, motivation may be particularly influenced by students’ perceptions of the practical relevance of learning activities because vocational learners often pursue competencies directly connected to future occupational roles [31, 32]. Therefore, online learning motivation reflects students’ willingness to actively engage with digital learning environments when they perceive learning activities as valuable, achievable, and aligned with their educational goals.

Previous research has demonstrated that technology-related beliefs and learning environment factors contribute to students’ motivation in online contexts. For example, perceived usefulness and ease of use influence students’ willingness to engage with online learning systems because technologies that are perceived as valuable and manageable reduce barriers to participation [13, 20]. Similarly, self-efficacy contributes to motivation by strengthening students’ confidence in their ability to successfully complete online learning tasks [33]. From a Community of Inquiry perspective, teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence provide the environmental conditions that support motivated learning by offering instructional guidance, social connection, and opportunities for meaningful knowledge construction [14]. In this study, online learning motivation is conceptualised as students’ motivational orientation toward participating and persisting in online learning activities. The measurement items were adapted from the validated scale developed by Zuo et al. [34], which captures students’ motivational tendencies within online learning environments. Therefore, OLM is examined as the outcome variable through which technology acceptance factors (PU, EU, and SE) and CoI dimensions (TP, SP, and CP) contribute to students’ online learning experiences.

## 2.3 Community of Inquiry and online learning motivation

The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework provides an important theoretical explanation of how online learning environments influence students’ online learning motivation (OLM). While TAM explains students’ acceptance and use of learning technologies, CoI explains how the quality of online learning experiences generated through teaching, social, and cognitive processes may strengthen students’ willingness and persistence in learning activities. According to Garrison et al. [14], meaningful online learning emerges through the interaction of teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence. These dimensions do not only support knowledge construction but also shape students’ willingness to participate, persist, and invest effort in online learning activities. Teaching presence refers to instructors’ capacity to design, organise, facilitate, and provide guidance throughout the learning process [14]. In online environments, effective instructional design, timely feedback, and clear learning expectations can reduce uncertainty and enhance students’ confidence in completing learning tasks [35]. From a motivational perspective, strong teaching presence may increase students’ perceived competence and value of learning activities, thereby encouraging sustained participation and persistence. This relationship may be particularly relevant in secondary vocational education, where students often require structured demonstrations, practical guidance, and instructor support when developing occupational competencies. Therefore, teaching presence is expected to positively influence online learning motivation.

Social presence reflects learners’ ability to interact with others, communicate effectively, and develop a sense of belonging within an online learning community [14]. Online learning can create feelings of isolation when opportunities for interaction are limited; however, meaningful communication with peers and instructors can provide emotional support and strengthen learners’ engagement with learning activities [26]. In line with self-determination theory, social interaction may satisfy students’ need for relatedness, which contributes to stronger motivation and persistence [28, 36]. Therefore, higher social presence is expected to enhance online learning motivation. Cognitive presence concerns students’ ability to construct meaning through reflection, inquiry, and problem-solving [14]. When online learning activities encourage students to analyse information, apply knowledge, and solve meaningful problems, learners are more likely to perceive learning as valuable and personally relevant. This process is especially important in vocational education because students are often motivated by opportunities to acquire practical competencies connected to future occupational roles. Therefore, cognitive presence may strengthen online learning motivation by promoting meaningful engagement and deeper learning processes. Based on these theoretical assumptions, this study proposes that CoI dimensions contribute positively to online learning motivation:

H1: Teaching presence has a positive effect on online learning motivation.

H2: Social presence has a positive effect on online learning motivation.

H3: Cognitive presence has a positive effect on online learning motivation.

## 2.4 Perceived usefulness (PU)

In online learning, perceived usefulness (PU) refers to students’ belief that digital platforms can enhance their academic performance and learning outcomes [37, 38]. PU is particularly relevant in vocational education, where learners often seek practical, career-aligned competencies and evaluate learning technologies based on their relevance to future occupational requirements [31, 32]. When students perceive online learning platforms as useful for achieving academic and professional goals, they are more likely to demonstrate greater motivation and engagement in learning activities [39]. Beyond its direct relationship with motivation, PU may influence students’ perceptions of the online learning environment through the dimensions of the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework. First, perceived usefulness may strengthen teaching presence (TP) because students who recognise the educational value of online platforms may perceive instructional activities as more organised, purposeful, and aligned with their learning needs. In vocational contexts, useful technologies may enhance students’ perceptions that instructors provide relevant guidance, resources, and demonstrations that support occupational skill development. Second, PU may contribute to social presence (SP) by encouraging students to participate more actively in online interactions. When students perceive digital platforms as valuable learning tools, they may be more willing to engage in discussion forums, collaborative activities, and communication channels that facilitate interaction with peers and instructors. Third, PU may influence cognitive presence (CP) by supporting meaningful knowledge construction. Although useful technologies can improve access to learning resources and learning activities, their contribution to deeper reflection and knowledge development depends on whether students engage in inquiry, analysis, and application of knowledge. In this regard, useful online platforms may provide conditions that facilitate cognitive engagement [14]. Therefore, perceived usefulness is expected to influence students’ perceptions of teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence, as well as their overall motivation toward online learning activities. Based on these theoretical assumptions, the following hypotheses are proposed:

H4: Perceived usefulness has a positive effect on cognitive presence.

H5: Perceived usefulness has a positive effect on social presence.

H6: Perceived usefulness has a positive direct effect on teaching presence.H7: Perceived usefulness has a positive effect on online learning motivation (OLM).

H8: Perceived usefulness has a positive effect on OLM through social presence, teaching presence, and cognitive presence.

## 2.5 Perceived ease of use (EU)

The EU represents the extent to which students find online platforms user-friendly and easy to navigate. Studies have shown that intuitive platforms enhance user satisfaction and reduce technological barriers, thereby supporting sustained engagement [40, 41]. In the CoI context, the EU facilitates: CP, by reducing cognitive load and enabling focus on content; SP, by making communication tools more accessible; and TP, by enabling effective course design and delivery [42, 43]. Improving the EU in online vocational learning requires simplifying user interfaces and offering technical support [44]. From a CoI perspective, perceived ease of use may influence students’ perceptions of the online learning environment because technologies that are easier to navigate reduce cognitive and technical barriers, allowing students to focus more effectively on interaction, instructional guidance, and knowledge construction. Therefore, perceived ease of use may contribute to teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence. Based on these insights, the following hypotheses are formulated:

H9: Ease of use has a positive effect on cognitive presence.

H10: Ease of use has a positive effect on social presence.

H11: Ease of use has a positive effect on teaching presence.

H12: Ease of use has a positive effect on online learning motivation (OLM).

H13: Ease of use has a positive effect on OLM through social presence, teaching presence, and cognitive presence.

## 2.6 Self-efficacy (SE)

Self-efficacy represents students’ confidence in using technological tools effectively, which influences their adoption and sustained use of educational technologies [45]. High self-efficacy is associated with greater digital engagement, active learning, and improved academic performance, while low self-efficacy can lead to avoidance behaviours, reduced persistence, and suboptimal learning outcomes [46, 47].

Self-efficacy enhances students’ cognitive presence, as students are more likely to approach tasks with a problem-solving mindset and persist through challenges [45, 48]. It also positively affects social presence, enabling students to participate confidently in peer interactions and collaborative activities [49]. Similarly, high self-efficacy contributes to teaching presence, as students actively respond to instructional cues and align their efforts with the course objectives [50]. Students with high self-efficacy are more likely to engage in meaningful discourse, persist in understanding complex materials, and actively respond to instructor guidance [51].

Vocational students often aim to develop career-specific competencies, and those with high self-efficacy demonstrate a greater ability to leverage learning tools to meet these goals [50, 52]. Research highlights that strategies such as fostering early success experiences, providing collaborative opportunities, and offering continuous instructor support can enhance students’ confidence in their technological abilities [53,54,55]. Additionally, iterative improvements in platform design based on student feedback further bolster self-efficacy by reducing technological barriers and promoting user satisfaction. Self-efficacy can significantly impact students’ online learning motivation (OLM) by fostering a sense of competence and readiness to engage in digital learning experiences. This influence could further be mediated by the CoI framework’s core constructs, emphasizing the interconnectedness of these elements in driving motivation [56, 57]. These insights inform the following hypotheses:

H14: Self-efficacy has a positive effect on cognitive presence.

H15: Self-efficacy has a positive effect on social presence.

H16: Self-efficacy has a positive effect on teaching presence.

H17: Self-efficacy positively affects Online Learning Motivation.

H18: Self-efficacy has a positive effect on OLM through social presence, teaching presence, and cognitive presence.

Additionally, the study by Zuo et al. [58] further argued that TP influences both CP and SP. Similarly, they found that SP has a significant effect on CP. This study investigates this relationship further by suggesting the following hypothesis:

H19: Teaching presence has a positive impact on cognitive presence.

H20: Teaching presence has a positive impact on social presence.

H21: Social presence has a positive impact on cognitive presence.

## 2.7 Interrelationships among usefulness (PU), ease of use (EU), and self-efficacy (SE)

Within TAM, perceived usefulness refers to the belief that online learning improves academic performance, while ease of use reflects how effortless the system is to operate [20]. EU has been shown to positively influence PU by reducing cognitive load and enhancing user confidence [13, 22, 59]. Self-efficacy also shapes PU and EU. Students with higher SE tend to find systems easier to use [33, 60] and are more likely to perceive them as useful for achieving learning goals [22]. However, some research shows that SE’s effect on PU may vary with context [23]. In vocational online learning, strengthening SE through user-centred design and support can positively influence both EU and PU. Based on the above discussion, the following hypotheses can be formulated in relation to online learning environments:

H22: Self-efficacy has a significant positive effect on Perceived Usefulness.

H23: Self-efficacy has a significant positive effect on Perceived Ease of Use.

H24: Perceived Ease of Use has a significant positive effect on Perceived Usefulness.

## 3 Methods

## 3.1 Strategy and participants

We employed a quantitative approach, analysing data from 962 secondary vocational students in China. Participants were primarily freshmen (62.9%) and sophomores (36.9%), with 86.5% female and 13.5% male representation (Table 1). A convenience sampling method was used to distribute an online questionnaire to students. While convenience sampling limits generalizability, it is efficient for exploratory research [61]. After data cleaning, only valid responses were retained. The study examined online learning models: synchronous (live online lectures, MOOCs, and discussions) and asynchronous (in-person learning supplemented by online activities). All courses used China’s Star platform (Chaoxing) for structured digital delivery, integrating discussions, assignments, and resources.

## 3.2 Instruments

The questionnaire was adapted from the validated instrument developed by Zuo et al. [34], which integrates constructs from the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework to assess students’ online learning experiences. The instrument consisted of seven constructs: self-efficacy (SE), perceived usefulness (PU), perceived ease of use (EU), teaching presence (TP), social presence (SP), cognitive presence (CP), and online learning motivation (OLM). Self-efficacy was measured using six items assessing students’ confidence in their ability to effectively use online learning technologies and complete online learning tasks. Perceived usefulness consisted of two items measuring students’ perceptions that online learning platforms improve learning effectiveness and academic outcomes. Perceived ease of use was assessed using three items capturing students’ perceptions regarding the simplicity and usability of online learning platforms.

The CoI dimensions were measured through teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence. Teaching presence included seven items assessing students’ perceptions of instructional design, teacher facilitation, and guidance during online learning activities. Social presence consisted of seven items measuring students’ ability to interact, communicate, and develop connections with peers and instructors in online environments. Cognitive presence included seven items evaluating students’ engagement in reflection, inquiry, and knowledge construction through online learning activities. Online learning motivation was measured using eight items adapted from Zuo et al. [34], assessing students’ willingness, interest, effort, persistence, and perceived value in participating in online learning activities. The scale captures students’ motivational orientation toward engaging with and sustaining participation in online learning rather than their intention to accept or use technology. Therefore, OLM was conceptualised as a learning-related motivational construct that is theoretically distinct from TAM variables such as perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use. All items were measured using a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree). The original instrument was translated into Chinese using a forward–backward translation procedure to ensure linguistic accuracy and conceptual equivalence. A pilot review was conducted to confirm item clarity and contextual suitability for secondary vocational students. The reliability and validity of the measurement model were evaluated using factor loadings, Cronbach’s alpha (α), composite reliability (CR), average variance extracted (AVE), and discriminant validity assessments. The results demonstrated satisfactory internal consistency and convergent validity; however, discriminant validity concerns were identified among several conceptually related constructs and are discussed in Sect. 4.1.2.

## 3.3 Data analysis

The analysis generated descriptive statistics using SPSS version 29, and partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) using SmartPLS 4.0.8. PLS-SEM was selected for its robustness with small samples and complex mediation models [62], while effectively handling both formative and reflective constructs [63]. The measurement model was evaluated using reliability, convergent validity, discriminant validity, and collinearity assessment following PLS-SEM guidelines. The model exhibited strong reliability, with all constructs exceeding thresholds for composite reliability (CR > 0.7) and Cronbach’s alpha (α > 0.7). Convergent validity was established through average variance extracted (AVE > 0.5) and high indicator loadings (> 0.800). Discriminant validity was confirmed via the Fornell-Larcker criterion, with √AVE exceeding inter-construct correlations (Table 3). Multicollinearity assessment led to the removal of one perceived usefulness item (PU3) due to high VIF (> 10) [64,65,66]. Model fit indices yielded excellent results (SRMR = 0.024), supported by supplementary metrics, including NFI and the chi-square value. Predictive relevance was confirmed through Q² predict values and error measures (RMSE, MAE). Bayesian Information Criterion evaluation ensured optimal model complexity. Effect sizes (ƒ²) were interpreted as: 0.02 (small), 0.15 (medium), and 0.35 (large). The comprehensive validation process followed established guidelines [67, 68], ensuring robust examination of hypothesised relationships while maintaining statistical rigour throughout the analytical framework.

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Measurement model assessment

4.1.1 Indicator reliability and convergent validity

The measurement model was assessed by examining indicator reliability, internal consistency reliability, and convergent validity. Indicator reliability was evaluated through outer loadings, while internal consistency reliability was assessed using Cronbach’s alpha (α), rho_A, and composite reliability (rho_C). Convergent validity was examined using average variance extracted (AVE) [65, 68]. As shown in Table 2, all indicators demonstrated satisfactory outer loadings above the recommended threshold of 0.70, indicating adequate indicator reliability [68]. All constructs achieved Cronbach’s alpha, rho_A, and composite reliability values exceeding the recommended minimum thresholds, demonstrating satisfactory internal consistency reliability ([68]; Nunnally & Bernstein, 1994). However, several constructs exhibited exceptionally high reliability coefficients (α = 0.954–0.981), suggesting potential item redundancy and possible overlap among indicators. Item deletion was not undertaken because all retained indicators demonstrated strong theoretical alignment with their respective constructs [68]. All AVE values exceeded the recommended threshold of 0.50, supporting convergent validity [67, 68]. Nevertheless, the exceptionally high reliability coefficients and subsequent discriminant validity results suggest that some constructs may share overlapping measurement content. Therefore, these findings are interpreted cautiously, and potential item redundancy is acknowledged as a limitation of the measurement model.

4.1.2 Discriminant validity

Discriminant validity was assessed using the Fornell–Larcker criterion and the heterotrait–monotrait ratio (HTMT). As presented in Table 3, the Fornell–Larcker criterion was satisfied for most constructs. However, a potential discriminant validity concern was observed for Online Learning Motivation (OLM). Specifically, the correlations between OLM and Perceived Usefulness (PU; r = .921) and Self-efficacy (SE; r = .939) exceeded the square root of OLM’s average variance extracted (√AVE = 0.918), indicating insufficient discriminant validity between these constructs according to the Fornell–Larcker criterion [67]. The HTMT results (Table 4) provided further evidence of construct overlap, with several construct pairs exceeding the recommended threshold of 0.90 [65]. The highest HTMT values were observed for OLM–SE (0.964), PU–SE (0.959), OLM–PU (0.956), and CP–SP (0.954). Nevertheless, the bootstrapped 95% confidence intervals for these construct pairs did not include 1.00, suggesting that complete lack of discriminant validity was not established. However, the elevated HTMT point estimates indicate substantial shared variance among several constructs, particularly among OLM, PU, and SE. Therefore, although the measurement model demonstrated acceptable reliability and convergent validity, the findings suggest that some constructs exhibit conceptual and empirical overlap. These results are interpreted cautiously, and potential construct redundancy among OLM, PU, and SE is acknowledged as a limitation of the study. Given the observed overlap among some constructs, particularly self-efficacy, perceived usefulness, and online learning motivation, the structural relationships involving these variables should be interpreted as associations rather than definitive evidence of distinct theoretical effects.

## 4.2 Structural model assessment

Following the assessment of the measurement model, the structural model was evaluated by examining explanatory power, predictive capability, predictive accuracy, and model discrepancy indicators. These assessments focus on the performance of the structural model rather than construct reliability and validity, which are evaluated during measurement model assessment [68, 69]. The explanatory power of the endogenous constructs was assessed using the coefficient of determination (R²). Online Learning Motivation (OLM) demonstrated substantial explanatory power, with an adjusted R² value of 0.919, indicating that the predictors in the model explained 91.9% of the variance in OLM. Cognitive Presence (CP) and Teaching Presence (TP) also demonstrated strong explanatory power, with adjusted R² values of 0.904 and 0.807, respectively. These findings indicate that the structural model provides substantial explanatory capability for the endogenous constructs [68]. Predictive capability was assessed using Q²_predict, while predictive accuracy was examined using root mean square error (RMSE) and mean absolute error (MAE) values. All endogenous constructs demonstrated positive Q²_predict values, including 0.905 for OLM, 0.754 for CP, 0.707 for Social Presence (SP), and 0.649 for TP, indicating predictive relevance of the model. RMSE and MAE values were examined as indicators of prediction error, where lower values indicate better predictive accuracy [69].

Model discrepancy indicators were also examined to evaluate the correspondence between observed and estimated model-implied correlations. The structural model produced an SRMR value of 0.024, indicating a low level of residual discrepancy. However, d_ULS and d_G values were interpreted based on their bootstrapped confidence intervals rather than absolute threshold values. The original d_ULS value (0.482) exceeded both the 95% (0.166) and 99% (0.194) bootstrap reference values, while the original d_G value (1.015) exceeded the corresponding 95% (0.640) and 99% (0.682) reference values. Although SRMR indicated minimal discrepancy, the d_ULS and d_G results suggest that model discrepancy should be considered when evaluating overall model adequacy. Therefore, these indices were treated as supplementary evidence rather than as standalone criteria for determining model quality. 4.2.1 Hypothesis testing for direct relationships

Table 5 presents the results of the direct relationship assessment using bootstrapping with 5,000 subsamples. The findings indicate that perceived usefulness (PU) significantly predicted social presence (SP) (β = 0.141, p < .05), teaching presence (TP) (β = 0.172, p < .05), and online learning motivation (OLM) (β = 0.242, p < .001), supporting H5, H6, and H7, respectively. However, PU did not significantly predict cognitive presence (CP) (β = 0.014, p > .05); therefore, H4 was not supported. Perceived ease of use (EU) significantly predicted SP (β = 0.116, p < .05), TP (β = 0.320, p < .001), and OLM (β = 0.121, p < .05), supporting H10, H11, and H12. EU also significantly predicted PU (β = 0.284, p < .001), supporting H24. However, EU did not significantly predict CP (β = 0.056, p > .05), resulting in H9 not being supported. Self-efficacy (SE) demonstrated significant positive effects on CP (β = 0.187, p < .001), TP (β = 0.343, p < .001), OLM (β = 0.416, p < .001), PU (β = 0.670, p < .001), and EU (β = 0.898, p < .001), supporting H14, H16, H17, H22, and H23. However, SE did not significantly predict SP (β = 0.106, p > .05); therefore, H15 was not supported. Regarding the relationships among the Community of Inquiry (CoI) dimensions, TP significantly predicted CP (β = 0.173, p < .001) and SP (β = 0.609, p < .001), supporting H19 and H20. Furthermore, SP significantly predicted CP (β = 0.570, p < .001), supporting H21. These findings suggest that TP serves as an important antecedent within the CoI framework by contributing to both social and cognitive learning processes. For the direct effects of CoI dimensions on OLM, TP demonstrated a significant positive relationship with OLM (β = 0.086, p < .05), supporting H1. In contrast, SP did not significantly predict OLM (β = 0.044, p > .05), and CP showed a positive but non-significant relationship with OLM (β = 0.101, p > .05). Therefore, H2 and H3 were not supported.

4.2.2 Hypothesis testing of mediating relationships

Mediating relationships were assessed using specific indirect effects based on 5,000 bootstrap subsamples. Statistical significance was determined using bias-corrected 95% bootstrap confidence intervals, with an indirect effect considered significant when the confidence interval did not include zero. The results indicated that teaching presence significantly mediated the relationship between perceived usefulness and online learning motivation (β = 0.015, BC 95% CI [0.001, 0.040]), supporting H8c. In contrast, the indirect effects of perceived usefulness through social presence and cognitive presence were not significant. Teaching presence also significantly mediated the relationships between perceived ease of use and online learning motivation (β = 0.028, BC 95% CI [0.008, 0.054]) and between self-efficacy and online learning motivation (β = 0.030, BC 95% CI [0.008, 0.065]), supporting H13b and H18b, respectively (see Table 6). Although several additional indirect pathways involving social and cognitive presence reached statistical significance, the hypothesized mediation effects involving these dimensions were generally weaker and less consistent. Overall, the findings suggest that teaching presence played a central but selective mediating role in explaining how technology-related perceptions and self-efficacy influenced online learning motivation. However, the significant indirect effects were small in magnitude and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

## 5 Discussion and implications of findings

This study examined how self-efficacy (SE), perceived ease of use (EU), and perceived usefulness (PU) were associated with online learning motivation (OLM) among secondary vocational students and how these relationships were explained through the Community of Inquiry (CoI) dimensions. By integrating the Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) and CoI framework, this study provides insight into how students’ personal beliefs, perceptions of technology, and online learning experiences interact within a vocational education context. The findings are based on 962 students from Chinese secondary vocational high schools, primarily from grades 10 and 11. Although the sample was predominantly female, the findings provide useful insights into early-stage vocational learners who are increasingly required to engage with digital learning environments. The discussion that follows highlights how technology-related perceptions and learning experiences may contribute to students’ motivation for online learning.

## 5.1 The role of PU, EU, and SE in fostering motivation for online learning

Self-efficacy was identified as an important factor associated with online learning motivation. This finding is consistent with Bandura’s [33] social cognitive theory, which proposes that individuals’ beliefs about their capabilities shape their motivation, effort, and persistence when engaging in tasks. Previous studies have similarly reported that students with stronger confidence in their ability to use digital technologies are more likely to demonstrate greater engagement and persistence in online learning environments [24, 45,46,47, 50]. In the present study, self-efficacy was also associated with teaching presence and cognitive presence, suggesting that students’ confidence in managing online learning activities may be related to how they perceive instructional support and learning processes within digital environments. However, self-efficacy was not significantly related to social presence. This finding differs from previous research suggesting that confident learners are more likely to participate in online interaction and collaborative activities [49]. One possible explanation is that confidence in using online technologies does not necessarily translate into social interaction. In structured vocational learning environments, students may use online platforms primarily for accessing materials, completing assignments, or following teacher-directed activities rather than engaging in peer-based communication. Therefore, opportunities for social interaction may depend more on how online activities are designed than on students’ individual confidence alone.

Perceived usefulness was also positively associated with online learning motivation, supporting previous research emphasizing the role of perceived value and relevance in technology acceptance and learning engagement [24, 70,71,72]. This finding suggests that vocational students may be more motivated to engage in online learning when they recognize its practical value and relevance to their educational goals. However, perceived usefulness was not associated with cognitive presence. This indicates that viewing online technologies as beneficial may not automatically involve deeper cognitive processes, such as reflection, knowledge construction, and problem-solving. In vocational education, students may appreciate online platforms for their convenience, access to learning resources, and support for practical tasks, while deeper cognitive engagement may require additional instructional strategies that encourage analysis and reflection.

Perceived ease of use was similarly associated with online learning motivation and several CoI dimensions, including teaching presence and social presence. This finding supports previous research showing that students’ perceptions of usability are important in shaping technology acceptance and participation in digital learning environments [24, 40, 41, 73]. When online platforms are perceived as easy to navigate and manage, students may experience fewer technological barriers and may be more willing to participate in online learning activities. This is particularly relevant in vocational education, where students may have different levels of previous experience with digital learning tools. However, the absence of a significant relationship between perceived ease of use and cognitive presence suggests that accessibility alone may not be sufficient to support deeper learning processes. Instead, cognitive engagement may depend on the quality and structure of learning activities provided through technology. These findings highlight that online learning motivation among vocational students is shaped by a combination of personal confidence, perceived technological value, and usability perceptions. However, these technology-related factors appear to support motivation differently across learning processes. While self-efficacy, usefulness, and ease of use are important motivational foundations, their contribution to meaningful online learning experiences may depend on how digital technologies are integrated into teaching and learning practices.

## 5.2 Relationships among PU, EU, and SE

The findings highlight the close relationship among self-efficacy, perceived ease of use, and perceived usefulness within online learning environments. Consistent with extended Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) research, students’ confidence in their ability to use digital technologies was associated with more positive perceptions of technology usability and value [22, 23, 54, 60]. This suggests that self-efficacy may represent an important personal factor that shapes how students interpret and engage with online learning technologies. Students who feel more capable of managing digital learning tasks may be more likely to perceive online platforms as accessible and meaningful for supporting their learning activities.

The relationship between perceived ease of use and perceived usefulness further supports the central assumptions of TAM [13, 59]. When students perceive online learning systems as easier to navigate and operate, they may be more likely to recognise their educational value. This relationship is particularly relevant in vocational education, where students often engage with digital platforms to support practical learning activities and develop occupationally relevant competencies. Therefore, designing online learning systems that are accessible, clear, and aligned with students’ learning goals may contribute to more favourable perceptions of technology. Collectively, these findings suggest that technology acceptance among vocational learners is not shaped by a single factor but reflects the interaction between students’ confidence, their experiences with technology, and their perceptions of usefulness. While TAM provides an explanation of how technology-related beliefs are formed, the findings also indicate that these beliefs should be considered alongside instructional and contextual factors that influence students’ broader online learning experiences.

## 5.3 Mediating roles of CoI dimensions

The Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework provided a partial explanation of how technology-related beliefs were associated with online learning motivation among secondary vocational students. Among the three CoI dimensions, teaching presence demonstrated the most consistent association with online learning motivation, although the indirect effects were small in magnitude. The findings suggest that students’ motivational experiences in online learning are closely associated with the quality of instructional organization, teacher guidance, and structured learning support. This interpretation is consistent with the central position of teaching presence within the CoI framework, where effective design, facilitation, and instructional guidance provide the foundation for meaningful online learning experiences [14, 35]. The importance of teaching presence may be particularly relevant in secondary vocational education, where online learning activities are often embedded within structured courses, practical training requirements, and teacher-guided learning processes. Unlike learning environments that rely heavily on independent exploration or peer collaboration, vocational students may experience online learning primarily through organised instructional activities, demonstrations, feedback, and task completion. Therefore, teaching presence may represent one possible pathway through which students’ perceptions of technology are associated with motivation.

However, the findings also indicate that the contribution of CoI dimensions may vary depending on the learning context. The absence of a significant relationship between perceived usefulness and cognitive presence suggests that recognising the value of online learning technologies may not necessarily be accompanied by deeper cognitive engagement. Cognitive presence involves processes such as reflection, inquiry, problem-solving, and the development of meaning through learning activities [14]. Although students may consider online platforms useful for accessing learning materials, completing assignments, or supporting practical tasks, these functional advantages alone may not provide sufficient conditions for deeper thinking and knowledge construction. This finding contributes to previous TAM research by suggesting that positive perceptions of technology value should not be assumed to automatically promote higher-order learning processes. Instead, cognitive engagement may depend on the presence of carefully designed learning activities that encourage students to analyse information, apply knowledge, reflect on experiences, and solve authentic problems. In vocational education, where learning is often connected with practical competencies, cognitive presence may require explicit instructional strategies that connect digital resources with meaningful vocational tasks. Similarly, the non-significant relationship between self-efficacy and social presence suggests that confidence in using digital technologies does not necessarily correspond with increased social interaction in online learning environments.

Although previous research indicates that self-efficacious learners may be more willing to participate in collaborative activities and online interaction [49], social engagement may also depend on contextual factors, including course structure, interaction opportunities, and classroom norms. In the present context, online activities may have been organised mainly around teacher-directed instruction, individual learning tasks, and assessment requirements rather than student-led discussion or collaboration. As a result, students with higher self-efficacy may have felt more capable of managing online tasks independently without necessarily engaging more frequently with peers. The limited mediating role of social presence and cognitive presence does not necessarily challenge the assumptions of the CoI framework. Rather, it suggests that the relative contribution of each presence dimension may depend on educational context and instructional design. The generally small magnitude of the indirect effects should also be interpreted in relation to the measurement characteristics of the model. As reported in Sect. 4.1.2, several constructs, particularly online learning motivation, perceived usefulness, and self-efficacy, demonstrated substantial empirical overlap. This shared variance may reduce the ability to distinguish the unique contribution of individual constructs within the structural model and may potentially contribute to the weak or non-significant indirect effects observed for several theoretically expected pathways. Therefore, the mediation findings should be interpreted as evidence of specific associations rather than definitive evidence of distinct mediating mechanisms. While CoI proposes that teaching presence, social presence, and cognitive presence collectively contribute to meaningful online learning experiences [14], the present findings indicate that these dimensions may not function as equally influential pathways in all learning environments.

The findings contribute to CoI research by suggesting that the relative contribution of different presence dimensions may vary across vocational education settings. In teacher-guided online learning environments, teaching presence may represent a more immediate motivational pathway, while social and cognitive presence may require intentional pedagogical design to become influential. Developing opportunities for peer interaction, collaborative learning, reflection, and problem-solving may therefore be necessary to strengthen these dimensions. Thus, technology acceptance alone may support students’ willingness to engage with online learning, but meaningful motivational experiences may depend on how digital environments are structured and facilitated by educators.

## 5.4 Implications for online learning design

The findings provide several implications for designing online learning environments in vocational education. First, the prominent role of teaching presence suggests that online learning should include strong instructional support rather than relying mainly on student independence or peer interaction. Vocational educators may strengthen online learning experiences through clear instructions, timely feedback, guided demonstrations, and competency-based activities. Given the practical orientation of vocational education, adapting the Community of Inquiry (CoI) framework may require greater attention to teaching presence as an important element connecting technology use with meaningful learning experiences [14, 58].

Second, the association between self-efficacy and online learning motivation highlights the need to support students’ confidence in using digital learning platforms. Although accessible technologies are important, usability alone may not be sufficient to support sustained engagement. Vocational institutions may therefore consider technology orientation programmes, scaffolded online activities, and continuous technical support to help students develop confidence in managing digital learning tasks. This is particularly relevant for students with limited prior experience of online learning, as self-efficacy is related to how learners perceive the usefulness and accessibility of technology [54, 59].

Third, the findings suggest that social presence and cognitive presence may require deliberate instructional planning rather than developing automatically through technology adoption. Educators may strengthen social presence by incorporating structured peer discussions, collaborative projects, and workplace-related group activities. Similarly, cognitive presence may be supported through reflective tasks, problem-solving activities, and authentic vocational scenarios that encourage students to apply and evaluate knowledge. These approaches are important because perceiving technology as useful does not necessarily involve deeper cognitive engagement unless learning activities are designed to encourage inquiry and knowledge construction [25, 74].

Finally, the findings indicate that online vocational education requires a context-sensitive application of CoI rather than a direct transfer of models developed in other online learning settings. Effective online learning design should integrate digital technologies with teacher guidance, confidence-building opportunities, and practical learning activities aligned with vocational goals. Future research should examine whether these design principles apply across different vocational disciplines and educational contexts, particularly where institutional practices and cultural factors may shape students’ online learning experiences.

## 6 Limitations and directions for future research

Although this study provides insights into the factors associated with online learning motivation among secondary vocational students, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the cross-sectional design limits the ability to examine changes in students’ motivation over time. Future longitudinal research could explore how students’ perceptions of technology, self-efficacy, and online learning experiences develop as they gain greater exposure to digital learning environments.

Second, the generalizability of the findings is limited by the sampling approach and research context. The study used convenience sampling, and participants were recruited from a single vocational education setting in China. In addition, the sample was predominantly female, which may limit the extent to which the findings represent broader vocational student populations. Future studies should validate these findings using larger and more diverse samples across different regions, vocational disciplines, and demographic groups. Further research may also consider additional contextual factors, such as institutional support, teacher characteristics, and digital infrastructure. In addition, the observed overlap among some constructs, particularly self-efficacy, perceived usefulness, and online learning motivation, suggests that future studies should further examine construct distinctiveness and the stability of structural relationships using refined measurement instruments and alternative validation approaches.

Third, the study relied on self-reported survey data, which may be affected by response bias. The absence of objective learning indicators, such as learning management system engagement records, completion data, or instructor evaluations, limits the opportunity to triangulate students’ reported motivation. Future research should combine survey data with behavioral and qualitative evidence to develop a more comprehensive understanding of online learning experiences.

Finally, emerging technologies, including AI-supported adaptive learning systems, provide opportunities for further investigation. Future studies could examine how these technologies interact with motivational factors and pedagogical approaches in vocational education. Addressing these limitations will contribute to developing more context-sensitive and comprehensive models of online learning motivation.
