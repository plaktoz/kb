# How to Learn Faster and Better: 10 Science-Backed Strategies

Research by Hermann Ebbinghaus shows people forget up to 70% of new information within 24 hours of learning it. Most study habits — rereading, highlighting, and cramming — hurt long-term memory. A few science-backed strategies make a big difference without requiring more study time.

## How the brain learns

New information is encoded through synaptic connections; the strength of those connections depends on how actively you interact with the material and how often you revisit it. The spacing effect (distributing study across multiple sessions rather than cramming) is one of the most replicated findings in learning science, confirmed by a 2013 review in Psychological Science in the Public Interest. The testing effect (retrieval practice) shows that actively pulling information from memory strengthens it far more than passive review — a landmark 2006 Roediger and Karpicke study found students who tested themselves retained significantly more a week later than those who only reread notes. Cognitive load theory explains why chunking related concepts into meaningful units helps, since working memory holds only about four items at once. Sleep plays a vital role in memory consolidation, strengthening the neural connections formed during the day.

## 10 techniques

1. **Test yourself before you feel ready** — close your notes and write down everything you remember; test within 24 hours of first exposure, then at increasing intervals.
2. **Review at the right moments** — use spaced repetition tools (e.g., Anki, Quizlet) to automate scheduling.
3. **Group information into meaningful units** — chess grandmasters recognize board patterns, not individual pieces; build a framework for new knowledge.
4. **Interleave topics** — switch between subjects or problem types within a session rather than drilling one exhaustively.
5. **Use the Feynman Technique** — write the concept, explain it simply as if teaching a novice, find where the explanation breaks down, and fill the gap.
6. **Ask "why"** — elaborative interrogation (generating explanations for why a fact is true) is especially effective for factual material.
7. **Practice deliberately** — Anders Ericsson's deliberate practice (focused, uncomfortable practice at the edge of ability with immediate feedback) beats longer casual practice.
8. **Use timed focus blocks** — standard Pomodoro-style blocks run 25 minutes of focused work followed by a 5-minute break, with a longer break after four blocks.
9. **Rest and sleep** — memory consolidation happens largely during sleep; a 10-minute rest after studying helps solidify what was just learned.
10. **Build a mind map** — a diagram branching from a central concept mirrors how the brain stores information in networks rather than lists.

## Common mistakes

Cramming produces retention that decays within days versus material studied across a week. Multitasking during study carries a real cognitive cost since the brain rapidly switches rather than truly multitasking. Skipping self-testing — defaulting to passive rereading and highlighting — is one of the costliest mistakes, since active recall and self-quizzing are the techniques that work best.

## A simple daily routine and spaced review schedule

Start each session with 10 minutes of active recall from previous material (no notes), spend 25–30 minutes in a focused block on new material, and close with 5 minutes summarizing in your own words. A basic spaced review schedule: first exposure on day 1, active-recall review on day 3, self-testing on day 7, flashcards on day 14, and a final review on day 30 — each review timed to land right before the memory would otherwise fade.
