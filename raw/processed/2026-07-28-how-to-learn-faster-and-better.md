# How to Learn Faster and Better: 10 Science-Backed Strategies That Work

Research by Hermann Ebbinghaus shows people forget up to 70% of new information within 24 hours. Most habits like rereading, highlighting, and cramming hurt long-term memory, but a handful of science-backed strategies make a measurable difference. Barbara Oakley (Oakland University, creator of Coursera's "Learning How to Learn") describes two brain modes learners should toggle between: focused mode for tackling new material head-on, and diffuse mode where the brain makes background connections during rest.

Key neuroscience principles: (1) encoding via synaptic connections strengthens with active interaction and repeated revisiting; (2) the spacing effect — distributing study across sessions rather than cramming — is one of the most replicated findings in learning science (Ebbinghaus, 1885; confirmed by a 2013 Psychological Science in the Public Interest review); (3) the testing effect / retrieval practice, where actively recalling information beats passive review (Roediger & Karpicke, 2006); (4) cognitive load theory and chunking, since working memory holds only about four items at once; (5) neuroplasticity and sleep-based memory consolidation.

Ten proven techniques: test yourself before you feel ready; review at spaced intervals (apps like Anki/Quizlet automate this); group information into meaningful chunks; interleave different topics in one session; use the Feynman Technique (explain simply, find the gaps, refill them); ask "why" to generate elaborative explanations; use deliberate practice (Anders Ericsson) at the edge of your ability with immediate feedback; use timed Pomodoro blocks (25 min work / 5 min break) to protect attention; prioritize sleep for memory consolidation; and build mind maps to mirror the brain's network-based storage.

Common mistakes: cramming (produces recognition, not retention), multitasking (the brain switches rather than parallel-processes, with a cognitive cost per switch), and skipping self-testing (passive rereading feels productive but works far worse than active recall). A suggested spaced-review schedule: Day 1 (first exposure), Day 3 (active recall), Day 7 (self-testing), Day 14 (flashcards), Day 30 (final review) — each review timed to happen just before the memory would otherwise fade.
