# How to Learn Faster and Better: 10 Science-Backed Strategies That Work

source_url: https://nibble-app.com/blog/how-to-learn-faster-and-better

---

Research by Hermann Ebbinghaus shows people forget up to 70% of new information within 24 hours of learning it. Most study habits — rereading, highlighting, cramming — hurt long-term memory, but a few science-backed strategies make a big difference without requiring more study time.

## Quick summary

- Test yourself regularly: active recall and self-quizzing strengthen long-term memory far more than rereading.
- Space out reviews: spaced repetition beats cramming by scheduling reviews right before you'd naturally forget the material.
- Use the Feynman Technique: explain a concept in your own words as simply as possible; your knowledge has gaps where the explanation breaks down.
- Mix up topics: interleaving different subjects in one session builds stronger retention than drilling one topic at a time.
- Sleep well: memory reinforcement happens during sleep; rest is part of the learning process, not a reward for it.

## How the brain learns

Barbara Oakley, a Distinguished Professor of Engineering at Oakland University, describes two brain modes: focused mode (tackling new material directly) and diffuse mode (background connections made during rest). Effective learners toggle between both.

1. **Encoding and synaptic connections** — new information is recorded through synaptic connections; actively interacting with material and revisiting it regularly strengthens those connections.
2. **The spacing effect** — distributing study time across multiple sessions rather than cramming significantly improves retention. Documented by Ebbinghaus in 1885 and confirmed by a 2013 review in *Psychological Science in the Public Interest*.
3. **The testing effect (retrieval practice)** — a landmark 2006 study by Roediger and Karpicke found students who tested themselves retained significantly more material a week later than those who only reread notes.
4. **Cognitive load theory and chunking** — John Sweller's cognitive load theory explains why chunking related concepts into meaningful units is effective; working memory holds only about four items at once.
5. **Neuroplasticity and memory consolidation** — practicing or recalling information strengthens neural connections (neuroplasticity); sleep plays a vital role in consolidating those connections.
6. **Active recall and spaced repetition** — forcing the brain to retrieve information, paired with review at increasing intervals, combats the forgetting curve and converts short-term knowledge into long-term memory.

## 10 proven techniques

1. **Test yourself before you feel ready** — close your notes and write down everything you can remember; use flashcard apps like Anki or Quizlet to automate spaced scheduling.
2. **Review at the right moments** — digital spaced-repetition tools handle scheduling automatically.
3. **Group information into meaningful units** — chess grandmasters recognize board patterns rather than memorizing individual pieces; build a framework to hang new knowledge on.
4. **Mix it up to build stronger retention** — interleaving different topics or problem types in one session beats drilling a single concept exhaustively.
5. **The Feynman technique** — write the concept's name, explain it simply as if teaching someone who knows nothing, find where the explanation breaks down, then fill the gaps and try again.
6. **Ask yourself why** — generate explanations for why a fact is true (elaborative interrogation) rather than just noting that it is.
7. **Slow down to speed up** — deliberate practice (Anders Ericsson) is focused, uncomfortable practice at the edge of current ability with immediate feedback; 20 minutes of deliberate practice outperforms two hours of casual repetition.
8. **Set a timer to protect your attention** — Pomodoro-style 25-minute focused blocks followed by 5-minute breaks give the brain a clear endpoint.
9. **Have a rest and sleep** — memory consolidation happens largely during sleep; a 10-minute rest after studying helps solidify what was just processed.
10. **Give your brain a picture** — mind maps mirror how the brain stores information in networks rather than lists.

## Common mistakes

Cramming produces material that decays within days; the same hours spread across a week produce dramatically stronger retention. Multitasking during study carries a real cognitive switching cost — the brain doesn't truly multitask. Skipping self-testing in favor of passive rereading and highlighting is arguably the costliest mistake, since active recall and self-quizzing are the techniques that work best.

## Matching approach to learning style (VARK)

- Visual learners: mind maps, color-coded notes, diagrams, charts.
- Auditory learners: reading aloud, podcasts, explaining ideas out loud.
- Reading/writing learners: detailed note-taking, summarizing in one's own words, Cornell-style notes.
- Kinesthetic learners: building projects, writing code, practicing physical skills, teaching others.

## Related frameworks mentioned

The 7-3-2-1 spaced repetition schedule reviews material 7, 3, 2, and 1 days before a test. The 70-20-10 rule holds that 70% of effective professional learning comes from direct experience, 20% from social learning (feedback, mentors, peers), and 10% from formal training. Scott Young's concept of "ultralearning" emphasizes direct practice, aggressive feedback-seeking, and heavy retrieval practice over passive review.
