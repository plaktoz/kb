# How AI is quietly exhausting you—and what to do about it

Among a team of developers using AI, engineers started writing code faster and getting answers in seconds, yet also reported feeling more exhausted than before. AI hasn't actually reduced the amount of work that needs to be done — it has changed its nature. Managing tools and constantly switching between them becomes more draining than performing the original tasks, and can even slow down finding a solution.

## Where the fatigue comes from

Developers used to search Google, use Stack Overflow, and ask colleagues, with reflection time between each step. Now, AI coding tools like Cursor or GitHub Copilot shorten the path to an answer, but developers are engaged in continuous evaluation of AI suggestions — accept, reject, rewrite the prompt, regenerate — dozens of micro-decisions with no pauses. Each carries a cognitive cost, contributing to decision fatigue.

Boston Consulting Group (BCG) surveyed nearly 1,500 U.S.-based workers and found 14% of people who use AI at work and need a high amount of oversight experience "AI brain fry" — mental fog and inability to focus — making them more likely to consider changing jobs and make more mistakes.

## More tools don't improve productivity

One or two AI tools genuinely boost productivity per BCG data, but productivity peaks at three tools and drops with a fourth. According to METR data, experienced developers using AI tools actually worked more slowly even while believing their task completion time decreased by nearly a quarter.

UC Berkeley researchers who spent eight months observing employees at a tech company found "workload creep": AI-driven productivity gains led employees to take on more tasks rather than rest, with the workday quietly stretching longer until fatigue affected decision quality.

## What to do about it

1. Rethink workflows before introducing AI — ask which tasks require human attention versus which can be automated.
2. Give managers a leading role in AI adoption; teams where managers personally help with AI tools show lower cognitive fatigue (per BCG).
3. Establish team rules for working with AI ("AI practice") — pauses before important decisions, sequential execution instead of multitasking, time for reflection.
4. Track cognitive load as a separate metric alongside productivity, engagement, and stress.
5. Explain the reasons behind AI-driven changes — transparency about valuing balance over pure output reduces mental fatigue by 28%, per BCG research.

The key question isn't "how do we use AI" but "why?" — start with the goal of freeing people from routine tasks rather than measuring success in tokens or lines of code.
