# Technology-enabled learning and perceived learning outcomes in online higher education

## Abstract

Technology-enabled learning (TEL) enables institutions to provide flexible learning environments and technology-supported instructional practices, as it has become a central feature of online higher education. However, empirical evidence explaining how perceived learning outcomes (PLO) are associated with technology-enabled learning (TEL), particularly in developing-country contexts, remains limited. Using survey data collected from 434 undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in online higher education programmes in India, this study examines the relationships among TEL, flexible learning (FL), motivation (MO), self-regulated learning (SRL), and PLO. The proposed conceptual model was tested by employing Partial Least Squares Structural Equation Modelling (PLS-SEM). The findings suggest that TEL is indirectly associated with PLO through FL, MO, and SRL. Learner motivation and self-regulated learning were positively related to Flexible Learning, which in turn, were associated with enhanced perceived learning outcomes. The model demonstrated substantial explanatory power, highlighting the importance of pedagogically designed technology-supported flexibility in online learning environments. The study provides empirical evidence that flexible learning functions as a key pedagogical mechanism linking technology-enabled learning with perceived learning outcomes, thereby contributing to the educational technology literature. The findings also offer practical implications for policymakers, higher education institutions and instructional designers, seeking to strengthen perceived learning outcomes through pedagogically informed technology-enabled learning strategies.

## 1 Introduction

The rapid advancement of digital technologies has transformed higher education by enabling scalable and flexible online learning environments. TEL enables learners to access learning resources anytime and anywhere while supporting diverse instructional approaches, self-paced learning and multiple learning pathways. Universities increasingly depend on online and blended learning to provide solutions for growing student populations, diverse learner profiles, and increasing demands for flexible access to learning [2, 6].

Such flexibility has become particularly important in online higher education, where learners often balance professional and personal commitments with academic accountability.

Although evidence regarding the effectiveness of TEL in improving PLO remains mixed, TEL has been widely adopted. Past research has highlighted the significance of learner-centered factors in finding academic success in digital ecosystems. However, relatively few empirical studies have examined how Flexible learning environments influence these psychological and behavioural mechanisms and how they jointly shape PLO. Large-scale reviews and comparative studies have reported that OL can achieve outcomes comparable to traditional teaching under certain conditions, while also demonstrating substantial variability across contexts, disciplines, and learner groups [30, 47]. This study addresses this gap by examining the relationship between TEL and PLO in online higher education through the mediating mechanisms of FL, MO, and SRL. Using survey data from 434 university students from north India and applying partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM), the survey provides empirical insights into the processes through which Flexible learning environments contribute to students’ perceived learning outcomes. The findings are consistent with the existing theories of online learning and SRL by combining them into a unified framework within an emerging country context. A substantial body of previous studies approached this problem via technology-centric models, emphasising system quality, usability, information quality, and learner contentment as main determinants of success [42]. Meta-analytic and review evidence shows that PLO are shaped less by the presence of technology itself and more by how technologies are utilized to structure learning operations and learner engagement [7, 44].

One such condition is FL, which has long been proven as a defining feature of online and distance education. Previous studies emphasised flexibility in time and access as a main advantage of OL, primarily for adult learners balancing multiple personal and professional responsibilities [43]. Subsequent studies have expanded this perspective by conceptualising FL as a multidimensional variable encompassing learner control over speed, pathways, and access to learning resources [46].

However, flexibility does not arise automatically from online delivery. Research on teaching design in blended and online ecosystems highlights that flexibility depends on how technological affordances are intentionally embedded in course structures and pedagogical strategies [15]. Consequently, similar technologies may endorse either rigid or FL experiences depending on teaching design decisions.

Existing literature further shows that Flexible learning environments support learner autonomy, engagement, and persistence by encouraging students to manage their learning processes according to individual requirements and contexts [25, 27]. These findings highlight flexibility as an important pedagogical condition that may explain variability in PLO. Although, previous studies have investigated online learning ecosystems, learner satisfaction, engagement, and technology adoption, limited research has examined how TEL is positively associated with PLO through learner motivation and SRL, especially in developing-country contexts.

This gap limits both theoretical understanding and practical guidance for institutions investing heavily in educational technologies. Without thorough insight into how technology contributes to PLO, institutions risk prioritising technology adoption over pedagogical design. Addressing this gap requires empirical models that move beyond direct links and explicitly investigate how technology enables conditions that endorse effective learning. Previous studies have indicated that technology-enhanced learning can improve PLO when combined with effective pedagogical designs [29, 44].

The current research addresses this requirement by proposing and empirically testing a mechanism-based model comprising FL, MO, and SRL integrating TEL with PLO. The analysis provides a clearer explanation of perceived learning outcomes by shifting the analytical emphasis from technology as an independent driver to flexibility as a pedagogical process. With this, it responds to calls for more theory-driven and process-oriented research in educational technology [34].

The remaining paper is structured as follows. The next section reviews important literature and develops the research hypotheses. This is followed by a description of the research methodology and data analysis processes. The outcomes are then presented and discussed in relation to previous studies, after which the paper ends with implications, limitations, and directions for future research. This investigation contributes to the literature by empirically examining how TEL is positively associated with PLO through FL, MO, and SRL in online higher education.

By combining transactional distance theory and flexibility theory, the investigation presents empirical evidence from a developing country context and extends existing theoretical frameworks of technology mediated learning.

## 1.1 Theoretical background

Online higher education is rooted in theories of transactional distance and community of inquiry, which focuses on the role of dialogue, structure, and learner autonomy in technology-mediated ecosystems [4, 16, 31]. Transactional distance theory posits that pedagogical distance in online learning ecosystems is influenced by dialogue, structure, and learner independence. TEL minimises transactional distance by increasing learner control, teaching adaptability, and interaction opportunities. Flexibility theory also shows that adaptive learning ecosystems support diverse learner needs and learning pathways, thereby promoting PLO. Online learning frameworks suggest that technology alone does not improve PLO, rather, pedagogical design and flexibility mediate the importance of technology-supported learning ecosystems [6, 34].

By combining these theoretical perspectives, this research conceptualizes FL as the main pedagogical process through which TEL is associated with PLO.

## 1.2 Research gap

Accordingly, this survey seeks to investigate the relationships within TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO. The literature reveals three key gaps. First, limited research has examined how TEL and FL foster learner MO and SRL, and how these attributes contribute to perceived learning results. Second, although FL is widely acknowledged as a critical feature of online education, it is rarely modelled as an explanatory mechanism linking TEL to PLO. Third, empirical studies testing such mechanism-based models using robust analytical techniques remain limited.

Accordingly, the present survey investigates the relationships within TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO.

## 2 Literature review

## 2.1 Technology-enabled learning (TEL)

Technology-enabled learning (TEL) refers to the use of learning management systems (LMS), online platforms, digital technologies and communication tools to support teaching and learning processes.

It focuses on the integration of technology to promote access, inter-exchange, engagement, and flexibility in learning ecosystems [4, 5].

### 2.1.1 Educational technology and online education

Educational technology has become pivotal to the delivery of online education in higher education using learning management systems, digital content platforms, and other multi-media tools. Research in this area has investigated how technological features and system characteristics shape learners’ experiences in online ecosystems.

Past work conceptualised educational technology through system-oriented perspectives, underlining usability, reliability, and information quality as determinants of e-learning success [42]. Subsequent models combined technical, organisational, and service-related dimensions to explain technology acceptance and satisfaction [1]. While influential, these models often highlight limited consistency in predicting PLO.

Large-scale reviews and comparative studies indicate that technology-enhanced learning produces highly variable outcomes across contexts, disciplines, and learner groups [7]. This variability suggests that technology alone is a necessary but not a sufficient requirement for effective learning. Scholars have thus argued that the educational value of technology depends less on its presence and more on how it is pedagogically combined into learning design [24, 38]. Design-oriented perspectives further emphasise that learning results improve when technological affordances are linked with instructional intentions and learner requirements [6]. Evidence-based syntheses reaffirm this view, highlighting that PLO depend on how and under what conditions learning is designed rather than on delivery alone [29].

## 2.2 Flexible learning (FL)

FL refers to educational methods that present learners with greater control over the time, location, speed, and type of learning. Through FL mechanisms, learners can balance educational activities with work, family, and other commitments [15, 20].

### 2.2.1 Flexible learning as a pedagogical condition

FL has long been identified as a defining feature of online and distance education. Initial reviews identified flexibility in time and access as a key advantage of OL, especially for adult and non-conventional learners managing multiple responsibilities [43, 47].

More recent scholarship conceptualises FL as a pedagogically driven construct, extending beyond convenience to include learner control over pace, sequencing, and participation [22]. From this perspective, flexibility emerges when instructional design intentionally leverages technological availability to support autonomy and choice. Research on blended learning design indicates that similar technologies may either constrain or improve flexibility depending on pedagogical decisions [15].

Design-based analyses further suggest that FL is closely associated with learner agency and personalisation of learning pathways. McLoughlin and Lee [28] argue that flexibility supports self-directed and learner-centred approaches by enabling students to adapt learning activities to their individual goals and contexts. These insights underscore that flexibility is not an automatic outcome of online delivery but a pedagogical condition that must be deliberately designed to create a synergistic effect together with the technological tools. FL has been conceptualized as an adaptive pedagogical approach that allows learners to control pace, place, and mode of learning [17, 22]. Emerging technologies further support FL designs by enabling personalized and adaptive learning environments [45].

### 2.2.2 Flexible learning (FL) and perceived learning outcomes (PLO)

Growing empirical evidence suggests a positive relationship between FL and learner outcomes. Studies on SRL indicate that flexibility enhances learners’ ability to plan, monitor, and adapt their learning activities, contributing to sustained engagement and academic achievement [9]. Research on student perceptions similarly shows that flexible course structures are strongly associated with engagement and satisfaction in online learning contexts [25, 27].

Beyond engagement, flexibility has been associated with perceived learning quality and persistence. Kahu and Nelson [23] highlight that pedagogical conditions supporting learner autonomy strengthen students’ connection with learning activities, thereby enhancing perceived effectiveness. Learning analytics research further suggests that flexible participation patterns are associated with adaptive learning behaviours and sustained interaction in online environments [21]. Despite these alignments, much of the literature treats FL as a contextual feature, the mechanisms through which flexibility translates into perceived learning outcomes remain underexplored.

## 2.3 Motivation (MO) in technology-enabled learning (TEL)

MO refers to the internal and external factors that stimulate and sustain learner engagement in educational activities. Self-Determination Theory differentiates between intrinsic MO, arising from personal interest and inherent satisfaction, and extrinsic MO, driven by external rewards like career advancement [37, 39].

In TEL ecosystems, MO plays a crucial role in sustaining learner participation, persistence, and engagement. Adult learners and working professionals often show a combination of intrinsic MOs, such as personal growth and knowledge acquisition, and extrinsic MOs, containing professional recognition, career advancement and promotion opportunities [13].

## 2.4 Self-regulated learning (SRL)

SRL refers to learners’ ability to control, plan, monitor, and evaluate their own learning processes. Self-regulated learners actively manage their strategies related to their studies, learning goals, time and effort to fulfil desired outcomes [33, 48].

SRL is particularly significant in in online and Flexible learning environments where learners enjoy higher autonomy and responsibility. Effective self-regulation enables learners to manage study schedules, monitor progress, and adapt learning strategies to attain required educational results [9].

## 2.5 Perceived learning outcomes (PLO)

PLO refer to learners’ subjective assessment of the competencies, skills, knowledge, and educational benefits received by participating in a learning programme.

PLO encompass learners’ assessments of knowledge acquisition, skill development, professional competence, and the overall effectiveness of their learning experiences. In online and Flexible learning environments, perceived outcomes are widely used as indicators of educational effectiveness as they reflect learners’ evaluation of the value and utility of their learning experiences [26, 40].

In the online learning literature, educational effectiveness has often been investigated through learners’ perceptions of their learning experiences and achievements. Therefore, the present investigation operationalises online learning effectiveness via the construct of PLO. PLO capture learners’ assessments of knowledge acquired, skill development, professional competence, and the complete value extracted from participation in TEL ecosystems. Thus, rather than treating online learning effectiveness as a separate construct, the survey measures effectiveness with the help of learners’ PLO, consistent with previous studies on online education [26, 40]. PLO were utilized as the operational measure of learning effectiveness in the current research, reflecting learners’ assessments of the educational value and perceived outcomes of the online learning experiences.

## 2.6 Technology enabled learning, flexible learning, and mechanism-based models

Recent advances in educational technology research emphasise the need for mechanism-based explanations that clarify how PLO emerge from interactions among technology, pedagogy, and learners. Meta-analytic evidence consistently highlights the role of instructional design and learning processes in shaping the effectiveness of technology-enhanced learning [7].

Scholars increasingly argue that pedagogical conditions such as FL mediate the relationship between technology use and PLO, explaining why direct-effect models often produce inconsistent results [19]. Mapping studies of educational technology research further demonstrate a shift toward examining engagement, design, and pedagogical mechanisms rather than technological features alone [8]. Testing such mechanisms requires analytical approaches capable of modelling indirect effects and latent constructs, for which structural equation modelling has been widely adopted in educational research [3, 18].

## 2.7 Development of conceptual framework and hypotheses

The proposed model posits that Flexible Learning influences Perceived Learning Outcomes indirectly through Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning. Rather, its impact operates through learners' motivational and self-regulatory processes, which are considered the primary mechanisms linking learning ecosystems to the perceived learning outcomes (Fig. 1).

Conceptual framework.

While Technology-Enabled Learning and Flexible Learning provide the specific conditions for learning, contemporary learning theories suggest that educational environments influence perceived learning outcomes through learners’ psychological and behavioural thought processes. Motivation reflects students' positive intent and persistence in engaging with learning activities, whereas Self-Regulated Learning represents the capacity to strategize, monitor, and regulate learning behaviours. Thus, the present survey conceptualises Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning as mediating mechanisms with the help of which Technology-Enabled Learning and Flexible Learning are associated with Perceived Learning Outcomes.

The present study proposes a conceptual framework in which TEL is linked to FL, while MO AND SRL are positively related to PLO. Accordingly, the following hypotheses are formulated:

Technology-Enabled learning → flexible learning

TEL ecosystems provide learners with access to digital resources, learning management systems (LMS), asynchronous communication, and personalised learning opportunities. These technological affordances promote flexibility by allowing learners greater control over time, speed, and location of learning [5, 22, 46]. Therefore:

## H1

Technology-Enabled Learning positively influences Flexible Learning.

Flexible learning → motivation

Flexible learning environments provide learners with autonomy and an opportunity, which are significant antecedents of MO with respect to the Self-Determination Theory. Greater flexibility allows learners to link learning activities with personal and professional commitments, thus promoting engagement and MO [23, 37].

## H2

Flexible learning positively influences learner motivation.

Flexible learning → self-regulated learning

FL requires learners to take greater responsibility for monitoring, planning and managing their learning activities. Thus, flexibility has been associated with stronger self-regulatory behaviours and learner autonomy [9, 28].

## H3

Flexible learning positively influences self-regulated learning.

Motivation (MO) → perceived learning outcomes (PLO)

Motivated learners are more likely to engage actively with learning activities, persist in challenging tasks, and achieve desired educational goals. Research consistently reports positive associations between motivation and PLO [35, 37].

## H4

Motivation positively influences perceived learning outcomes.

Self-regulated learning (SRL) → perceived learning outcomes (PLO)

Self-regulated learners effectively handle their learning strategies, monitor progress, and adapt their techniques to achieve academic success. Many studies have identified self-regulation as a robust predictor of learning performance and educational outcomes [9, 48].

## H5

Self-regulated learning positively influences perceived learning outcomes.

## 3 Methodology

## 3.1 Research design

This survey adopted a quantitative, cross-sectional survey design to examine the relationships among TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO in higher education. A survey-based approach was considered appropriate given the research objective of empirically testing hypothesised relationships among latent constructs using data collected from a large student sample. This design enabled the assessment of relationships among TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO within a single analytical framework.

## 3.2 Participants and data collection

The survey employed a cross-sectional survey design. Non-probability convenience sampling was used due to the absence of a comprehensive sampling frame of online learners. Data were collected from 434 undergraduate and postgraduate students enrolled in online and distance education programmes offered by higher education institutions in India. All participants were adults aged 18 years and above. The sample contained both traditional-age students and mid-age working professionals who were pursuing or pursued online or distance education programmes.

Participation was voluntary, and respondents were informed of the purpose of the survey and assured of anonymity. The sample size exceeded the minimum recommendations for structural equation modelling, providing adequate statistical power for estimating model parameters and testing mediation effects [18].

Respondents represented diverse academic disciplines, age groups, gender, marital status and levels of study, thereby enhancing the generalisability of the findings within the higher education context.

The survey was administered online using a structured questionnaire distributed through academic networks and institutional contacts. Participants accessed the questionnaire through a secure link. The final dataset comprised 434 valid responses after data screening.

## 3.3 Measurement instrument

The survey instrument consisted of demographic questions and measurement items concerning TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO. All items were measured using validated scales from prior studies, adapted to the context of online higher education. A five-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree) was used to measure participants’ responses.

The dataset used in this survey was extracted from the first author's broader doctoral research project. The original survey instrument measured Technology-Enabled Learning, Flexible Learning, Motivation, Self-Regulated Learning, and Perceived Learning Outcomes, along with additional constructs that formed part of the wider research framework. So, Motivation and Self-Regulated Learning were part of the original data collection process and were not introduced after data collection. In the present study, these constructs are incorporated as mediating variables to provide a more theoretically grounded explanation of the relationships among the research variables.

### 3.3.1 Technology-enabled learning

This construct captured students’ perceptions of the adequacy, accessibility, and usability of technological systems supporting Online Learning. Items assessed the reliability of platforms, ease of access to learning resources, and perceived technological support for learning activities.

### 3.3.2 Flexible learning

FL measured the extent to which online programmes allowed learners’ control over learning time, pace, and access to instructional materials. Items focused on learners’ ability to engage with content asynchronously, revisit materials, and adapt learning activities to individual needs.

### 3.3.3 Motivation

MO measured learners’ intrinsic and extrinsic drivers for participation in online learning activities. Items evaluated learners’ enthusiasm, perseverance, interest in learning, and perceptions of professional and personal advantages associated with participation in online education.

### 3.3.4 Self-regulated learning

SRL measured learners’ ability to plan, monitor, regulate, and evaluate their own learning activities. Items focused on time management, target setting, self-monitoring, and adaptation of learning strategies.

### 3.3.5 Perceived learning outcomes (PLO)

PLO measured learners’ perceptions regarding knowledge acquired, professional competence, skill development, and the overall effectiveness of their learning experiences.

Measurement items were adapted from validated instruments used in prior research. Details of the constructs are presented in the Table 1. The complete questionnaire items used in the present survey are provided in Appendix.

## 3.4 Data analysis procedure

Partial least squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) was conducted using SmartPLS 4 software. PLS-SEM was used to investigate the relationships among TEL, FL, MO, SRL, and PLO. The structural model was evaluated through path coefficients, coefficient of determination (R2), and bootstrapping process to assess the significance of hypothesised relationships. SEM was selected because it allows simultaneous estimation of measurement and structural models and is well suited for testing mediation effects involving latent variables [3].

A two-step analytical approach was followed. First, the measurement model was evaluated to assess reliability and validity of the constructs. Internal consistency reliability was examined using composite reliability, while convergent validity was assessed through factor loadings and average variance extracted. Second, the structural model was tested to examine the hypothesised relationships and mediation effects.

Bootstrapping was used to assess the significance of direct and indirect effects and the hypothesised relationships among the constructs, providing robust estimates of indirect effects and confidence intervals without assuming normality [18]. The structural model was evaluated using coefficient of determination (R2), estimated path coefficients and significance levels.

Recent methodological research in educational technology has theoretically focussed on specified structural models, complementary or alternative model assessments and examination of mediating pathways [10,11,12]. The present study specifies a theory-informed, mechanism-based model and examines both direct and indirect relationships using PLS-SEM and bootstrapping, which is consistent with this methodological orientation. Further to evaluate the stability of the explanatory relationships, alternative structural specifications were examined as a sensitivity assessment.

## 3.5 Ethical considerations

Ethical standards for research involving human participants were followed throughout the survey. Participation was voluntary, informed consent was obtained from all respondents, and no personally identifiable information was collected. The study involved an anonymous minimal-risk online questionnaire. According to the institutional policy governing minimal-risk educational research at Chitkara University, formal ethical approval was not required. The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki.

## 4 Results

## 4.1 Preliminary data screening

Prior to hypothesis testing, the data were screened for completeness and suitability for multivariate analysis. Responses with substantial missing data were excluded, resulting in a final sample of 434 valid cases. Descriptive statistics indicated that all measurement items exhibited acceptable variability. Preliminary data screening indicated that the dataset was suitable for structural equation modelling.

## 4.2 Measurement model assessment

All constructs indicated satisfactory reliability and convergent validity, with composite reliability values above 0.70 and AVE values above 0.50. Indicator reliability was assessed through outer loadings, and all retained items exhibited loadings above the recommended threshold of 0.70 (Appendix).

The reliability and validity of the measurement model were evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha, Composite Reliability (CR), Average Variance Extracted (AVE), and outer loadings. Cronbach’s alpha values were in the range of 0.883 to 0.927, while composite reliability values range from 0.919 to 0.942, exceeding the threshold value of 0.70. AVE values ranged from 0.697 to 0.746, highlighting sufficient convergent validity. Furthermore, all indicator loadings exceeded the threshold value of 0.70, indicating satisfactory indicator reliability (Table 2).

## 4.3 Discriminant validity was assessed using the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT)

Discriminant validity was assessed with the help of the Heterotrait-Monotrait Ratio (HTMT). Most HTMT values were below the recommended threshold level of 0.90, indicating acceptable discriminant validity between the constructs. However, the HTMT values for TEL and FL (0.936) and FL and PLO (0.916) marginally exceeded the recommended threshold level, suggesting a degree of conceptual relatedness within these constructs. Given the strong theoretical distinction within the constructs and the satisfactory reliability and convergent validity outcomes, the measurement model was maintained for further structural analysis (Table 3).

## 4.4 Structural model and hypothesis testing

The structural model was tested to examine the hypothesised relationships among the constructs. Overall model fit indices indicated an acceptable fit between the proposed model and the observed data.

Direct Effects:

Results indicated a positive and statistically significant effect of Technology-Enabled Learning on FL, thereby supporting H1. This indicates that students’ perceptions of technological adequacy and usability were positively associated with the degree of flexibility experienced in OL.

TEL is positively associated with FL (β = 0.862, p < 0.001). FL is linked to MO (β = 0.660, p < 0.001) and SRL (β = 0.737, p < 0.001). Further, MO (β = 0.265, p < 0.001) and SRL (β = 0.578, p < 0.001) showed a positive relationship with PLO. All hypothesised relationships were supported. The structural model results indicate that TEL is positively related to FL, while FL contributes to PLO indirectly through MO and SRL. ## 4.5 Structural model assessment

TEL is positively associated with FL (β = 0.862, t = 40.963, p < 0.001). FL, in turn, exerted significant positive effects on both MO and SRL. Furthermore, both MO (β = 0.265, t = 2.919, p < 0.001) as well as SRL (β = 0.578, t = 6.687, p < 0.001) significantly contribute to PLO, thereby supporting hypothesised relationships (Table 4).

## 4.6 R-square values

Table 5 presents the coefficient of determination (R2) values for the endogenous constructs.

The results indicate substantial explanatory power for the endogenous constructs in the model. The model explained 74.4% of variance in FL. The model explained 43.6% of the variance in MO (R2 = 0.436), 54.2% of the variance in SRL (R2 = 0.542), and 62.3% of the variance in Perceived Learning Outcomes (PLO) (R2 = 0.623), indicating moderate to substantial explanatory power.

## 4.7 Mediation analysis

The mediation analysis showed that TEL exerted an indirect impact on PLO via FL, MO, and SRL. The indirect effect via MO was 0.151, whereas the indirect effect via SRL was 0.367. The total indirect effect of TEL over PLO was 0.518. FL mediated the indirect association between TEL and PLO through MO and SRL, which is indicated by the mediation analysis.

Among the two mediating pathways, the SRL pathway showed a stronger effect than the MO pathway (Table 6).

As an additional sensitivity assessment, the other structural specifications were examined by removing individual antecedent paths from the endogenous constructs. The resulting patterns were broadly consistent with the proposed structural relationships, suggesting that the observed explanatory relationships were not driven only by one predictor pathway. ## 5 Discussion

The present study examined how Technology-Enabled Learning is associated with the learners’ perception of the online education. Technology-Enabled Learning appears to be associated with the learning environments that support greater flexibility and accessibility. This positive association extends to learners motivation and self-regulated learning. The analysis provides empirical support for a process-based explanation, highlighting their association. This result contributes to ongoing debates in educational technology research by offering a clearer explanation for the mixed effects of technology adoption reported in earlier studies.

The strong statistical relationship between FL and SRL suggests that Flexible learning environments may support learners to take greater responsibility for planning, monitoring, and evaluating their own learning activities. This finding is consistent with Zimmerman [48] and Broadbent and Poon [9], who argue that learner autonomy and self-management are important attributes of successful online learning. The substantial effect of SRL on PLO (β = 0.578) further suggests that the benefits of FL might be realised when learners actively engage in self-regulatory behaviours. Thus, self-regulation emerges as a key process linking Flexible learning environments to improved PLO.

Flexible learning has a positive impact on Motivation which supports Self-Determination Theory, suggesting that learners feel more motivated when they experience autonomy and control over their learning process. Flexible learning environments allow students to choose when, where, and how they engage with learning tools, thereby strengthening intrinsic motivation. Although the effect of MO on PLO was weaker than that of SRL, it stays statistically significant, indicating that motivated learners are more likely to persist in learning activities and perceive their learning experiences and learning journey more positively.

The stronger effect of SRL suggests that behavioural learning strategies may play a more significant role than MO in achieving perceived learning outcomes in a technology environment. This finding highlights the importance of fostering learners' capacity to manage, monitor, and regulate their own learning processes.

Further conceptual clarification is warranted because of the relatively high association between Technology-Enabled Learning and Flexible Learning. The two constructs represent different dimensions of the online learning environment, even though they are closely related to each other. Learning is delivered through Technology-Enabled Learning, which primarily refers to the technological infrastructure, digital platforms, and technology-supported practices, Flexible Learning, on the other hand, provides learners with greater flexibility in terms of time, place, pace, scheduling, and learning pathways, referring to the pedagogical and institutional arrangements. Institutions and instructors determine how technological affordances are translated into flexible learning opportunities, since availability of technology does not automatically result in flexible learning. For example, it is the prerogative of the institution whether learners are permitted to take the examination from home or are required to attend a designated proctored location, even when institutions conduct online examinations remotely with the help of online technology. Similarly, a university may still maintain fixed schedules, deadlines, or examination arrangements, even when they may use technology to deliver online learning. In other words, institutions may accommodate learners’ professional and personal commitments by introducing flexible schedules or alternative examination opportunities. Whereas FL reflects the pedagogical and institutional choices through which flexibility is provided to learners, TEL represents an enabling technological condition. Respondents nevertheless perceived these two dimensions as closely interconnected, which is understandable because learners often experience flexibility through technology-enabled learning environments, indicating a relatively high HTMT value observed in this study. The empirical distinction between the constructs may be less pronounced from the learner’s perspective than their conceptual distinction suggests. The elevated association therefore warrants cautious interpretation.

This finding aligns with research emphasising that digital platforms enable pedagogical conditions—such as asynchronous access, modular course design, and repeated engagement with learning materials—that support learner control over time and pace [6, 15].

Previous research that FL, MO and SRL positively contributes to the central determinant of perceived learning quality and learner satisfaction, reinforces the close association between different constructs. Prior studies have shown that flexibility in scheduling and accessibility is particularly important for higher education learners who balance academic responsibilities with work and personal commitments [43, 47]. In addition, research on SRL and learner MO in online environments support learners’ ability to plan, monitor, and adapt their learning activities, thereby contributing to sustained engagement and PLO [9, 25].

The results suggest that FL plays an important role as a pedagogical mechanism connecting technology with learner motivation and self-regulated learning, which subsequently appears to be associated with the students’ perceived learning outcomes. This finding clarifies why earlier evaluations of educational technology have reported inconsistent outcomes. Studies that focus primarily on technological features or system quality may fail to capture whether such technologies are used to support FL, MO and SRL conditions, thereby underestimating their pedagogical impact [1]. Similar mechanism-based explanations have been proposed in recent syntheses of online and blended learning research, which highlight the importance of instructional design processes in shaping PLO [30].

This analysis offers an integrated explanation of prior research on technology use and perceived learning outcomes. Meta-analytic evidence has shown that the effects of technology-enhanced learning vary considerably across contexts, suggesting the presence of underlying pedagogical mechanisms that shape these outcomes [7]. The present analysis provides empirical support for validating FL, MO and SRL as multiple mechanisms and provides a more process-oriented understanding of how TEL is positively associated with PLO, through these mediating mechanisms. PLO, therefore, captures the learner-centred experience-based assessment of learning, relevant to this study.

The results also have implications for how PLO is conceptualised and evaluated. Much of the existing research assesses effectiveness in terms of satisfaction or performance outcomes without adequately considering the learning conditions that give rise to these outcomes. By focusing on FL, MO and SRL as explanatory process, the study aligns with calls for more theory-driven and process-oriented approaches in educational technology research [4, 34].

Overall, the discussion suggests the importance of shifting attention from technology-centred models toward pedagogically grounded explanations of PLO. The findings also suggest that institutions seeking to improve PLO need to prioritise the design of FL environments that leverage technological affordances to support learner MO and SRL, pacing, and access.

The findings extend prior research on FL, MO and SRL by providing empirical evidence with respect to the mechanism through which TEL is positively associated with the PLO.

While previous studies have examined the role of TEL, FL, MO, SRL independently, this survey integrates all the above constructs and provides evidence in this sample that FL serves as the main pedagogical mechanism through which TEL appears to be positively related with learner MO and SRL, which subsequently contributes to a positive perceived learning outcomes of the respondents. This supports theoretical propositions that learner autonomy, motivation, self-regulation, adaptable instructional design, and flexible pacing might be critical determinants of successful online learning environments.

In the context of developing countries, such as India, technology can address barriers related to geographical constraints, employment commitments, and infrastructural limitations. The results indicate the importance of institutional strategies that prioritize FL designs, including asynchronous content delivery, modular learning structures, and adaptive assessment systems.

The cross-sectional nature of the present study limits the interpretation of these findings. Temporal ordering or causal relationships among Technology-Enabled Learning, Flexible Learning, Motivation, Self-Regulated Learning, and Perceived Learning Outcomes is not established because the data were collected at a single point in time. The direct and indirect relationships should not be interpreted as definitive evidence of causality, even though the PLS-SEM results identify significant direct and indirect associations, consistent with the proposed theoretical model. The findings instead provide mechanisms within the observed sample and empirical support for the proposed pattern of relationships. Longitudinal or experimental research would be valuable for investigating causal and the temporal relationships among these constructs.

## 6 Conclusion, implications, and future research

## 6.1 Conclusion

This analysis investigated how TEL is associated with PLO through FL, MO, and SRL. Using structural equation modelling and survey data from 434 higher education students, the findings suggest that TEL is positively associated with FL, which is subsequently associated with higher levels of MO and SRL, which in turn are positively associated with PLO. FL emerged as an important pedagogical mechanism associated with learner motivation and self-regulated learning.

By adopting a mechanism-based perspective, the analysis advances understanding beyond technology-centric explanations that dominate much of the existing literature. The findings clarify why prior research has reported mixed results regarding the effectiveness of educational technology: technological systems alone are insufficient unless they are used to create learning environments that provide learners with flexibility in time, speed, and access. In doing so, the study helps explain how and why TEL indirectly contributes to PLO in higher education.

## 6.2 Theoretical contributions

This research contributes in three ways to Technology-Enabled Learning and PLO. First, the research repositions Flexible Learning (FL) from a descriptive feature of online education to a theoretically significant pedagogical mechanism linking TEL with learner motivation, self-regulated learning, and perceived learning outcomes. While flexibility has long been recognised as a positive influence on the online education, it has not always been explicitly modelled as an explanatory mechanism. The present analysis addresses this gap by empirically suggesting that FL serves as an important pedagogical process through which TEL is indirectly associated with PLO through FL, MO, and SRL. The study appears to contribute to educational technology literature by highlighting a sequential mechanism in which TEL may contribute to FL, which subsequently indicates a positive relationship with MO and SRL, which might enhance PLO.

Second, the study contributes to the process-oriented and mechanism-based models in educational technology research. Rather than assuming direct effects of TEL on PLO, the proposed model indicates the importance of examining intermediate learning conditions. This approach offers a more nuanced understanding of PLO, which appears to contribute to learner engagement, academic resilience, digital quality, learning analytics, or technology self-efficacy.

By explaining Perceived Learning Outcomes in online higher education, the study appears to contribute to the literature by integrating Technology-Enabled Learning, Flexible Learning, Motivation, and Self-Regulated Learning within a single mechanism-based framework. The study empirically examines their interconnected roles within a unified model and provides evidence from an Indian higher education context, rather than proposing these relationships as entirely new theoretical constructs. By shifting attention from direct associations between technology and perceived learning outcomes towards the processes through which technology-enabled learning may be translated into perceived learning outcomes through pedagogical flexibility and learner-related mechanisms, this integrated perspective may extend the existing research.

## 6.3 Practical implications

The outcomes provide several implications for higher education institutions and policymakers. It might be beneficial for the institutions to invest in technological infrastructure that supports FL designs, like learning management systems with adaptive attributes and mobile-compatible platforms. Instructional designers may consider introducing flexible learning pathways, self-paced modules, and personalized learning resources, which might enhance PLO. Policymakers may consider FL as a strategic component of national online education frameworks, particularly in developing countries where learners face diverse challenges in different sectors.

First, institutional investments in educational technology might be followed by conscious pedagogical strategies with the objective of enhancing FL. Simply adopting advanced platforms or tools may not improve PLO unless these technologies are used to support learner control over time, speed, and access to learning resources.

Second, instructional designers might prioritise course designs that leverage technological affordances, which appears to help in creating Flexible learning environments. Attributes such as asynchronous participation, modular content, and revisitable learning materials might be associated with the online programmes to maximise their impact.

Third, faculty development initiatives should put emphasis not just on technical training but also on pedagogical design for flexibility. Supporting faculty in designing flexible learning experiences may have a greater impact on perceived learning outcomes than investing solely in technological infrastructure.

## 6.4 Limitations and future research

This survey has many limitations. First, the use of self-reported survey data may bring common method bias. Second, since the variables were measured at a single point in time, the cross-sectional design limits causal interpretation. To examine the temporal ordering and causal relationships proposed in the present model, longitudinal and experimental studies are recommended. Third, the convenience sampling method and the focus on higher education students in India may restrict generalizability. Future research should engage longitudinal designs, objective learning performance measures, and nation-wise samples to validate and extend the findings.

The survey investigated perceived learning outcomes rather than objectively assessed academic achievement or competence. Thus, the survey results should be seen as reflecting students’ perceptions of learning rather than analysing independently verified learning performance. Although the two constructs viz. TEL and FL are conceptually distinct, the HTMT analysis indicated a higher-than-recommended degree of association between them in the present sample.

This suggests that respondents tended to perceive learning flexibility and technological support as closely intertwined attributes of their online learning experience. In contemporary online higher education, it is sometimes difficult for learners to distinguish between the technological infrastructure and the flexibility it affords, as flexibility is frequently achieved through technology-enabled platforms. Consequently, future research should further refine measurement items and validate these constructs across diverse educational contexts to strengthen discriminant validity, even while the theoretical distinction between the constructs is well established.

## 6.5 Directions for future research

Future research can build on this study in many ways. Longitudinal studies could investigate how FL evolves over time and how sustained exposure to Flexible learning environments influences learning results. Experimental research could also test the causal role of FL by manipulating specific design attributes. Additionally, future studies could extend the proposed model by incorporating other pedagogical mechanisms, such as learner engagement, academic resilience, digital quality, learning analytics, or technology self-efficacy.

Comparative studies across disciplines or institutional contexts may also provide insights into how FL operates under different conditions.
