# Book Review: China's Examination Hell

Scott H. Young explores why East Asian students dominate global academic rankings (PISA), attributing it largely to culture rooted in China's imperial civil service exam system — the **keju** (科举).

East Asian nations claim most top PISA spots in math and reading; Chinese metro regions rank even above Singapore. Students in these countries study significantly more hours, many attending evening cram schools.

The keju, launched in 605 CE, ran for ~1,300 years, staffing government via merit-based exams rather than hereditary privilege. Exams were brutally competitive — roughly 1–2% pass rates at early stages, with perhaps a dozen candidates per 100,000 ultimately earning top *jinshi* status. Conditions were harsh: candidates locked in small cells for three days, exposed to weather and superstition-fueled anxiety.

Anti-corruption measures were extensive, including anonymized answer sheets and scribal copying. One study found doubling regional jinshi density correlates with an ~8.5% rise in educational attainment today.

## Legacy considerations

- Suppressed military culture; strengthened civilian governance.
- May have hindered the kind of decentralized innovation driving Europe's Industrial Revolution.
- Young questions whether credential-focused cultures — past or present — optimally develop human potential.
