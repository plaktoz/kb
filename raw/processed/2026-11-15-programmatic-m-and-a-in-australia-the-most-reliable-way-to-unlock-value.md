# Programmatic M&A in Australia: The most reliable way to unlock value

**In the high-stakes realm of M&A,** the winners' main strengths are patience, practice, and strategic discipline. Outperformers grow more steadily, shape their portfolios continuously to thrive as marketplaces change, and deliver more value to shareholders while keeping risk to a minimum.

Decades of McKinsey research on the world's 2,000 largest public companies confirm that "programmatic" M&A—a steady cadence of small and midsize deals, each tied to a clear strategic theme—is the approach most likely to create value and presents the least risk.

Our latest Global 2,000 analysis from January 2016 to December 2025 revealed that programmatic acquirers delivered median excess TSR of about 3.5 percent a year, while companies relying mainly on organic growth trailed peers by more than 2 percent. In that decade, less than 5 percent of Australian-listed companies took what we consider a programmatic approach, compared with roughly 20 percent in the United States and Canada, 10 percent in Japan, 15 percent in the United Kingdom, and 30 percent in Germany. (In the Global 2,000 analysis, we categorise programmatic dealmaking as more than two deals a year, or roughly 20 or more over a decade. In our Australian extension, we lower the bar to ten or more acquisitions from 2016 through 2025, reflecting a smaller and less acquisitive market; we discuss the choice later in the article.)

Why hasn't programmatic M&A taken hold in Australia? Some say the country has only a short list of targets, a shareholder base that rewards dividends and organic execution, and a history of transformational deals that went wrong. Regardless, our new research shows that programmatic M&A pays off. Using the same approach as the Global 2,000 study, we found that among Australia's 365 largest listed companies, those that built programmatic acquisition engines delivered the highest median excess TSR of any cohort, while those pursuing organic growth delivered among the lowest. Australia is not an exception to the global pattern; it is simply a place where few companies have tested programmatic approaches.

In this article, we recap the global evidence, show how Australia compares with other markets on deal volume, examine the return profiles of Australian acquirers, and explain how companies can build their own dynamic buy-and-sell programs.

## The global perspective

### Four approaches to M&A

*Programmatic.* Programmatic M&A is defined as more than two small or midsize deals a year, adding up to a meaningful share of the acquirer's market capitalisation—a median of 11 percent over a decade.

*Selective.* Selective M&A is defined as two or fewer deals a year, with cumulative value above 1.4 percent of market capitalisation.

*Large deal.* Large-deal M&A is defined as at least one acquisition in which the target's market capitalisation is 30 percent or more of the acquirer's.

*Organic.* Organic M&A is defined as at most one deal every three years, with cumulative deal value below 2 percent of market capitalisation.

Our latest Global 2,000 analysis tracks the companies that were among the 2,000 largest by market capitalisation at the end of 2015 and were still trading at the end of 2025. It then sorts them into four approaches to M&A (see sidebar "Four approaches to M&A").

The results fit a pattern we have tracked for more than two decades: Programmatic acquirers, about 13 percent of the sample, delivered a median excess TSR of about 3.5 percent a year, the highest of any group. Companies relying on organic growth, roughly 30 percent of the sample, were the weakest performers, and the spread between the two approaches has widened to about 5.7 percentage points. The median excess TSR of selective acquirers, the largest group at 46 percent, and large-deal acquirers, 11 percent of the sample, both hovered around zero.

The pattern holds around the globe. In Canada, Constellation Software has acquired hundreds of small vertical-market software businesses since 1995, typically dozens a year, most too small to announce individually, deploying about US $1.6 billion on acquisitions in 2025 alone. In the United States, Danaher Corporation has completed more than 400 acquisitions since 1984, integrating each through the Danaher Business System and spinning off three platforms that now run the same playbook. Sweden's ASSA ABLOY has made more than 400 acquisitions since it was formed in 1994, including 23 in 2025 and a record 26 in 2024, against a standing target of 5 percent acquired growth over a business cycle. Last, in Japan, Hitachi has run the most complete buy-and-sell program of any large conglomerate: Over a little more than a decade, it sold or deconsolidated all 22 of its listed subsidiaries while acquiring ABB's power grids business (now Hitachi Energy), digital engineering firm GlobalLogic for US $9.6 billion, and Thales's rail-signalling division, completing about 50 acquisitions in all.

(Deal counts from 2016 to 2025 for these companies in the Global 2,000 sample are 563 for Constellation Software, 121 for ASSA ABLOY, 50 for Hitachi, and 31 for Danaher. All four are classified as programmatic acquirers in the sample.)

None of these companies are defined by a single transaction. Rather, each is defined by a repeatable approach to buying and selling. All four delivered annual TSR well ahead of their peers.

Programmatic M&A is the top-performing strategy in essentially every sector we examined, including—and most relevant to Australia—energy and materials (about 3.7 percent median excess TSR) and financials (about 2.8 percent).

Within the programmatic group, more is more: The most-acquisitive companies, those completing more than five deals a year, earned a median excess TSR of about 4.1 percent. Programmatic divestors also led their peers, with a median excess TSR of about 2.4 percent, a reminder that the approach is about continuous portfolio reshaping rather than accumulation alone.

Programmatic acquirers account for about 13 percent of the Global 2,000 but roughly a quarter of deal value—close to half of it in downturns.

Two further findings stood out in the global research. First, about 95 percent of acquisitions by number were small, with targets worth less than 5 percent of the acquirer's market capitalisation. Second, large deals remain a coin flip on their own, but acquirers who pursue them with a programmatic approach fare markedly better, with a median excess TSR of about 4 percent. In other words, dealmaking experience raises the odds that a large bet will pay off.

Australia contributed fewer than five programmatic companies to the global sample, too few to reveal anything about the local market. That is why Australia warrants its own deep dive.

## Australia's M&A volume

Relative to peer markets, programmatic M&A in Australia is rare. To measure it properly, we extended the Global 2,000 database to a larger set of Australian-listed companies through the same 2016–2025 window. Because the Australian sample is far smaller than the Global 2,000, we set the programmatic bar at ten or more acquisitions over the decade rather than the global rule of more than two per year.

Using this bar, of those Australian companies, roughly 10 percent qualify as programmatic acquirers, compared with 35 percent in the United States, 42 percent in the United Kingdom, 30 percent in Canada, nearly 30 percent in Japan, and 53 percent in Germany.

Some observers suggest that Australia's industry mix makes this gap inevitable, but our experience and research suggest there's more to the story. Financials and energy and materials account for about 48 percent of companies in the Australia sample set, roughly 11 percentage points above their weight in the Global 2,000. While these two industries tend to have fewer programmatic acquirers globally (8 percent and 11 percent, respectively, versus 15 percent for the remaining industries using the higher bar of 20 or more acquisitions), Australian programmatic acquirers in these two industries are even less active (just 5 percent at the lower bar of ten or more acquisitions).

Looking beyond research findings and misplaced assumptions, we see the consequences of strategic mistakes and capability gaps in M&A. Headlines about acquisition failures, for example, spur many to be wary of dealmaking in general, but history confirms that the biggest risks apply mostly to the one-off big bets.

And few ASX-listed companies have what they need to improve the odds of dealmaking success, such as the strategic rationale for M&A, standing deal pipelines, integration playbooks, or postmortem habits. Programmatic acquirers are twice as likely to have a dedicated integration team and about 2.5 times more likely to run postmortems.

Deal scarcity in Australia is therefore less a feature of the market than a result of board and management choices.

## Deep dive: Australia TSR performance

Companies pursuing a programmatic M&A strategy are scarce in Australia, despite outperforming all other programs on both the buy and sell side.

### Buy side

As noted above, for Australia, we classify a company as programmatic if it completed ten or more acquisitions from 2016 to 2025. As in the global study, any company whose single largest deal exceeded 30 percent of its market cap is classified as a large-deal acquirer. Companies with at most one deal every three years are classified as organic.

The results echo the global pattern. The 32 programmatic acquirers delivered a median excess TSR of 1.7 percentage points a year against the benchmark. By contrast, the 133 selective acquirers returned –0.5 percentage points, the 58 large-deal acquirers 0.5 percentage points, and the 142 organic companies –0.7 percentage points.

## Large deals

Large deals remain risky, but programmatic experience helps. While our global findings suggest that large deals as a category deliver neutral to negative returns, acquirers that pursue large deals alongside a programmatic approach perform significantly better, achieving a median excess TSR of 4 percent. This suggests that programmatic capabilities provide a crucial advantage in executing large, complex transactions.

In Australia, the findings were similar: Acquirers who combine a large deal with a programmatic approach earn a median excess TSR of 2.8 percent, compared with –0.3 percent for those who do not.

Programmatic and large-deal acquirers are the only cohorts to beat the benchmark. Among the 58 large-deal acquirers, 14 also pursue a programmatic M&A strategy, achieving a median excess TSR of 2.8 percent, compared with –0.3 percent for those exclusively pursuing large deals (see sidebar "Large deals") (Exhibit 1).

Programmatic acquirers are the only Australian cohort to outperform on every benchmark we tested.

### Sell side

The sell side shows the same pattern, with a sharper downside. Applying the same thresholds to divestitures, 17 of the 364 companies (4.7 percent) qualify as programmatic divestors, completing ten or more disposals over the decade—typically about 5.7 percent of market capitalisation in aggregate.

They delivered a median excess TSR of two percentage points a year, and 65 percent beat their benchmark. Selective divestors (93 companies) and organic companies (230 companies) had flat TSR, at –0.1 and zero percentage points, respectively. The 24 companies that made a single disposal of 30 percent or more of their market capitalisation trailed by 8.5 percentage points, and only 25 percent beat the benchmark.

Frequency, however, is not the same as discipline: Only six of the 32 programmatic acquirers were also programmatic divestors, but those six had median excess TSR of about 5.7 percentage points, compared with 0.3 percentage points for the others. Many of the large-deal sellers were small companies disposing of their core asset under pressure, so this is as much a story about sales forced by circumstance as about sales driven by strategy. Large sales can still work when planned well. For example, Mineral Resources, Perenti, and Origin Energy all beat their benchmarks.

The programmatic signature is visible in individual companies in Australia. ResMed, a medical device and software company, completed 18 acquisitions from 2016 through 2025, compounding shareholder returns well ahead of the ASX 300 (Exhibit 2).

ResMed used its program to buy capabilities, not scale, in a textbook example of programmatic strategy. The company's 18 acquisitions redefined the business.

Beginning with Brightree, a cloud software provider, acquired for roughly AU $1 billion in 2016, ResMed assembled an out-of-hospital software portfolio deal by deal through 2025. It bought into connected care before its core device business matured and kept buying through the pandemic and a competitor's 2021 recall. The program was powered by a strong organic engine. Revenue grew at close to double digits, gross margins expanded, and acquisitions were funded from cash flow rather than leverage. The company also benefited from structural tailwinds in its core market, including a large patient population and a recurring-revenue resupply model.

Market capitalisation rose from about AU $12 billion to AU $61 billion, TSR compounded at 17.6 percent a year. This is the profile our global research associates with the strongest programmatic acquirers: a larger share of deals outside the core and a willingness to pay for growth when the value created exceeds the price.

What the Australian and global cases have in common is that each company links its acquisitions to a small number of strategic themes, keeps individual deals well below the size at which a single misstep would seriously harm the company, and has built organisational routines to integrate the next acquisition while the last one is still bedding down.

## Build a dynamic buy-and-sell engine

The scarcity of programmatic acquirers in Australia presents opportunities for companies looking to build their own deal programs. Our research and experience across industries show that programmatic dealmaking is a capability, not an event, and that the capability itself becomes a source of competitive advantage.

Building an M&A engine is rewarding, but it can be difficult. It usually requires senior leaders and board members to think and act in new ways. By default, many Australian leaders pursue organic growth, wait for a perfect target at the right moment, or reach for a single transformational deal when shareholders or circumstances demand a step change—all risky approaches compared to the patience, discipline, and practice of programmatic dealmaking.

The evidence shows that Australian executives who commit to a continuous, strategy-linked program of small and midsize acquisitions, matched by equally disciplined divestitures, and improve their skills with every transaction can develop a durable source of outperformance.
