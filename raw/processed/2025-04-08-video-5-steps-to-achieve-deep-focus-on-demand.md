## Step 1: Find Your Prime Focus Times [00:00:41]

**Host:** Step one, find your prime focus times. At night, you cycle between REM and deep sleep roughly every 90 minutes. You may already know that, but did you know these cycles continue while you're awake?

Instead of REM peaks and deep sleep troughs, your brain goes through restless peaks and deep focus troughs during the day. If you try to focus on a difficult task during a restless part of your biological attention cycle, you're setting yourself up to fail because your mind will resist focusing on one thing. Your mind acts like a hyperactive dog that hasn't been walked and wants to tear around the house.

These daily attention cycles are called ultradian cycles, and since each last about 90 minutes, you go through roughly seven to nine per day. Of all your daily attention cycles, two are noticeably stronger than the rest, allowing you to focus deeper than at any other time.

Through years of self-observation, I found mine are 5:30 to 7:00 a.m. and 11:30 to 1:00 p.m. You may already know what your peak focus times are, but if you don't, here's what to do.

When you wake up, tell your phone to set alarms every 90 minutes for the next 15 hours. When an alarm goes off, rate your ability to focus over the past 90 minutes on a scale of 1 to 10 in a note on your phone.

You'll get the most accurate picture focus cycle if you limit your caffeine, eat small meals throughout the day, avoid sugar as much as possible, and never eat beyond 80% full to avoid insulin spikes that crash your blood sugar and mess with your ability to naturally focus.

Once you identify your two prime biological focus windows, schedule deep work or critical study time during those blocks and protect them like your life depends on it. This might involve negotiating meeting times with co-workers and clients so they rarely fall in your prime focus blocks or turning down lunch requests if your focus block lands around noon.

Once you identify and block out your prime daily focus windows, it's time to move to deep focus step number two.

## Step 2: Get Writing [00:02:49]

**Host:** Get writing. At the start of a focus session, take out a blank sheet of paper. Either rip a page out of a notebook or grab a sheet of printer paper. Then fold it in half. A half page will be less intimidating than a full page.

Now grab a pen and write the following question at the top. What's my intention? Describe what you're going to work on for the next 60 to 90 minutes in as much detail as you can. Think of this as a list of instructions you'd give someone else to execute on your behalf. Feel free to doodle or sketch. Just don't stop until you fill the half sheet of paper.

When I do this exercise, I imagine I'm writing instructions for a robot that can read even the messiest handwriting, so I don't worry about how it looks.

This writing exercise is powerful for three reasons. First, using pen and paper sharpens your focus because there are no apps, browser tabs, or notifications pulling at your attention.

Second, when you write down specifically what you want to focus on for the next 60 to 90 minutes, your mind will quickly notice and resist distractions throughout the session.

Lastly, studies show that people who physically write down their goals are more likely to follow through. This is largely because writing activates a region in the brain called the reticular activating system, which is responsible for filtering what's important and what's not. Therefore, whatever you write down is perceived by your brain as more important than what you type or merely think about.

If you can spend one to two minutes writing out specific details about your upcoming focus session, you will fill up your attention with the upcoming task and eliminate distractions.

## Step 3: Lock In [00:05:35]

**Host:** Lock in. When people are in an intense state of focus, they use a significant amount of norepinephrine and acetylcholine, two neurochemicals that increase attention and alertness.

A simple brain hack to increase these two neurochemicals and thereby increase your focus is to fixate on a tiny point in front of you, like a predator ready to pounce on its prey.

So, after you've written out what you want from your focus session on that half sheet of paper, pick a small target in front of you, a word on a page or a spot on your desk 12 to 18 inches away. Stare at it for 30 seconds without letting your eyes drift. Blink as needed, but keep your gaze fixed.

The longer you fixate your eyes on a tiny point in front of you, the more attention-fueling chemicals your brain releases, further fueling your deep work session.

## Step 4: Start a Goldilocks Shot Clock [00:06:28]

**Host:** Deep focusing step number four, start a Goldilocks shot clock. In basketball, the shot clock forces the players on the court to focus and provides the right level of urgency to get the ball in the net. Using a short countdown timer while you work can have the same effect.

While basketball players feel pressure to get the ball in the net, you should feel pressure to hit your mini milestone before a timer expires. Mini milestones are any meaningful sign of progress that you can reach in the next 5 to 30 minutes. If you're writing, it might be 500 words. If you're studying, maybe it's mastering one concept.

The key is to pair that milestone with a shot clock time that hits the sweet spot, not so short that it creates anxiety, but not so long that it feels easy. Choose a time that you're confident you can hit if you don't get distracted by email, texts, or miscellaneous side tasks. These are what author Cal Newport calls shallow work.

When you choose a mini milestone and then tell your phone to set a timer for an amount of time that falls in the Goldilocks zone, between anxiety and boredom, you are likely to experience flow, the mental state where you feel your best and perform your best.

## Step 5: Push Twice, Then Quit [00:07:42]

**Host:** Deep focusing step number five, push twice, then quit. After pushing yourself for a few Goldilocks shot clocks, your focus will start to fade and distractions will creep in. You may think you've reached the end of your focus willpower, but you're not even close.

David Goggins calls this the 40% rule. When you think you're spent, you're only at 40% of your true capacity. What seems like a willpower wall is just a hurdle.

To get over this hurdle, use the twice then quit technique. When you reach your perceived focusing limit during a session, commit to two more focus sprints and then shut it down. You get to choose the sprint time. You might challenge yourself with two 20-minute sprints or decide to go with two 5-minute sprints.

The point is to provide your brain with a clear end point and commit to completely disconnecting after the final two timers. You'll find that when you give your mind a clear end point like twice then quit, you'll encounter less resistance and discover additional focus you didn't know you had.

On a recent Huberman Lab podcast, I learned that pushing through mental resistance provides a spike of acetylcholine, the neurotransmitter that enhances attention and focus. In other words, when you push a little further, your brain rewards you with the chemicals to keep going.
