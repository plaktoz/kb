# 5 Things Moving Markets: Oil, Fed, Big Tech

Global markets enter the final week of October facing a difficult combination of rising energy costs, uncertain interest-rate expectations and increasingly demanding valuations in technology stocks. Investors must also navigate a packed calendar of central-bank meetings and corporate earnings, with several of the world's largest companies reporting results.

The S&P 500 and Nasdaq both fell over the past week, while the Dow edged higher. Brent crude surged to $116.72 a barrel on Friday, its highest settlement since April, and the US 10-year Treasury yield stood at 4.99%.

These moves matter because expensive energy can reignite inflation just as investors are looking for clarity on interest rates and the sustainability of corporate profits. Here are the five things moving markets this week and what to watch next.

## 1. Oil Surges Above $116: The Inflation Risk Is Back

Oil is once again one of the most important variables for global markets. Brent crude settled at $116.72 a barrel on Friday, up around 6% over the week, while US West Texas Intermediate reached $103.27.

The latest surge followed attacks by Houthi forces in Saudi Arabia and growing concerns about disruption to energy infrastructure and shipping routes across the Middle East.

For investors, the concern extends well beyond petrol prices. Sustained increases in crude can feed into transport, manufacturing, food distribution and other business costs.

Companies may attempt to pass those costs on to consumers, but where demand is weak or competition is intense, higher input prices can instead squeeze profit margins. That creates an uncomfortable combination: slower growth alongside stubborn inflation.

This also complicates the decisions facing central banks. If oil remains elevated, policymakers may have less room to cut interest rates even if economic activity begins to weaken. Bond yields, equity valuations and currency markets could all react to changing expectations about the duration of higher rates. Energy producers may benefit from higher realised prices, but energy-intensive businesses and households face a tougher environment.

Takeaway: Do not treat the oil rally as merely a short-term commodity story. Watch whether supply disruptions persist and whether higher energy costs begin to appear in inflation data and corporate guidance. Investors should assess energy exposure, business cost sensitivity and portfolio concentration rather than assume that higher oil prices will quickly reverse.

## 2. Central Banks Take Centre Stage: Rates, Inflation and the Bond Market

The Federal Reserve, European Central Bank and Bank of Japan are all scheduled to announce policy decisions this week. The Bank of Canada and Reserve Bank of New Zealand are also meeting.

The challenge for policymakers is that energy-driven inflation is increasing just as markets are trying to establish how restrictive monetary policy will need to remain. The Fed's decision on Wednesday 28 October will be particularly important. September's stronger US inflation reading has revived concern that price pressures may prove more persistent than expected.

Even if the Fed leaves rates unchanged, investors will scrutinise its assessment of inflation, growth and the likelihood of further tightening. The question is not simply whether rates change this week, but whether the next move becomes less favourable for risk assets.

In Europe, the ECB must consider the impact of higher energy prices alongside uneven economic conditions and renewed concern about French government borrowing costs.

The Bank of Japan faces its own balancing act as it assesses inflation and the path towards policy normalisation. Differences in central-bank decisions and guidance can move currencies as well as bonds, influencing returns on overseas investments for international investors.

Takeaway: Focus on the direction of interest-rate expectations, not just the headline decision. A central bank holding rates steady does not automatically make markets safer if its message suggests borrowing costs will remain high for longer. Review the sensitivity of growth stocks, highly leveraged companies and long-duration bonds to changing yields.

## 3. Big Tech Earnings: Can AI Spending Deliver the Profits Investors Expect?

This is a crucial week for the AI investment story, with Alphabet, Microsoft and Amazon due to report results. Investors are looking beyond revenue growth to the economics of the AI build-out: capital expenditure, cloud demand, margins, cash generation and evidence that customers are willing to pay for AI products and services.

The market has become less forgiving. Recent weakness in technology shares and disappointment around some AI-related earnings have raised questions about whether the expected returns justify the scale of investment in data centres, chips and computing infrastructure.

Strong earnings alone may not be enough if companies simultaneously signal that spending will rise faster than revenue or that monetisation will take longer than expected.

The implications reach well beyond the technology sector. AI-related companies have played a major role in the broader equity rally, and their performance influences index returns, investor sentiment and demand for semiconductor and infrastructure suppliers.

If the largest technology companies demonstrate that AI investment is translating into durable earnings and cash flow, confidence could recover. If guidance disappoints, richly valued stocks may face further pressure, potentially affecting diversified portfolios through their exposure to major index constituents.

Investors should also distinguish between businesses that are spending heavily on AI and those that can demonstrate a credible commercial return. Infrastructure suppliers, cloud platforms and companies adopting AI may have very different revenue drivers, competitive positions and capital requirements.

Takeaway: Watch cash flow, capital expenditure, cloud growth and management guidance, not just headline earnings or excitement around AI. A compelling long-term technology theme does not guarantee that every company involved is attractively valued today.

## 4. The Growth Test: US Data and China's Uneven Recovery

This week brings important US economic releases, including the advance estimate of third-quarter GDP and September personal consumption expenditures inflation, alongside earnings from a broad range of companies.

The GDP figure will help investors assess the resilience of the US economy, while the PCE report will provide another indication of whether inflation is easing or becoming more persistent.

A combination of solid growth and sticky inflation could make it difficult for the Fed to loosen policy. Conversely, weaker growth alongside elevated prices would raise concerns about a stagflationary environment, in which companies face cost pressures while consumers and businesses become more cautious.

Neither scenario is straightforward for equity markets, and the market reaction will depend on how the data compare with expectations and what they imply for future policy.

China provides a different but equally important part of the global growth picture. Its economy grew 4.5% year on year in the third quarter, slightly ahead of expectations and up from 4.3% in the previous quarter.

September industrial production and retail sales also improved, but fixed-asset investment remained weak. The headline growth figure is therefore encouraging without resolving questions about the strength of domestic demand and investment.

For investors, China's performance affects more than Chinese equities. It influences demand for industrial commodities, Asian manufacturing, global luxury goods and the earnings outlook for multinational companies. A stronger recovery could support parts of the global economy, while persistent weakness in investment and consumer confidence would limit the benefit.

Takeaway: Look at growth and inflation together. Strong economic data are not automatically positive if they delay rate relief, and a headline recovery in China should be assessed alongside consumer spending and investment. The quality and durability of growth matter as much as the headline numbers.

## 5. Europe's Fiscal Pressure: French Borrowing Costs and Political Risk

European markets face a separate source of uncertainty as investors scrutinise French public finances and the political challenges surrounding the country's budget.

French borrowing costs have come under renewed pressure, with the premium investors demand to hold French government debt over safer euro-area benchmarks reaching its highest level since the eurozone debt crisis of 2011.

Higher borrowing costs can become self-reinforcing. They increase the expense of refinancing government debt, narrow the room for fiscal manoeuvre and can make investors more sensitive to political disagreements over spending, taxation and deficit reduction.

The concern is not necessarily that a crisis is imminent, but that a sustained rise in risk premiums could become an additional drag on confidence and financing conditions.

The implications extend to European equities, banks and the euro. Banks can be affected by changes in sovereign bond valuations and the relationship between government debt and financial-sector balance sheets.

Companies may also face a less supportive economic environment if borrowing costs remain elevated. At the same time, the UK Autumn Budget is approaching, putting fiscal policy and the government's borrowing plans on investors' radar.

Investors should avoid treating European government bonds as interchangeable. Fiscal positions, political stability and market liquidity can differ significantly between countries, and those differences may become more visible when global markets are already under pressure from energy prices and uncertain monetary policy.

Takeaway: Monitor sovereign yield spreads, budget developments and the response of the euro. In uncertain markets, the fiscal strength of the country behind a bond matters alongside its yield.

## Key Takeaways: Portfolio Themes to Consider

| Theme | What to consider |
|---|---|
| Oil and inflation | Review exposure to energy-intensive businesses and consider how sustained high energy costs could affect margins and household spending. Avoid assuming that a temporary supply disruption will quickly resolve. |
| Central-bank decisions | Assess portfolio sensitivity to interest rates. High valuations and long-duration assets can be vulnerable if markets push expected rate cuts further into the future. |
| AI and Big Tech earnings | Look for evidence of cash-flow generation, profitable growth and disciplined capital expenditure. Avoid relying solely on the AI narrative or recent share-price performance. |

| European fiscal risk | Monitor French borrowing spreads, budget negotiations and the euro. Diversification across assets and issuers can help reduce exposure to a single fiscal or political risk. |

## Final Word

This week is about testing assumptions. The assumption that inflation will ease, that central banks can soon become more supportive, and that AI investment will translate into profits all face important tests over the coming days.

Higher oil prices could complicate the inflation outlook. Central-bank guidance could shift expectations for borrowing costs. Big Tech earnings could either restore confidence in the AI trade or expose the gap between investment spending and financial returns. Meanwhile, European fiscal risks remind investors that government borrowing costs can become a market issue in their own right.

The answer is not to predict every headline or react to every market move. It is to understand what drives the companies and assets in a portfolio, assess what is already reflected in their prices, and maintain sensible diversification. Volatility can create opportunities, but only when the underlying investment case and the risks are understood.

The investor's advantage is not knowing tomorrow's headline. It is being prepared for what that headline could mean.
