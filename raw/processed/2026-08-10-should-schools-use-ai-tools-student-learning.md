# Should Schools Use Artificial Intelligence Tools for Student Learning?

Cognitive science shows students learn best when actively engaged, working through challenges, collaborating, and receiving meaningful feedback — conditions current AI tools may weaken by encouraging reliance on technology over independent thinking.

Key findings:
- Students using LLMs put forth less effort, feel less ownership, learn fewer new concepts, and produce less complete responses (Melumad & Yun, 2025).
- AI creates a false sense of learning: students who used GPT scored 17% lower on a math test than non-AI users once the tool was removed (Bastani et al., 2024); AI use can cause people to overestimate what they know (Matueny & Nyamai, 2025).
- Learning has a social component AI can't replace — generative AI can reduce student-to-student interaction (Hou et al., 2025) and create a "zone of no development" when AI assistance replaces productive struggle and teacher/peer support (dos Santos & Birdwell, 2025).
- Equity concerns: 20% of teens in households earning under $30,000 report doing most homework with AI, versus 7% of teens in households earning over $75,000 (Pew Research Center, 2026).

Policy recommendations: delay widespread adoption pending pilot data showing measurable learning improvements; restrict AI use in foundational reading/writing/math instruction; treat AI as a potentially high-risk educational intervention requiring peer-reviewed research and equity impact assessments; prioritize teacher-facing AI tools (lesson planning, rubrics, data analysis) over student-facing tools, and keep teachers as the final instructional decision-makers.
