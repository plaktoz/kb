# Antitrust Implications of the Build vs. Buy Playbook in the AI Era

### Summary

* Firms deciding whether to build or buy their AI capabilities face trade-offs involving cost, speed, access to data, customization needs, and long-term strategic control.
* The unique features of AI, especially rapid technological change and the role of data, can affect whether firms choose in-house development, acquisitions, or partnerships.
* Partnerships and acquisitions can accelerate innovation and market entry, but they may also create risks such as vendor lock-in, foreclosure, concentration, or reduced competition in some circumstances.
* Antitrust review should assess whether an AI acquisition or partnership displaces likely in-house entry, while also weighing potential efficiencies against risks of foreclosure and concentration.

## Introduction

In the context of merger control, antitrust authorities often inquire about the motive behind a proposed acquisition, especially when the acquiring company is perceived to have sufficient resources to build a product in-house. The central question becomes: why is the firm choosing to acquire another company’s product (or hold investment equity, or form strategic partnerships) rather than build its own? As stated in the 2023 US Merger Guidelines, “[i]n general, expansion into a concentrated market via internal growth rather than via acquisition benefits competition.”

Antitrust authorities are particularly interested in whether the proposed transaction reduces innovation competition by, for example, eliminating the need to develop an in-house product or service to compete against the target. The authorities have expressed concerns that these strategies can harm both short- and long-term consumer welfare.

Over the past several years, the artificial intelligence (AI) industry has experienced unprecedented growth and developed a fulsome value stack centered around foundation models, offering a wide range of applications across many industries. Figure 1 below visually summarizes examples of vendors at each layer of the AI value chain. Notably, while the foundation models stack covers a few broad categories (language, image generation, coding, and videos), those models can be applied to a variety of business functions and use cases, as shown in the applications stack. Since hardware, compute, and data are typically considered upstream inputs, the question of why partner with others rather than build one’s own AI models and tools focuses on the foundation model stack as well as the applications that directly build upon the foundation models.

This question has already raised concerns from antitrust agencies.

In this article, we explore under which circumstances firms may find it most efficient or procompetitive to build their own AI foundation models versus acquire or partner with another company to access the models. We focus on key features, namely the rapid pace of technological change and the role of data as an economic asset, that play a unique role in driving such business decisions related to AI models. The answer to the build versus buy question has meaningful implications for safeguarding competition among AI foundation models and their applications.

We recognize that, in many ways, the debate regarding the “build versus buy” strategy is not new. Firms routinely decide whether to insource or outsource software development, weighing the expected return on their investments against their costs and risks. In the AI space, similarly, the build versus buy decision is likely driven by specific cost-benefit trade-offs faced by each firm in the short and long run. What distinguishes the AI space from more traditional software is the pace of innovation and the role played by different types of data. In particular, we highlight that incumbency advantages may be weaker or less durable in certain AI end applications because training and deploying AI models involve positive and sometimes significant marginal compute costs, almost no user feedback loop, and quickly diminishing marginal value of historical data. Additionally, a policy environment that strongly encourages in-house development of AI models can have unintended consequences, such as the forgone benefits of having a general-purpose model, the proliferation of duplicative specific-purpose models, and, perhaps inadvertently, increased barriers to entry that discourage new competitors from developing AI models independently. Finally, while acquisitions and partnerships in the AI foundation model space have their own procompetitive merits, if left unchecked they may lead to foreclosure, concentration, and ultimately dampened competition. Therefore, we welcome antitrust agencies’ continued monitoring of developments in the AI space and recommend considering tools that promote open-source models, data interoperability, and/or non-exclusive partnerships or agreements as potential guardrails.

## Build versus Buy in AI

### 1. The Build Strategy.

For many companies that want to gain a foothold in the AI space, building their own AI capabilities has important pros and cons. The requirement of significant investment in compute, data, and talent resources to train a viable AI model means large upfront costs with uncertain financial return, at least in the short run. Additionally, for companies using AI to build final products, developing AI tools in-house could take a considerable amount of time, which could mean losing this precious time to go to market ahead of competitors. At the same time, an in-house AI model could give the company ample room to customize and improve the model to meet specific business needs, protect valuable intellectual property, and safeguard the proprietary data used by the models, which can translate into clear competitive advantages in the medium and long run. Recent statements by Meta illustrate this strategic consideration. Mark Zuckerberg and other Meta executives emphasized that investing in its proprietary AI model could preserve the firm’s strategic flexibility and supply chain leverage and create opportunities to pursue new customers and expand into new spaces in the long run.

Finally, once the company has developed its in-house AI offerings, it can avoid subscription or usage-based fees paid to third-party providers, which may be cost-effective in the long run.

Weighing the short- and long-run costs and benefits, any individual company’s decision to build in-house AI models will depend on its cost structure, access to external funding, risk tolerance, long-term business vision, and the competitive conditions it faces currently and expects to face in the future. Differences across firms in these dimensions result in similarly sized companies in the same industry taking opposite paths. Consider the automotive industry, for example. There, Tesla opted to build its own full-stack AI models for next-generation self-driving cars. Toyota instead partnered with Alphabet-owned Waymo to combine Waymo’s AI technology with Toyota’s manufacturing expertise. Finally, General Motors instead acquired Cruise to obtain autonomous-driving technology and talent, and subsequently integrated Cruise into its own in-house innovation efforts.

Among the key inputs of AI foundation models, data plays a unique role in shaping firms’ build decisions. Recent economic and business literature has recognized features that distinguish data from traditional, more tangible economic assets in the context of AI and machine learning (ML) technologies.

In certain platform industries, the value of user behavior data as an input of AI-powered recommendation algorithms could be highly temporal. For example, user data about best-selling movies, TV series, video games, or memes on social media quickly becomes less relevant after an initial period of time because users’ interest and content availability continually evolve.

In these industries, it may not be sufficient for firms to rely on historical data advantages. Instead, both established and new platforms may need to leverage more recent data and invest in their recommendation quality to stay competitive. Contemporaneous data may be less difficult for small players to collect, given its limited volume and the prevalence of multi-homing on both the user and content developer sides.

Given that competitors can collect more recent data, platforms have a strong incentive to build and maintain their own customized AI recommendation systems to differentiate their offerings from competitors. In the video streaming industry, for example, despite being a well-established incumbent, Netflix continuously updates its models to improve recommendation accuracy using advanced ML techniques tailored to evolving user preferences.

A better-known example lies in the social media industry. TikTok, once a new entrant with no historical user data, attracted mass attention thanks to its deep-learning–based recommendation system aimed at discovering content with the highest relevance to the user, capturing a share of user attention from incumbent social media platforms.

In addition to the success of Netflix and TikTok, we continue to observe multiple video streaming platforms (such as Disney+) and social media platforms (such as Instagram Reels and YouTube Shorts), each offering a differentiated value proposition and competing vigorously with AI-powered algorithms developed mostly in-house and with continued investment in fresh and differentiated content.

The Netflix and TikTok examples also demonstrate that several characteristics of data matter for the AI build versus buy decision. Data differs in whether it is proprietary, its recency, and its relevance to the intended use case. While user behavior data on video streaming platforms and social media platforms might be highly temporal and recent data can be accumulated by smaller, newer firms relatively quickly, other types of proprietary data such as financial transaction data and clinical health data could retain value for longer periods of time, and may be harder for rivals to reproduce at scale in a short period of time. The use of financial transaction data and clinical health data is also subject to more stringent regulation and compliance requirements. For companies that have already collected a large volume of high-quality proprietary data that are subject to stringent privacy rules, such data can be viewed as a unique, complementary asset that gives companies a cost advantage in building their own AI models and entering the next-generation innovation race. Companies are especially motivated to build when the proprietary data they own can be used to train AI models for their own core use cases and their proprietary data cannot be safely shared with an external vendor. The financial services industry provides prominent examples. JPMorgan Chase reportedly invested over $18 billion in its technology infrastructure in 2025 and budgeted another $19.8 billion in 2026, most of which is dedicated to in-house AI use cases, including an award-winning, proprietary, generative AI foundation model that enables more accurate and context-based financial predictions, analyses, and decision-making.

Other financial institutions have similarly invested heavily in their in-house AI research capabilities on core use cases such as credit underwriting models or fraud detection, where the input data used to train the models include proprietary customer transaction-level data, behavioral data, financial history data, and identity data.

On the other hand, when the data is not subject to privacy rules, is not proprietary but instead is gathered from open sources, is publicly available, and/or can be regenerated by synthetic methods, it is said to be “nonrival” and can be repurposed to train models for different use cases. Data nonrivalry is typically characterized by increasing returns to scale, and promoting broad use of the data by multiple AI developers has significant potential to improve consumer welfare.

In certain instances, companies may recognize the distinct value of building ML platforms (not AI foundation models per se) by leveraging access to nonrival data for long-term strategic reasons. This is especially true for companies in technology sectors that offer a multiplicity of first- and third-party services to their users. Salient examples include Amazon and Google. Amazon’s AWS Data Exchange houses a multitude of nonrival datasets related to finance, healthcare, retail, geolocation, and scientific research. Amazon’s fully managed ML platform, SageMaker, allows users to leverage data available on the Exchange to build, train, and deploy special-use AI foundation models.

Notably, Amazon has leveraged SageMaker to build its own Titan AI foundation model.

Similarly, Google offers a managed ML platform called Vertex AI that allows users to connect to nonrival datasets housed on BigQuery to train and fine-tune AI models.

Google has also built its in-house foundation models on the Vertex AI platform.

In these examples, Amazon and Google are not only building their own foundation models, but also recognize the potential to gain long-term competitive advantage from building a *platform* for third parties to build AI models on by leveraging the value of nonrival data.

In summary, a company’s decision to build its own AI offerings may be due to cost-benefit trade-offs it faces in the short run versus long run and could be shaped further by the type of data available to train the AI models. As explained in the next section, a company’s assessment of costs and benefits, rather than anticompetitive motives, also could drive its decision to buy or partner with a third-party AI developer.

### 2. The Buy or Partner Strategy.

Opting to buy or partner to deploy AI capabilities can yield substantial benefits, particularly when a firm’s internal resources—its proprietary data, staff, and/or computational infrastructure—are insufficient to develop a competitive model quickly and efficiently on its own.

In particular, as discussed in the previous section, data plays a unique role: firms building AI-based final products may have the financial resources to purchase the computational infrastructure or hire talent, but data could remain an entry barrier to build AI models from scratch, especially when nonrival data does not exist or is insufficient to train models to meet specific product goals. In addition, proprietary data may take time to accumulate to achieve minimum viable scale sufficient to train the AI models. In such circumstances, licensing or integrating a third‑party AI product allows the firm to achieve adoption without potentially losing valuable time to go to market. AI partnerships can also alleviate liability risks companies may face, because the partnering AI vendors typically bear the regulatory obligations of addressing safety concerns raised by consumers.

Due to AI vendors’ specialized knowledge and economies of scale from partnering with multiple firms, AI vendors can be better positioned to address safety concerns than the individual firms can.

The buying or partnering approach is particularly compelling when the AI component is general purpose (for example, a large language model (LLM) or image-­recognition engine) rather than domain specific. The firm sidesteps the burden of reinventing foundational components, focusing instead on non-foundational aspects such as integration and differentiation of final products.

Buying or partnering with another firm could deliver an additional layer of efficiency: by multi-homing, companies can buy or partner with more than one AI model developer and test the partners’ near-term capabilities, without incurring full-scale internal development costs. This in turn increases the likelihood of successful deployment of AI models that meet the companies’ business needs. Indeed, we have seen big tech companies involved in partnerships with multiple AI model developers in parallel.

Nevertheless, this strategy carries inherent trade-offs. Notably, in the long run, the adopting firm may become reliant on the provider of AI capabilities and face constraints in customizing the model. In fact, more significant concerns may arise when companies become overly reliant on third-party AI vendors, including diminished control over proprietary data. In addition, because licensing or partnership typically requires the company to pay certain fees or commit certain financial investments to access the partner’s AI technology, costs may add up to a nontrivial figure, especially compared with owning an in-house AI model that can be utilized at marginal cost.

Partnership example in healthcare. The motivation to form AI partnerships may be even more pronounced in regulated or technically specialized sectors such as healthcare. Due to stringent regulatory validation, privacy safeguards, and a lack of specialized expertise in training and fine-tuning AI models, constructing fully homegrown AI systems in such environments often incurs prohibitively high costs and risks, especially for small and midsize companies. To illustrate, some healthcare systems have forged partnerships with cloud providers or specialized AI vendors to integrate predictive risk scoring, workflow automation, or diagnostic tools. These collaborations allow clinical institutions to leverage tested AI modules and focus internal effort on implementation. For example, Mayo Clinic partnered with [hellocare.ai](http://hellocare.ai/), an AI-assisted virtual care platform, to advance ambient clinical intelligence.

Pharmaceutical companies including GSK, Merck, Bayer, Bristol Myers Squibb, and AstraZeneca have partnered with AI startups to develop models and applications that can reshape drug discovery, clinical trials, and manufacturing landscapes.

Lessons from past technological innovation. The incentive for firms to buy or partner is not unique to AI. Similar dynamics have played out across past waves of technological transformation. In the smartphone era, hardware manufacturers such as Samsung opted to use Google’s Android operating system rather than build their own, allowing Samsung to leverage a mature mobile ecosystem and compete more effectively with Apple.

In cloud computing, Netflix migrated to Amazon Web Services rather than building its own global server infrastructure—a decision driven by scalability and cost-efficiency, despite Amazon also being a direct competitor in the video streaming market.

In the search engine space, Yahoo’s choice to outsource search to Microsoft’s Bing shows how firms may retreat from in-house development when market dynamics or technical complexity render such efforts inefficient.

There are many parallels between these cases and the AI context. The drive to reduce costs and accelerate time to market has been well acknowledged by companies involved in past waves of technological innovation.

On the flip side, firms that decide to buy or partner, rather than build their own AI technology, could face vendor lock-in, where heavy reliance on a third-party provider can limit their flexibility to switch vendors or develop in-house alternatives in the future. This dependency may also reduce the firm’s bargaining power in negotiations. For example, hospitals that adopt AI diagnostic tools from specialized vendors may become overly reliant on the vendors’ proprietary models, algorithms, and model parameters that have been trained and fine-tuned based on the hospital’s own data. If the vendor raises prices or discontinues support, the hospital may face challenges identifying an alternative vendor capable of replicating the model’s performance in a timely manner, potentially requiring substantial reinvestment in implementation, validation, and deployment of the AI models to ensure continuity of care.

Vendor lock-in has been extensively studied in the economics literature, focusing on how switching costs, network effects, and path dependence can create dependencies that limit firms’ flexibility and bargaining power. Notably, as discussed earlier, one feature that distinguishes AI models from the previous waves of technological advancement is the rapid pace of innovation and the repeated observation of competing AI providers leapfrogging one another. This dynamic could create a countervailing force against vendor lock-in because vendors who fail to innovate can quickly fall behind others. Nevertheless, because customers’ switching costs could arise from factors other than technological superiority, such as organizational integration and customization, vendor lock-in could still emerge in the AI segment. Seminal work by Farrell and Klemperer shows that while switching costs can grant incumbents market power by binding users to a platform, they can also promote initial investments and innovation by securing a stable user base.

The UK Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) also highlights the risk of lock‑in in its updated AI technical report, noting that heavy reliance on dominant foundation model providers can constrain choice, raise barriers to switching, and entrench incumbents.

In sum, whether the AI segment will exhibit persistent vendor lock-in or continued leapfrogging remains an empirical question.

Finally, the buy or partner pathway also offers a vital entry route for new entrants. By embedding third‑party AI models, firms can avoid the steep fixed costs and talent constraints of base‑model development. The role of acquisition or partnership in promoting entry has been observed repeatedly in past waves of technological innovation as well. For example, many brick-and-mortar retailers and restaurants partnered with third-party platforms such as Uber and DoorDash to enter the online delivery space rather than build their own delivery platforms.

A more flexible, hybrid AI strategy. Notably, we are starting to see companies adopting a more flexible “hybrid” strategy where they build in-house AI models for core use cases and partner with third-party AI model developers for non-core use cases.

The pharmaceutical manufacturer Roche offers a good example: through its subsidiary Genentech, Roche has used its own research and clinical trials data to train proprietary AI models to predict drug targets and therapeutic molecules for drug discovery and development. Meanwhile, Roche has expanded its digital pathology capabilities through partnerships with multiple AI labs and startups, offering a wide range of diagnostic tools such as cancer detection and analysis.

## Implications for Competition

Competition economists and regulators generally view organic, in-house capability development as more conducive to preserving competition than growth through acquisition. The concern is that mergers, particularly involving large incumbents acquiring nascent or innovative startup firms, can narrow future avenues of competition and weaken the dynamic rivalry that drives innovation. The literature on “killer acquisitions” formalizes this intuition: incumbents may acquire promising entrants to discontinue overlapping research or prevent future market entry.

Reflecting this, the 2023 US Department of Justice and Federal Trade Commission Merger Guidelines emphasize scrutiny of acquisitions that may substantially lessen competition.

Agency preferences for organic growth through in-house capability development are rooted in an effort to maintain independent centers of innovation and avoid the potential of market tipping where such conduct may substantially lessen competition.

Underlying any preference for organic growth is the implicit assumption that but for the acquisition or partnership, the acquiring/partnering company will choose to build its own product or services and compete with the existing technologies via *de novo* entry, such that there would be more competitive markets if the acquisition or partnership is not allowed to proceed.

In other words, the acquisition or partnership is assumed to have a “replacement effect” with respect to the build strategy, thus reducing potential competition that is important to price and other market outcomes. However, in the context of AI models, the competitive logic underpinning that conventional wisdom may be less clear-cut. If firms receive the strong signal from agencies that only by building in-house large-scale AI foundation models can they avoid regulatory risks of acquisitions or partnerships, some firms may decide not to build any AI models due to large upfront costs, near-term risks of failure, lack of key inputs (in particular, data) that take time to acquire, and competing alternative business priorities. Firms may be further disincentivized to enter a space with their in-house AI capabilities when they expect everyone else to build and are not in a uniquely advantageous position to gain market share. In other words, a blanket skepticism toward AI acquisitions or partnerships may not result in more *de novo* entry.

Even when firms decide to build in-house AI models, the sector may suffer from duplicated fixed costs and fragmented innovation efforts. In addition, the rapid pace of AI innovation creates substantial obsolescence risks, especially for firms that lack unique proprietary data, expertise, or other assets that can be leveraged in AI development. Foundation models are characterized by massive upfront costs in compute, data, and talent, coupled with uncertainties regarding technical success and the speed with which new rival models may render the success obsolete in a short period of time. These features make shared infrastructure or collaboration potentially more efficient than parallel development. That is, vertical integration and cross-layer partnerships across the AI stack can significantly enhance innovation and growth by allowing firms to internalize the benefits of their breakthroughs that spill over to other players, access compute more efficiently through partnerships rather than traditional financing methods, and optimally choose to develop the most value-added applications across the AI stack. Such arrangements can improve incentives for innovation where the social benefits of AI breakthroughs exceed private returns earned from standalone AI model development.

To see the potential efficiencies delivered from acquisitions and partnerships to access AI models, one ought to recognize that the economic mechanisms that drove concentration during the Web 2.0 era, including strong network effects, zero pricing, and powerful data feedback loops, may be much less pronounced in the generative AI market.

Network effects are often hindered because current AI models serve individual users rather than facilitate multisided interactions, and data incumbency advantages may be more limited because the models rely largely on nonproprietary datasets. As a result, incumbents may not always enjoy the same self-reinforcing advantages that previously fueled market tipping. Under these conditions, partnerships and acquisitions may foster competition by accelerating technological diffusion and allowing established firms to deploy proven AI capabilities more quickly. The availability of collaboration or licensing channels may also encourage small, independent labs and open-source communities to innovate, knowing that commercialization pathways exist outside full vertical integration.

Despite these potential efficiencies, unchecked integration and partnership strategies may also lead to enhanced market power and create avenues for foreclosure and concentration under some circumstances.

Control over critical inputs such as compute, data, and talent could turn early foundation model developers into indispensable intermediaries able to extract rents and steer downstream innovation.

Similarly, vertical integration along the AI stack may allow incumbents to leverage upstream control to shape downstream market access.

These dynamics mirror the risks observed in past platform markets, where open ecosystems gradually evolved into closed systems that sometimes received significant antitrust scrutiny. The key policy challenge is thus to balance innovation incentives generated from partnerships and vertical integration against foreclosure and exclusion concerns. Agencies should carefully and closely monitor the development of the marketplace dynamics and, when appropriate, consider interventions to preserve competition while maintaining incentives for innovation.

Promoting the use and application of open-source AI models can be a potentially effective guardrail to ensure that barriers to advancing AI remain low for small firms and new entrants. Recent studies have highlighted the speed at which open-source AI models are closing the gap with closed-source AI models in terms of performance. Figure 2 shows that leading open-source models have significantly narrowed the gap with leading closed-source models in terms of technical performance.

Still, some scholars have expressed caution about the safety risks of open-source models.

And the mechanisms underlying this convergence remain a subject of debate.

Encouraging multi-homing could be another avenue to mitigate competition risks. We have witnessed examples of multi-homing in AI model partnerships and have explained how that can increase the likelihood of successful model development. When firms are encouraged to engage with multiple partners, competition could be intensified, with less room for foreclosure or other exclusionary conduct. Notably, the AI marketplace may inherently facilitate multi-homing more than the previous waves of technological advancement, because of tools and interfaces designed to make it easy for firms to access and switch among multiple AI models simultaneously.

Finally, we recognize that partnerships among AI firms could raise questions about coordinated effects. For example, if multiple firms rely on the same AI vendor, some may be concerned that the relationship could, under some circumstances, soften competition and facilitate coordination. While such concerns may be valid in certain settings, their applicability to the AI marketplace remains uncertain. The rapid pace of innovation and investment, together with the repeated episodes of multiple firms leapfrogging one another, suggests that firms likely have a stronger incentive to steal business from each other than to coordinate on prices or innovation efforts, at least in the short run. A closer examination may be warranted when the marketplace evolves into a more stable oligopolistic setting.

## Conclusion

The debate over “build versus buy” and its implications for potential competition, innovation, and entry is not new in antitrust enforcement history. In the context of AI, however, enforcers and practitioners should pay close attention to the distinctive features of the marketplace and assess the specific facts when evaluating firms’ decisions to acquire or partner with third parties to access AI foundation models. The acquisition or partnership decision could be driven by a cost-benefit analysis that takes into account the firm’s cost structure, access to key inputs (especially data), value of intellectual property, need for highly customized models, and the observed speed of innovation and leapfrogging by rivals. Forgoing development of an in-house AI model (and therefore reducing potential competition) may not be a driver of the acquisition/partnership decision.

The AI industry bears unique features that distinguish it from the prior wave of digital platform innovation in important ways: rapid innovation and leapfrogging, the quickly diminishing value of some historical data, the availability of nonrival data, and weaker network effects and data feedback loops. Therefore, a stringent policy against AI partnerships or acquisitions may have unintended consequences, such as the forgone benefits of having more general-purpose models, duplicative specific-purpose models, and elevated entry barriers. While cautioning against the risks of foreclosure and concentration, we agree that enforcers ought to closely monitor the development of this industry and consider certain tools that preserve competition while maintaining incentives for innovation and investment.
