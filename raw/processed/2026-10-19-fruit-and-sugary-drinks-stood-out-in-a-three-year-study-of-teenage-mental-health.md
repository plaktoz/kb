# Fruit and sugary drinks stood out in a three-year study of teenage mental health

Could the foods teenagers eat and the drinks they choose offer clues to their mental health? Researchers followed thousands of Australian students over three years to investigate the connection.

Study: Longitudinal associations between dietary intake and common mental disorders among adolescents in Australia. Image Credit: Pixel-Shot / Shutterstock

In a recent study published in the journal Child and Adolescent Mental Health, researchers investigated whether greater fruit and vegetable intake and lower sugar-sweetened beverage (SSB) consumption were linked to common mental disorder (CMD) risk among adolescents in Australia. While greater fruit intake was linked to lower risks of meeting screening thresholds for depression and anxiety, higher SSB intake was associated with greater risk, with a numerically stronger association for anxiety among males. Fruit intake had an inverse relationship with both outcomes, with depression risk 13% lower and anxiety risk 7% lower for each additional daily serving. Based on the findings, everyday dietary choices may be relevant to adolescent mental health and inform early prevention strategies.

What children and teenagers eat may affect their mental well-being, but the evidence is not yet consistent. Much of the research so far has captured diet and mental health at a single point in time, while studies that followed young people over longer periods have reached different conclusions. More research is needed to clarify this relationship.

Dietary habits established during childhood and adolescence may have lasting implications for mental well-being and could represent an important area for early prevention. Because diet is potentially modifiable, exploring associations between specific dietary factors and CMD risk could help healthcare providers develop interventions to prevent or delay CMD onset.

## About the study

In the present study, researchers investigated whether fruit, vegetable and SSB consumption was associated with meeting screening thresholds for anxiety and depression over three years among 6,537 adolescents (mean age, approximately 13 years; about half were female) across 71 schools in Australia.

Depression and anxiety outcomes were determined based on binary cut-offs in validated symptom scales, rather than clinical diagnoses. To assess depression among the participants, the team evaluated eight items of the Patient Health Questionnaire-Adolescent (PHQ-A-8), excluding assessment of thoughts of suicide. Anxiety levels were evaluated using the 13-item Patient-Reported Outcomes Measurement Information System Anxiety-Pediatric (PROMIS-AP) scale.

Participants attended secondary schools in New South Wales, Queensland, and Western Australia, and had previously taken part in the Health4Life trial. They completed digital surveys at baseline (Grade 7, 12-13 years old) and again after one, two, and three years to report their daily intake of vegetables, fruit, and SSBs. A portion size of 150 grams represented one serving of fruit, while one serving of vegetables was 75 grams, consistent with the National Health and Medical Research Council (NHMRC) guidelines. SSB intake was based on cups of sport drinks, soft drinks, or cordials, including lemonade, Coke, and Gatorade, reported weekly or daily and converted into cups per day.

The team used statistical models to assess associations between self-reported dietary intake and CMD risk over time, accounting for sex, age, physical activity, trial allocation, timepoint, and family affluence. To assess participants' socioeconomic background, the researchers evaluated family affluence using the FAS III questionnaire. The results were stratified by sex. In sensitivity analyses, the team tested models with fewer covariates and assessed the influence of missing data. Participants who met the relevant symptom threshold at baseline, or who lacked baseline outcome information, were excluded to focus on symptoms that increased during follow-up.

## Results and discussion

Family affluence scores indicated medium-to-high socioeconomic status among participants. Over time, females generally had higher fruit and vegetable intake, whereas males consumed SSBs more frequently. Adolescents who ate more fruit tended to have lower risks of elevated symptoms of depression and anxiety. The findings were similar between sexes for depression, but the fruit-anxiety association was not statistically significant when each sex was analyzed separately.

In the adjusted models, greater vegetable consumption showed no statistically significant association with depression or anxiety. By comparison, greater SSB consumption was linked to an increased risk of meeting CMD symptom thresholds. The positive link between SSB intake and CMD risk was observed in both sexes, with larger risk estimates among males, especially for anxiety. The analyses did not establish whether the apparent sex difference was statistically meaningful. The researchers used multiple imputation to estimate missing survey values in the main analyses. Analyses with fewer covariates supported the main findings, but complete-case analyses no longer showed significant associations between fruit and anxiety overall or between fruit and depression among males.

The researchers proposed that the SSB associations might involve circulating inflammatory biomarkers or hormones such as cortisol, both of which may influence mood. This study did not test these mechanisms, and SSB consumption may also reflect other dietary and behavioral patterns that were not fully measured. Future studies should investigate causal associations and explore the biological pathways that may contribute to internalizing symptoms.

## Conclusion and Future Directions

The findings suggest that adolescents who ate more fruit and drank fewer SSBs were less likely to screen positive for anxiety and depression during the three-year study period. The associations were more consistent for SSBs than for fruit, and no significant association was found for vegetables. This may be especially relevant since adolescence is a period of heightened sensitivity to environmental influences. The observational design, self-reported diet, potential unmeasured confounding, and reliance on symptom screening mean the findings cannot establish whether dietary changes prevent mental disorders. If further studies confirm these associations, health policies and school programs could be designed to improve dietary habits among young people as part of broader mental health prevention efforts.

Future studies could examine how dietary components, including lean proteins, healthy fats, whole grains, and ultra-processed foods (UPFs), relate to CMD risk and whether better adolescent eating habits could reduce the long-term burden of these disorders. The influence of potential confounders such as race, ethnicity, total energy intake per day, household dietary patterns, family structure, and history of mental health disorders in families should also be explored in both sexes.
