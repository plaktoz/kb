# Why reducing protein intake could promote healthy aging, according to new research

While recent trends have pushed higher protein intake, a new review published in Cell Press — combing over 350 papers on animal and human trials — suggests that restricting overall dietary protein, or specific essential amino acids, could improve health span and extend life span similarly to caloric restriction.

Researchers found eating less protein helped the body burn more calories, reduced body fat, and better managed insulin via increased Fibroblast Growth Factor 21 (FGF21), a hormone that protects against aging. A low-protein diet also inhibited mTORC1 and activated GCN2 — nutrient sensors that regulate cell growth and stress response — telling cells to slow aging and clear cellular "trash" through autophagy. It also reduced senescent ("zombie") cells that drive background inflammation, and improved mitochondrial function while decreasing reactive oxygen species damage. In animal studies, lab animals fed less protein lived longer with lower age-related frailty.

The review specifically identifies three essential amino acids — isoleucine, valine, and methionine — that appear beneficial when restricted, plus nonessential amino acids tyrosine and cysteine. It also found three amino acids (glycine, serine, proline) that may be beneficial when supplemented rather than restricted, so the effect isn't simply "eat less protein" across the board.

Outside dietitians cautioned that higher protein intake remains important for muscle mass, strength, and recovery, especially with aging and exercise — the findings shouldn't be read as blanket advice to eat less protein, but as evidence that specific amino acid composition matters as much as total intake. The study focuses on cellular longevity (keeping cells healthy at a molecular level) rather than functional longevity (staying strong and mobile), which is where most prior nutrition research has focused. It also notes that protein restriction could be harmful for adults over 65, who face higher risk of frailty and sarcopenia (muscle wasting) from insufficient protein. Plant protein, compared with animal protein, has separately been associated with lower frailty risk. Much of the underlying research is still animal-based, and experts say more long-term human data is needed.
