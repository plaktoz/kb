# Stop Multitasking: Science-Backed Habits for Deep Work Productivity

Most people treat multitasking like a badge of competence — open tabs, Slack pings, email checks, half-finished notes feel like progress, but rarely are. What looks like juggling is almost always rapid task-switching, and every switch carries a measurable cost.

When you move from one demanding task to another, your brain must unload the rules and goals of the first task and load the rules of the second; that reconfiguration takes time and accuracy. The American Psychological Association's review of the research estimates that these switch costs can reduce effective productivity by up to 40 percent on complex work.

Gloria Mark's field studies at UC Irvine found that after a typical interruption it takes an average of 23 minutes and 15 seconds to return to the original task at the same level of engagement. Sophie Leroy's work on "attention residue" shows that part of your attention often stays stuck on the unfinished task, so the new task receives only partial cognitive resources.
