# Want to Slow Your Biological Aging? Sleeping 6.4 to 7.8 Hours a Night May Help

A study published in Nature found that too little or too much sleep may speed aging in the brain and other organs, extending prior research that had linked short sleep to faster brain aging specifically.

The research team, led by Junhao Wen, PhD, of Columbia University, used data from half a million UK Biobank participants and machine learning to build 23 "aging clocks" spanning 17 organs — computational models estimating how much faster or slower a person's tissues are aging relative to their chronological age. They then compared self-reported sleep duration against these biological age estimates, defining too little sleep as under 6 hours and too much as over 8 hours.

People who slept too little or too much showed faster biological aging across the body than those sleeping between 6.4 and 7.8 hours a night — a U-shaped pattern. Short sleep was linked to immune dysregulation, increased systemic inflammation, disrupted glucose regulation, and higher sympathetic nervous system activity, all of which can accelerate cellular aging. Long sleep may instead often reflect an underlying illness that itself drives both the extra sleep and the faster aging, rather than the sleep causing it.

Short sleep was associated with brain disorders like depression and anxiety, plus physical conditions including obesity, type 2 diabetes, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, and heart arrhythmias. Both short and long sleep were linked to COPD, asthma, and gastrointestinal disorders like GERD and gastritis.

The study also found sleep duration may influence late-life depression: mediation analysis suggested too little sleep directly affects the disease burden of late-life depression, while too much sleep appears to act through a separate pathway involving brain and fat-tissue aging clocks. The CDC recommends at least 7 hours of sleep for adults 18–60, 7–9 hours for ages 61–64, and 7–8 hours for 65 and older. Experts suggest building in an 8-hour sleep window (even if actual sleep is closer to 7 hours) and keeping a consistent wake time to stabilize circadian rhythm.
