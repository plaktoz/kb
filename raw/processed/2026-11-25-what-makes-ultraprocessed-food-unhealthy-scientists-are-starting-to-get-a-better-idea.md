# What makes ultraprocessed food unhealthy? Scientists are starting to get a better idea

*These foods seem to displace healthy nutrients, pack in more calories per gram of food, and be associated with hyperpalatability. But we still don't know exactly how they worsen health.*

There's plenty of evidence linking a diet replete in ultraprocessed foods (UPFs) to poor health outcomes ([1]). And that doesn't just mean obesity. Studies highlight more than 30 health conditions, as well as all-cause mortality ([2]). One meta-analysis published in September tied UPF consumption to major chronic diseases, based on observational data from nearly 9 million people ([3]).

No single study makes a clear-cut case about the roots of such ailments. And observational studies, which dominate the research to date, face numerous limitations including often imprecise measurements of what people actually eat. But for many scientists, the cumulative research creates a persuasive picture. "The evidence all points in the same direction: Diets high in UPFs are bad for health," says Marion Nestle, an emeritus professor of nutrition at New York University.

The issue has drawn national and international attention. Labeling UPFs "poison," US Health and Human Services Secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. is targeting them as the main culprit in diet-related chronic disease. For the first time, US dietary guidelines, published in January, explicitly advise Americans to limit certain highly processed foods. And in September, California Governor Gavin Newsom signed legislation to create a standardized label for non-ultraprocessed foods. The World Health Organization, meanwhile, is developing global guidelines.

Still, the many unknowns about how UPFs might cause harm—and how to define them—have seeded disagreement about what measures to take. "I don't even like the name ultraprocessed foods, because it might be focusing on the wrong thing," says Kevin Hall, a prominent nutrition researcher who was with the NIH until 2025 and is now at AstraZeneca. "Is it the processing per se, or is it the formulation?"

Clearly, the displacement of healthy nutrients is part of the story. And early results suggest that increased energy density, or more calories per gram of food, and hyperpalatability—combinations of high levels of fat and sodium, fat and sugar, or carbohydrates and sodium—have real effects on obesity. Other suspects, such as chemical additives and toxic contaminants, are also gaining attention.

Meanwhile, the list of purported health impacts grows, with depression and cancer among the possible downstream effects. More than half of US adults now have metabolic disorders or other chronic diseases linked to diets high in UPFs, Hall says. "I think that we are getting to some explanations, and like all good science, you explain some things and you raise other questions." Further answers could have significant implications for nutrition science and policy—not to mention supermarket shelves.

## Convenience at What Cost?

Starting in the 1990s, nutritional epidemiologist Carlos Monteiro, now an emeritus professor at the University of São Paulo in Brazil, became among the first to shine a light on the problem. He was seeking to explain the obesity epidemic when he identified a dimension of diet and health that seemed neglected: so-called "industrial processing." His team went on to develop a food classification system that gave researchers around the world a common framework to quantify UPF consumption, test its associations with health outcomes, and begin to isolate which characteristics matter ([4]).

When Hall began leveraging that framework to investigate the role of UPFs in obesity, he presumed that conventional nutritional characteristics would account for the association. "I thought that the main drivers of overeating would be how much sugar and refined carbs were there," he says.

Results from his landmark 2019 study changed his mind ([5]). Hall and his coauthors randomized adults to ultraprocessed and unprocessed diets that had similar amounts of calories, protein, sugar, salt, carbohydrates, fiber, and fat. (Some differences inevitably remained, including amounts of saturated fat.) They found that participants consumed far more calories (about 500 additional) per day on the ultraprocessed diet, while feeling just as full as they did when eating unprocessed foods.

Why these participants overate remained unclear. Some researchers speculated that people simply ate UPFs so quickly that their satiety signals couldn't keep up. But when Hall and his team studied their data using a statistical technique called a mediation analysis, two other possible drivers emerged: high energy density and hyperpalatability ([6]).

That finding informed a follow-up study, the results of which Hall presented in a 2025 lecture at Oxford University. His team tested three ultraprocessed diets that varied in energy density and palatability. Compared with a minimally processed diet, the hyperpalatable, energy-dense version led participants to consume an average of nearly 950 additional calories per day. Reducing the number of hyperpalatable foods in the diet narrowed that difference by about 160 calories; lowering energy density as well cut it by another 660 calories. Importantly, participants didn't eat the ultraprocessed meals at a faster rate. "This suggests that we're starting to get at the mechanisms," Hall says, alluding to the roles of hyperpalatability and energy density.

Participants lost weight on both the "low-low" ultraprocessed diet and the minimally processed diet. However, only the minimally processed diet produced a loss of body fat. Hall isn't sure exactly why. The study tested each diet for only one week. Still, the results align with a separate randomized trial coauthored by Hall, in which participants lost more weight during eight weeks on a minimally processed diet than on an ultraprocessed one ([7]).

Other research adds evidence that excess calories are likely not the sole culprit when it comes to the obesity connection. One small, randomized trial fixed the total calories among participants and linked an ultraprocessed diet with several adverse outcomes, including increased fat mass, body weight, and hormonal changes ([8]).

There are caveats. Ciarán Forde, a sensory scientist at Wageningen University in the Netherlands, argues that the health impact of UPFs depends on how they are engineered. His own small, randomized trial found that UPFs with textural properties that slow how quickly people eat reduced intake by an average of nearly 370 calories a day compared to UPFs with properties that raise eating speed ([9]). "You can gain, keep the same, or lose weight on a UPF diet," Forde says.

## Bigger Picture

Answers are likely only to arise with much larger studies. A French population-based cohort is leading the way.

The NutriNet-Santé study has enrolled more than 183,000 participants since its launch in 2009. The web-based study attempts to overcome some of the shortcomings common in observational studies. Food-frequency questionnaires, for example, may not capture the ingredient-level detail needed to accurately classify foods, such as distinguishing ultraprocessed oatmeal from non-ultraprocessed oatmeal, notes Kevin Klatt, a molecular nutritionist and dietitian at the University of Toronto in Canada. In the end, he says, UPFs known to be associated with poor health outcomes—sugary drinks, refined grains, and processed meats—can get lumped together with foods that might be protective against chronic disease, such as whole grains and yogurt.

Every six months, NutriNet-Santé participants detail everything they eat and drink—including brand and product names—over three 24-hour periods: two weekdays and one weekend day. Participants also periodically complete questionnaires that cover demographic, lifestyle, and further relevant factors, including exposure to environmental pollutants. Even after accounting for many of these potential confounders, as well as intake of calories, sugar, salt, fiber, and other dietary components, investigators have uncovered statistically significant associations between UPF intake and numerous health conditions—from Crohn's disease to various cancers.

The study's level of detail is also helping researchers explore possible mechanisms, says study leader Mathilde Touvier, a nutritional epidemiologist at the French National Institute for Health and Medical Research. For example, based on blood, urine, and stool samples from a subset of participants, they have linked greater UPF consumption with elevated markers of inflammation. "These biomarkers could mediate the associations between ultraprocessed food and cardiometabolic diseases," she says.

The study has started to reveal the roles of food additives and contaminants as well. So far, researchers have connected artificial sweeteners to type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease, as well as nitrites and nitrates to certain cancers ([10]–[12]). Touvier and colleagues have results under review examining contaminants created during processing, such as acrylamide, polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons, polyfluoroalkyl substances, and brominated flame retardants.

Touvier is particularly concerned about contaminants from product packaging and whether they migrate into processed foods over months or years of storage or leach into microwavable meals. Heat can release microplastics and chemical additives. To garner greater clarity, study participants can now scan barcodes, allowing investigators to distinguish between, for example, a Diet Coke sold in a metallic can versus one sold in a plastic bottle.

## Beyond the Waistline

And there are preliminary indications that UPFs are having impacts on other diseases. A rise in colorectal cancer among young people in recent years, for example, has some scientists again pointing to UPFs as a contributing factor ([13]).

"We're seeing troubling trends—early signals that there is something changing in our environment or lifestyle that could influence cancer rates," says Andrew Chan, a gastroenterologist at Harvard Medical School in Massachusetts. In a paper published online ahead of print last November, Chan and his team tracked nearly 30,000 women under 50 for 14 years and found 45% higher odds of precancerous colorectal growths in women consuming a diet high in UPFs compared to those with the lowest consumption ([14]). The finding held after adjusting for confounders such as obesity.

Other recent studies zero in on changes in the microbiome ([15]). Components of UPFs may disrupt the balance of gut bacteria that maintain gut lining integrity, Chan says, and also affect the balance of healthy metabolites—by-products of bacteria as they break down dietary components. "There are multiple ways in which these foods can disrupt the microbiome, because they are so different from the foods that we evolved eating," says Filippa Juul, a nutritional epidemiologist at SUNY Downstate Health Sciences University, in Brooklyn, New York.

The potential harms of artificial sweeteners, such as impaired glucose tolerance, may stem in part from changes they make to the microbiome ([16]). Emulsifiers are another leading suspect. Human and animal studies show that certain emulsifiers can alter gut microbiota in a way that provokes inflammation and damage to the bowels ([17], [18]). In one study that exposed human microbiota derived from fecal samples to 20 commonly used emulsifiers, carrageenans and gums proved particularly detrimental. Another study showed that a low-emulsifier diet may ease Crohn's disease symptoms ([19]).

The well-documented mind–gut connection could partly account for another troubling outcome. "These studies all show the same thing: more ultraprocessed food, more depression," Monteiro says. For example, a study from NutriNet-Santé found that with each 10-percentage-point increase in UPFs as a proportion of the diet, the risk of new depressive symptoms was 21 percent higher ([20]).

The effect might even snowball. "It's a vicious cycle where these products further exacerbate low mood through inflammation and the microbiome, making you more vulnerable to go back," says Ashley Gearhardt, a psychologist who focuses on food and addiction at the University of Michigan. "That bag of potato chips looks a lot more appealing when you're feeling low."

Gearhardt co-authored a randomized pilot study offering minimally processed meals to people with moderate-to-severe depression; symptom severity dropped to mild ([21]). "The only intervention was the nature of food provided," she says. Other randomized and prospective studies echo the finding—either implicating UPFs in the onset of depression or showing that a minimally processed diet eases symptoms ([22]–[24]).

## Breaking the Matrix

Scientists caution that even as they chase individual mechanisms, the real picture is probably more complex. Humans evolved to eat nutrients bundled together within a food's natural matrix, and not as isolated components extracted and recombined. "We, as any living organism, eat other living organisms," Monteiro says. "We cannot eat rocks."

Traditional diets are the result of experiments—in Asia, in Africa, in Latin America. Each society developed dietary patterns that combined different whole foods, he explains. Processed foods, on the other hand, rely on food substances, like protein isolates, oils, and fats. Because these "are not together in the same food matrix," Monteiro says, they are absorbed and used by the body very differently.

Consider fiber, for example. UPFs tend to be relatively low in fiber, although some are fortified with refined fiber. Those added fibers can have benefits. But research shows that certain refined fibers may also disrupt the microbiome and increase inflammation. In intact plant foods, by contrast, some flavonoids and other polyphenols are bound to or trapped within the plants' fibrous cell walls. That matrix can then help shuttle these beneficial compounds through the digestive tract for gut bacteria to process.

Another example underscores why the term "natural" can be misleading. Touvier's team found an association between certain natural food colorings and increased disease risk. She suggests that this shouldn't be surprising: "When you isolate one substance—which might be found naturally in fruits and vegetables—and then put it in a reconstituted UPF or a dietary supplement, it may totally change the way this substance will act on your body and microbiota."

And what happens when these isolated substances meet for the first time in a novel food? Neither the European Food Safety Authority nor the US Food and Drug Administration accounts for possible cocktail effects among food additives. "In real life, we do not eat one additive alone; we eat a mixture," Touvier says. In their cohort study of 180,000-plus people, her team linked two mixtures of additives often found in ultra-processed foods and drinks with increased incidence of type 2 diabetes ([25]). One mixture included modified starches, carrageenan, and guar gum; the other included aspartame, sucralose, and acesulfame potassium.

Much remains to be disentangled. And many UPF studies are funded by industry, potentially injecting bias and complicating conclusions. So, what should lawmakers and the public do with the currently incomplete picture? "We don't need to know exact mechanisms," Juul says, "to protect people from aggressive marketing of these foods and to enact policies."

## Precautionary Approach

A recent survey shows that most Americans have heard of UPFs, but only about a third are confident that they understand what the term means (ref. [26]; see "Defining ultraprocessed Foods"). Still, the survey found broad bipartisan support for several public policy and legal strategies to reduce consumption of these foods, such as testing laboratory-made chemicals for safety and requiring warning labels.

Scientists generally agree that diet-related disease needs to be addressed, even if they disagree on the best approach. Klatt questions the feasibility of enforcing appropriate labeling. He also warns that it could create consumer confusion or encourage reformulation without meaningful nutritional improvement.

"There is certainly something critically contributory about modern processed foods," says David Ludwig, an endocrinologist and pediatrician at Harvard Medical School. "But these public health arrows are expensive. If we send them flying off wildly, we spend a lot of money and we lose public credibility." He also notes that UPFs fill an important need. They are often cheaper, more shelf-stable, and easier to find in food deserts. For now, he argues, focusing on the more concrete category of processed carbohydrates—refined grains and added sugars that can produce rapid spikes in glucose and insulin—would address much of the problem.

### Box 1. Defining ultraprocessed foods.

Definitions for ultraprocessed foods (UPFs) remain controversial. A classification called NOVA, developed by Carlos Monteiro, now an emeritus professor at the University of São Paulo, and his team, includes four groups of foods sorted from least to most processed. The most-processed food group, UPFs, covers what Monteiro describes as industrial formulations made from cheap ingredients that are extracted or derived from real food and combined with additives.

Take, for example, corn. On the cob, it would be classified as NOVA 1. Corn oil would be NOVA 2. A tortilla chip made with only oil and salt added would be NOVA 3. And a Doritos-style flavored corn chip would be NOVA 4. In addition to the nature and extent of the processing, the classification also hinges on inferred intent. UPFs are "designed to displace the other NOVA groups and to maximize profit," Monteiro says.

There are other classification schemes. The Novo Nordisk Foundation, for example, is pursuing a parallel effort that considers nutritional quality alongside processing. And in August, the US Department of Health and Human Services and the US Department of Agriculture submitted to the White House the federal government's first proposed definition of UPFs for final review. They did not disclose the text or when it will be released.

David Ludwig, of Harvard Medical School, calls the NOVA concept "hopelessly imprecise." It doesn't distinguish healthy from harmful, he says. A soy-based meat substitute might be considered ultraprocessed, for example, whereas a homemade cake made from sugar, refined flour, and butter wouldn't be.

The NOVA categories may be somewhat fuzzy, concedes Marion Nestle, an emeritus professor at New York University, but the number of truly nutritious foods defined as ultraprocessed is small. She calls NOVA a "brilliant concept," despite its imperfections. "It's the first real advance in diet and chronic disease prevention that we've had in ages," she says.

Why even bother debating definitions? Without an agreed-upon definition, says Kevin Hall, a prominent nutrition researcher, it's easy for the food industry to delay actions that could benefit public health.

Many researchers, however, continue to sound alarms about UPFs. Some, including Hall, want to target which specific characteristics are most to blame. With that information, governments could impose taxes or mandate nutritional markers, as a few countries already do. The food industry, too, could use the science to reformulate healthier ultraprocessed products—perhaps lowering a food's energy density or slowing eating, Forde notes.

None of this rules out other tools. Hall points to GLP-1 agonists as a complementary approach to improving diet quality and tamping down obesity. The two approaches may reinforce each other: GLP-1 drugs appear to blunt cravings for many of the kinds of foods that are often ultraprocessed, possibly by changing how the brain processes reward ([27], [28]). "People start craving more citrus and fresh foods," Juul says. "That's sent the food industry in a bit of a panic."

Indeed, some researchers go as far as to argue that UPFs are addictive, raising further policy implications. "Ultraprocessed foods are engineered in a way to deliver rewarding nutrient signatures that Mother Nature just doesn't hand us," Gearhardt says. Rodent studies have found neurotransmitter release comparable to nicotine responses in the brains of animals that consumed sugar and other highly palatable foods ([29], [30]). Human imaging studies, too, show that combinations of fat and carbohydrate increase activation of the brain's motivation-desire circuitry ([31], [32]). UPFs don't satiate in the way that whole foods do, so people just keep eating, Gearhardt says. "Our brains are just trying to make sure we don't starve."

Already, in 2024, an 18-year-old who developed type 2 diabetes and fatty liver disease in childhood filed a personal injury lawsuit against major manufacturers of UPFs. In 2025, San Francisco's city attorney sued many of the same manufacturers for knowingly making people sick. Still, the notion of "addictive" UPFs remains controversial.

As evidence mounts, researchers—and attorneys—will continue to try to map out cause and effect. But one upshot already seems clear. "Flawed and all, the evidence has a very clear message: Limit intake of ultraprocessed foods," Nestle says. "I can't think of any reason not to say that."
