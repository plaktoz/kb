# How To Teach Adult Learners: My Top 8 Strategies From 20+ Years of Experience

## TL;DR: How to Teach Adults

Adult learners bring life experience, autonomy, and purpose to learning. They also face real obstacles, including limited time, insecurity, and resistance to new methods.

8 strategies that work:

- Keep lessons relevant with real-world examples.
- Use learners’ life experience and match your language to their level.
- Tell stories to improve retention.
- Break content into short sections to prevent overload.
- Give feedback at the moment of need.
- Use visuals with one clear focal point.
- Encourage questions and discussion.
- Stay flexible with structure while keeping control.

Methods to teach with: Case studies, role play, simulations, problem-based learning, peer discussion, microlearning, and flipped or blended formats.

Most important habit: Keep learning yourself. Trainers who stay relevant treat their own development as seriously as their learners do.

You’re never too old to learn something new.

## What Is Adult Teaching?

Adult teaching or training is the act of helping adult learners achieve specific career, personal, or life goals by acquiring new skills and knowledge. The approach builds on adult learning theory, the principles Malcolm Knowles popularized for teaching adults.

It’s an approach to education that recognizes and builds upon the unique characteristics and needs of adult learners.

And it is drastically different from teaching children or young college students.

At its core, adult teaching acknowledges that grown-up students bring rich life experiences and established perspectives to the learning environment.

Unlike children, who are often learning things for the first time, adults approach education with pre-existing knowledge, career experiences, and formed opinions.

The essence of adult teaching lies in its respect for learner autonomy and self-direction. Adults typically choose to learn for specific reasons – whether it’s career advancement, personal interest, or solving practical problems.

This motivation shapes how they engage with learning material. For instance, a professional attending a project management course isn’t just memorizing concepts. They’re actively thinking about how to apply these skills to their current workplace challenges.

**Who are adult learners?** Adult learners are typically defined as people aged 25 and older who return to education or training while managing work, family, and other responsibilities. Unlike traditional students, they bring established professional experience, self-directed motivation, and a strong need for learning to be immediately relevant. The term is also used more broadly to include any learner whose primary motivation is practical skill development rather than academic achievement.

## How Is Teaching Adults Different From Teaching Children?

Teaching adults differs from teaching children in seven main ways. Adults choose to learn, bring years of experience, push back on methods that clash with what has worked for them, and need each lesson to connect to their work or life right now. Here’s how the two groups compare side by side.

## The Main Challenges Of Teaching Adult Learners

Teaching adult learners comes with its own set of challenges. Here are the five that come up most often, and what to do about each one.

## Challenge #1: Resistance To New Methods

When you teach adults, you’ll often face their strong resistance to new methods.

And it’s understandable.

If you’ve done something successfully for years, you’d naturally question someone telling you to do it differently.

We see this resistance regularly in our training sessions where people challenge new approaches, defending their tried-and-true methods.

You’ll hear comments like “I’ve always done it this way” or “My method works fine.”

The key challenge we face isn’t just teaching new skills, it’s helping learners let go of deeply ingrained habits.

But instead of dismissing their experience, we must show them how new methods can make their existing skills even more effective.

**How to handle it:** Start by asking learners how they handle the task today, then show where the new method improves on it. Let them test the new approach on a real piece of their own work, so the proof comes from their results instead of your claims.

## Challenge #2: Motivation And Engagement

Adults need clear, practical reasons for everything they learn, especially in professional development contexts where time is scarce and the cost of irrelevant training is real. They want to see how it helps them today, not someday.

If your training feels too theoretical or disconnected from their daily work, you’ll watch their interest fade fast.

The situation gets even trickier with mandatory training. When adults feel forced to learn something they didn’t choose, they often mentally check out before you even begin.

**How to handle it:** Open each module with the problem it solves and the result learners will get. For mandatory training, connect the content to a task learners already do each week, and keep the required parts short. My guide to increasing engagement covers more tactics for online programs.

## Challenge #3: Insecurity

The lack of self-confidence can be a big hurdle for adult learners. In fact, in my experience, it is one of the biggest challenges of teaching adult learners.

People often worry about looking incompetent in front of their peers, which makes them hold back. They can also feel anxious about diving back into formal learning after years away, wondering if they’ll be able to keep up.

Sitting next to younger learners can add another layer of stress, making them feel like they’re in a silent competition.

That’s why it’s so important to create a space where they feel comfortable and supported.

**How to handle it:** Give learners low-stakes ways to practice before anything counts, such as ungraded practice quizzes and private feedback. Small early wins rebuild confidence faster than encouragement alone.

## Challenge #4: Availability And Time Constraints

Adult learners are juggling work, family, and everything else life throws at them. It’s tough to carve out time for assignments or even attend classes regularly when your schedule is already packed.

After a long day at work, sitting through a lesson can feel almost impossible. Keeping up week after week becomes a real struggle, especially when other responsibilities keep coming up.

As a trainer, you must empathize with adult learners and understand their challenges while structuring your courses.

**How to handle it:** Break content into short lessons learners can finish in 10 to 15 minutes, offer recordings of live sessions, and set clear deadlines with some flexibility built in.

## Challenge #5: Personality Clashes And Ego

When you put a group of adults together, everyone brings their own opinions, experiences, and personalities to the table. Sometimes that creates great discussions, but other times it leads to friction.

You might have someone who dominates the conversation or resists feedback because they think they already know it all. On the flip side, quieter learners might shut down if they feel overshadowed.

Managing these dynamics takes patience and a firm hand to ensure everyone feels heard and respected. It’s about balancing the room so learning stays on track.

**How to handle it:** Set ground rules for discussion at the start. Use time limits, call on quieter learners directly, and give dominant voices a role, such as summarizing the group’s points.

## How To Teach Adult Learners: Best Practices And Proven Strategies

Teaching adult learners has many potential advantages over teaching younger students.

They have much more life experience, which you can use as part of the knowledge that is shared among the participants in your class.

If your aim is to make money teaching online, adults obviously tend to have much more direct control over the decision to spend money on an online learning experience.

However, while you may not need to put the same effort into engaging your class that a school teacher would, you still need to build rapport with your learners and develop a teaching strategy that maximizes their learning outcomes.

Understanding how adults parse and retain new knowledge is critical to creating a learning experience that achieves its goals.

Let’s discuss the key adult learning strategies and best practices that change outcomes.

## 1. Keep Your Lessons Relevant

The most significant way adult learners digest information is through real-world filters.

As already noted, adults have far more life experience than children, and that comes with a stronger need to understand the hows and whys of your material.

By making connections with their own experiences, they confirm for themselves the truth of your teaching, and your lessons will stick more strongly in their minds if you can make those connections for them.

Including real-world applications or outcomes of your lessons when teaching adult learners is important so your students can understand and visualize how to apply what you teach.

Use examples of times when people should follow your teaching in their everyday lives, or of the pitfalls that can befall them if they fail to do so.

By putting your lessons in context, they become easier for adult learners to understand and retain.

**In an online course:** Open each module with a short video showing a real situation the lesson solves, and end it with a task learners apply to their own work.

## 2. Focus on Your Learners’ Life Experience

If you’re teaching a coding bootcamp for beginners, for example, don’t overwhelm them with jargon.

Equally, if you’re teaching business leaders a new recruiting strategy, don’t talk down to them.

In general, when teaching adult learners, use familiar terms and address them at an appropriate level for their experience, background, and age.

Sometimes using acronyms and buzzwords is the most appropriate way of teaching. It can quickly convey your experience and authority within your field, and reassure learners that you know what you’re talking about.

However, with a novice audience, too many new terms will only be confusing. You want your learners to be able to follow what you’re saying without having to focus on deciphering the meaning of your words.

The average age of your learners is also an important consideration when you choose how to approach teaching your course. Bear in mind that older learners might not be up to date with the latest online trends and fads, while young adult learners will be put off by edupreneurs who try to emulate their style of talking.

Most learners respond best when educators use established frames of reference and limit their use of slang and jargon.

**In an online course:** Add a short intake survey to learn each person’s experience level, then offer a beginner track and an advanced track for key modules.

## 3. Tell Stories as You’re Teaching

Stories have been used as mnemonics for centuries. From recalling the colors of the rainbow to the order of notes in music, children are taught a significant amount of information by converting it into stories.

As adults, stories remain powerful memory devices. Adult learners are often more emotionally driven, and storytelling can tap into their emotions and help them retain your lessons.

Tie storytelling into your real-world examples by talking about a time your lesson helped someone, or could have helped them. The same point runs through Harold Stolovitch’s book Telling Ain’t Training.

If you use slides or charts to illustrate your lessons, choose powerful images, colors, and even fonts that are evocative of particular emotions.

Think about the educators who have most influenced you in the past, and the lessons that stand out most sharply.

What methods did your teachers use to gain and keep your interest? What technique made one lesson stand out above the others?

Chances are good that stories were a part of their effectiveness, and you may even be able to borrow from some of those stories.

**In an online course:** Record a two-minute story for each module about a learner or client who used the idea, or who ignored it and paid the price.

## 4. Break Up Information to Avoid Cognitive Overload

Adult learners can follow more, and more complex, information than children, but you should still aim to structure your course into compartmentalized lessons that progress logically as you teach.

By breaking down your course, you can ensure that all learners follow your teaching, and build in time to recap each section and ensure everyone is keeping up.

Use notes or slides to highlight the key points of each lesson, and provide learners with these materials either before or after each session so they can independently review what they learned.

Make a list or bullet points of each step of your session, and identify where individual sections begin and end. This style of teaching will help keep you on track, as well as improve your learners’ outcomes.

If you have to pause for any reason or find the class discussion wandered away from your original subject, it’s easy to return to your lesson plan and continue from where you left off.

**In an online course:** Keep video lessons to 5 to 10 minutes, and follow each one with a three-question recap quiz.

## 5. Provide Feedback in the Moment of Need

When creating your online course, masterclass, seminar, or other offering, it’s a good idea to structure your teaching so you have space for a recap and questions at the end of each section.

This ensures that your learners can keep up with your material and prevents anyone from being left behind.

This is especially important if your session is particularly long, or covers a wide range of material.

While interruptions to your teaching can be disruptive and prevent students from learning, it’s important to correct mistakes or wrong assumptions the moment you become aware of them.

This prevents the incorrect idea from taking hold and confusing learners. It is almost always better to interrupt your session at the “moment of need” (the point at which the mistake has been made) in order to set your learners on the right path, rather than circle back to it at a later stage.

**In an online course:** Use auto-graded quizzes that explain each answer, so learners get feedback the moment they make a mistake.

## 6. Make Your Material Visually Stimulating

It isn’t only children who find color and images appealing. If your handouts and slides are too plain they become boring, and your learners’ minds will switch off.

Instead, attract learners’ attention with the judicious use of color, fonts, and pictures to highlight important information and boost the memorability of your lessons.

Be mindful of going too far with this tip. Too much color, or too many images, also detracts from your lesson. If your slides and handouts are visually overwhelming, that can also turn learners off.

Instead, aim to have one focal point on each page. It could be highlighting key words or phrases in a different color or font, including an amusing doodle that demonstrates the point of your lesson, or switching out boring bullet points for a subject-relevant icon.

Remember that whatever element you choose to stand out will be the thing your learners remember most clearly, so make it count.

**In an online course:** Design slides with one idea each, and add captions to every video. My guide to PowerPoint for e-learning covers the basics, and AI presentation makers can speed up the design work.

### A note on learning styles

Many guides tell you to match your teaching to each learner’s visual, auditory, or kinesthetic style. The research doesn’t back that up. A major 2008 review by Harold Pashler and colleagues found no solid evidence that matching instruction to a preferred style improves learning. Use visuals because they help every learner understand and remember.

## 7. Encourage Questions and Discussion

Adult learners typically need to understand new material in the context of their own life experiences, and one of the best ways to make those connections is by talking.

Allowing your learners time to ask you questions, and to discuss your lessons among each other, is a great way of helping cement your teaching in their minds.

It’s also a good time for you to spot any mistakes or incorrect assumptions your learners are making, and by opening the floor to general questions you might find learners are approaching your material in ways you hadn’t envisioned.

This provides invaluable feedback and insight into your teaching, and gives you the opportunity to make adjustments in real time.

When choosing a host for your online educational offerings, look for the option to include a live chat alongside your video so learners can make notes, talk, and ask questions.

Some learners will always be reluctant to speak up in a group setting, so allowing them to send you messages or ask questions in writing will encourage more participation and generate better results than asking learners to talk before the whole group.

**In an online course:** Give learners a community space for questions, and keep a written Q&A option open during live online classes for those who won’t speak up.

## 8. Be Flexible With Your Course Structure

Unlike kids, adult learners don’t have to be kept to a rigid schedule to keep learning. Structuring your courses is a great idea, both to keep you on track and to help frame your lessons in a logical, progressive way.

However, you can afford to allow short breaks and discussions without your learners losing track of what you were teaching.

Trust in your learners’ enthusiasm for your subject, and their passion for learning from you. Many school teachers are addressing a captive audience who would rather be elsewhere, and turning your back for five minutes can quickly result in the conversation moving away from the class material and toward something more interesting.

When you host a masterclass for adult learners, you and your lessons are the most interesting thing. This means you can relax a little and let your session flow more naturally.

Broadening the scope of discussion during a learning experience is also a great way of understanding how your learners are interacting with your teaching, and what information they’re really taking away from your class.

Listen with an open mind and you can learn a lot about becoming a better educator. If you immediately close down any discussion you think isn’t relevant, you’ll never understand where the original connection to your material came from.

Ultimately, you control and lead your class, so don’t be afraid to make the call to get back on schedule once you think the general discussion has gone on long enough.

But by opening the floor to your adult learners, they can teach you as much about being an edupreneur as you can teach them about your subject.

**In an online course:** Leave open time at the end of live calls, and let learners in cohort-based courses suggest topics for the next session.

## Teaching Adults Online: What Changes

Most of the strategies above apply to live classrooms and online courses alike. Online teaching changes a few things, and course creators need to plan for them.

**Self-paced learners drop off quietly:** Nobody notices an empty seat in a self-paced course. Track progress and send reminders when learners stall. My guide to online course completion rates covers the tactics.

**Community replaces the classroom:** Peer discussion has to live somewhere. Build it into the course with a community space or regular live calls.

**Live and self-paced need different designs:** Live sessions work best for discussion and practice. Recorded lessons work best for explanations and demos.

**AI helps with practice more than thinking:** AI can give instant feedback on practice tasks and generate new scenarios. Learners who let AI do the thinking for them learn less.

A 2026 randomized trial at the University of Maryland found that only about 15% of students offered a course AI tutor used it, and students with access earned lower final grades. My overview of generative AI use cases covers where AI fits in course design.

## The Number One Best Practice For Teaching Adult Learners

Be open to learning yourself.

Teaching and training adult learners is significantly different from teaching young kids or teenagers. Adult learners with lifelong experiences challenge you as a trainer.

They value their time and can easily spot filler content if your lessons aren’t up to the mark.

In my experience, the most critical part of teaching adult learners is to make it an interactive experience where you help them shape their experiences to understand new realities.

Exchange ideas, encourage discussion, and create an environment where everyone can speak their mind, share their doubts, and ask questions without the fear of being judged or ridiculed.

## Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

**1. What are the best methods for teaching adults?**

The most effective methods for teaching adults are case studies, role play, simulations, problem-based learning, peer discussion, demonstration with practice, and project work. Each one puts learners to work on realistic problems. Microlearning, flipped learning, and blended formats help busy adults fit learning around their jobs.

**2. What is the best way to assess learning progress in adult learners?**

Use formative assessments like quizzes, reflection prompts, peer feedback, or project-based evaluations. These allow adults to apply knowledge and get feedback without the pressure of traditional tests. The goal is to surface misunderstandings early and give learners a sense of real progress, not just measure recall.

**3. How can I adapt my teaching for neurodiverse adult learners?**

Offer multiple formats, such as video, audio, and text, and allow flexible pacing so learners can move at a speed that works for them. Use clear, consistent layouts and avoid overloading slides with text. Where possible, check in individually rather than relying solely on group feedback, as neurodiverse learners may not flag difficulties in a public setting.

**4. Should I use gamification when teaching adults?**

Yes, if used strategically. Adults respond well to progress tracking, skill-based badges, and challenge frameworks, particularly when these reflect real professional growth rather than arbitrary points. The key distinction is purpose: gamification that mirrors mastery works, while gamification that feels like a children’s game will cause disengagement. Tie every mechanic to an outcome the learner already cares about.

**5. How do I handle tech issues when teaching adults online?**

Start every course with a tech orientation so learners know what tools they will need and how to use them. Provide backup formats like downloadable PDFs for key content, and offer simple troubleshooting guides. Having a support channel, even just an email address or chat option, reduces the anxiety that tech problems create for adult learners, especially those returning to formal learning after a long gap.

**6. What role does emotional intelligence play in teaching adults?**

Emotional intelligence is one of the most underrated skills in adult training. Adults come into learning environments carrying job stress, self-doubt, and sometimes resistance to being taught. A trainer who can read the room, noticing when someone is disengaged, when group dynamics are creating friction, or when a learner needs encouragement rather than correction, will get far better outcomes than one who focuses only on content delivery. Trust is the prerequisite for learning in adult settings.

**7. How can I support adult learners who are returning to education after many years?**

Reassure them early that their experience is an asset, not a liability. Set clear expectations so they know exactly what is required and when. Build in small wins early in the program to rebuild learning confidence. Adults returning after a long gap often feel like they are behind before they have even started. Your job is to show them quickly that they are not.

**8. What is the ideal class size for adult learning environments?**

Smaller is generally better, ideally under 20 students for live or synchronous settings where discussion and interaction matter. For asynchronous online courses, class size is less of a constraint, but you should ensure there are support structures like discussion boards, feedback loops, or community forums to replace the interaction that smaller groups naturally provide.

**9. How important is certification for adult learners?**

Very important, particularly for career-driven learners. A certificate of completion, digital badge, or CEU credits signals to employers that the learning happened and was validated. It also gives adults a tangible outcome to point to, which reinforces their motivation to finish. For edupreneurs, offering recognized credentials, even simple certificates, can directly increase enrollment and course completion rates.

**10. How should I deal with adult learners who dominate group discussions?**

Set ground rules at the start of the program so expectations around participation are clear for everyone. During discussions, use time limits, directed questions to quieter learners, or rotating roles in group activities to redistribute airtime. The goal is not to silence dominant voices but to ensure every learner feels the space is theirs too, because when quieter adults feel overshadowed, they disengage silently and you often won’t notice until it’s too late.

**11. How do I encourage lifelong learning beyond the course?**

End every program with a concrete action plan. Go beyond a summary of what was covered and list specific next steps the learner can take in the next 30 days. Share curated resources, communities, or follow-up courses that extend the learning naturally. Encourage learners to set personal growth goals and track their own progress. The courses that create lifelong learners are the ones that make learning feel like a habit worth keeping, not a task that’s finished.
