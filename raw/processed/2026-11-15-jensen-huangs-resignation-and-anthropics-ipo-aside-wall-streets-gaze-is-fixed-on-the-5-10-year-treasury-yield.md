# Jensen Huang's Resignation and Anthropic's IPO Aside, Wall Street's Gaze Is Fixed on the 5% 10-Year Treasury Yield

Even with Nvidia's biggest management shakeup and the initial public offering (IPO) of AI company Anthropic falling in the same week, the key variable Wall Street is watching ultimately comes down to U.S. Treasury yields, according to one analysis. With the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield climbing back above 5%, concerns are mounting that it could offset AI-related tailwinds.

According to the financial investment industry on the 15th, analyst Lee Jae-man, in a stock market outlook report titled "Many Events, But Ultimately Rates," diagnosed that while Nvidia's earnings announcement scheduled for the 17th (local time) and Anthropic's listing set for the 19th are drawing market attention, the variable that will determine direction is the U.S. Treasury yield.

The most immediate shock came from Nvidia. News of CEO Jensen Huang's resignation sent Nvidia shares down more than 13% this week, extending a six-week losing streak and marking the longest slump since October 2012. With the head of the company that symbolized the AI boom stepping down, questions about the sustainability of the AI industry as a whole weighed on investor sentiment.

The analyst viewed the CEO change as not a variable that would immediately shake earnings. He estimated Nvidia's revenue for the current quarter would rise 13% from the prior quarter, with data center revenue up 14% and net profit up 12%. He projected the operating margin would hold at the same 66% level as the prior quarter. However, there is a separate reason he does not view the management change favorably: if Nvidia reduces its equity investments in AI-related companies, its symbolic status as the bellwether of the AI ecosystem could be undermined.

Valuation pressure, by contrast, has eased, according to the assessment. Nvidia's 12-month forward price-to-earnings ratio (P/E) stands at 11x, down sharply from this year's high of 22x. Having fallen to 2012-2013 levels, the analysis suggests there is room for the P/E to rebound if earnings exceed market expectations.

Anthropic's listing is also seen as unlikely to trigger a major supply-demand shock. The IPO volume this year, including Anthropic, is estimated at 0.3% of S&P 500 market capitalization. That is low compared with 0.8% during the 2000 tech boom, 0.9% during the 2007 BRICs investment boom, and 0.8% during the 2021 special purpose acquisition company (SPAC) listing boom. The explanation is that this is not a stage at which to worry about oversupply from large-scale IPOs.

The problem is rates. As concerns over a Federal Reserve (Fed) benchmark rate hike come to the fore, the U.S. 10-year Treasury yield has climbed back above 5%. The point is that rising market rates could offset the positive effects of individual events such as Nvidia's earnings or AI-related IPOs. The S&P 500's current 12-month forward P/E stands at around 18x, and the earnings yield on stocks calculated on that basis is about 5.5%. If the 10-year yield rises further above 5%, the relative appeal of stocks versus bonds could diminish.

The type of stock that benefits also depends on the nature of the rate rise. The analysis suggests growth stocks are relatively favored in a flattening phase driven by a rise in 2-year yields, while value stocks are relatively favored in a steepening phase driven by a rise in 10-year yields.

Market participants are advising that investors keep in mind the possibility of further increases in both short- and long-term yields ahead of the Fed's rate decision on the 9th of next month. To judge whether upward momentum is strengthening, they note, one should examine whether next year's earnings estimates are being revised upward and whether foreign investors are switching to net buying. There is also a recommendation that it is effective to increase the weighting of companies whose P/E is expected to fall on the back of high earnings growth next year, or companies whose earnings growth rate will be higher next year than this year.

The macro environment is also not favorable. Although the rise in the October consumer price index (CPI) slowed, it exceeded Wall Street expectations, and the producer price index (PPI) posted its largest annual increase in about four years, driven by a surge in energy costs. With upward pressure on food prices persisting, the 10-year yield jumped from 4.85% a week earlier to 5.15%. Bob Schwartz, senior economist at Oxford Economics, projected that core personal consumption expenditures (PCE) inflation would accelerate to 3.8% year over year.

Comments from Fed officials are also tilting hawkish. Mary Daly, president of the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, said a policy rate hike is necessary, while Susan Collins, president of the Boston Fed, remarked that tightening is imminent due to high inflation. Interest rate futures markets are pricing in a near-90% probability of a December hike, and the possibility of additional hikes in the first quarter of next year is also being priced in. Deutsche Bank projected three 25bp hikes—in December, January, and March of next year—that would bring the federal funds rate to 4.5-4.75% and raise the neutral rate to 3.75-4%.

Last week, U.S. stocks ended their correction phase and rebounded. The Nasdaq Composite and S&P 500 ended four-week losing streaks, and all 11 S&P 500 sectors rose. Energy gained the most at 5.1%, followed by industrials (3.7%) and communication services (3.5%). Consumer discretionary, financials, and health care also rose more than 2%, while materials gained just 0.3%.

Institutional views are mixed. Anthony Saglimbene, chief market strategist at Ameriprise Financial, said "the AI trade will experience volatility, and through year-end, bulls and bears will spar over the direction of this story." Keith Lerner, chief investment officer at Truist Wealth, noted that the forward P/E of the S&P 500 technology sector at 17x fell below that of the index overall (18x) for the first time since 2017, assessing that bad news has been largely priced in. Morgan Chase Asset Management recommended a "barbell" strategy of tech stocks and cyclical stocks, citing low recession risk and the ongoing AI capital expenditure cycle. BlackRock, by contrast, tactically lowered its U.S. equity overweight to neutral and advised holding AI bottleneck areas such as chips, data centers, and power infrastructure alongside short-term Treasuries.

In the end, the market's focal point this week comes down to how well the two AI events—Nvidia's earnings and Anthropic's listing—can withstand the headwind of 5%-level Treasury yields. Volatility is expected to remain elevated until the Fed's October meeting minutes and the rate decision path are confirmed.
