# Atrial Fibrillation: Eye Scan May Predict Risk 4 Years Sooner

Approximately 52.55 million people worldwide live with atrial fibrillation (AFib), a cardiovascular condition where the heart's upper and lower chambers beat out of sync, producing a fluttering or racing heartbeat. AFib raises the risk of stroke, heart failure, heart attack, and dementia — yet roughly 62% of patients are unaware they have it before diagnosis, largely because symptoms are subtle or shared with other conditions.

A new study published in *PLOS Digital Health* suggests that specific changes in the eye's retina may signal AFib risk up to **4 years before clinical diagnosis**.

## Study Design

Researchers examined optical coherence tomography (OCT) scans and color retinal photography from more than 90,000 participants drawn from two large cohort studies: AlzEye and the U.K. Biobank.

Lead author Josef Huemer, MD, consultant ophthalmologist at Moorfields Eye Hospital, noted that "the retina is the only place in the body where blood vessels and nerve fiber tissue can be analyzed noninvasively" using widely available eye tests.

## Key Findings

People with AFib consistently showed thinning of the **macular ganglion cells and inner plexiform layer (mGCIPL)** — a combined retinal structure involved in relaying visual information to the brain.

Huemer explained that this thinning "is most likely caused by mini strokes in the brain, a common finding in patients with AFib," and that the result appeared in both a hospital-based cohort and a healthy volunteer cohort, strengthening confidence in the findings.

Researchers also observed thinning of the **inner nuclear layer**, which Huemer hypothesized is linked to cardiovascular disease and vulnerability to micro-strokes of the eye.

Among participants who did not have AFib at the time their retinal images were taken, those who later developed the condition already showed thinner mGCIPL layers — on average **4 years before presenting to hospital** with AFib.

## Mechanism: How the Eye Reflects Heart Risk

Huemer noted that the retina's high energy demands make its vasculature especially susceptible to cardiovascular damage, drawing a parallel to diabetic retinopathy. He added that the link between cardiovascular problems and subtle retinal changes was "first mentioned in the late 1800s," and that modern imaging and AI-assisted analysis now allow more robust evidence to be established.

Ophthalmologist Benjamin Bert, MD (MemorialCare Orange Coast Medical Center), not involved in the study, offered two possible explanations: "silent ischemia" — small episodes of poor blood perfusion causing tissue damage — or mild brain injury leading to cellular atrophy in the retina.

## Clinical Implications

Interventional cardiologist Cheng-Han Chen, MD (MemorialCare Saddleback Medical Center), also not involved, observed that routine eye imaging capable of identifying elevated AFib risk "could potentially broaden our ability to recognize risk earlier" and flag patients for closer cardiovascular monitoring.

Chen cautioned that "the underlying mechanism linking AFib with changes in the cells or blood vessels of the eye is not yet well understood," and that future studies must determine how reliably these changes predict AFib across diverse populations before eye imaging could become a practical screening tool.

## Next Steps

Huemer's team is expanding OCT image analysis in large-scale cardiovascular datasets and working toward regulatory approval of oculomics algorithms as a medical device, intended for use in local optometry settings to support earlier detection of systemic disease.
