# Corporate-Speak: Three Days On, Four Days Off, Dream On

Three days on, four days off, dream on.

Just as we were beginning to think the four-day working week might have had its day, along comes an even more ambitious thought bubble.

Apparently, artificial intelligence (AI) could soon have us working just three days a week.

But if working four days a week sounded too good to be true, its three-day cousin truly takes wishful thinking to a whole new level.

Mind you, that has not stopped some of the biggest names in business and technology from laying it on thick and trying to convince us that a four-day weekend is a real possibility.

Their thinking is that AI will make us more productive than an accountant at tax time. That would mean five days of work could eventually be squeezed into three.

Amazon founder Jeff Bezos has suggested AI could eventually allow some people to earn a living working in just three days a week.

Zoom boss Eric Yuan has gone further, claiming that a three-day working week could become a reality within five years.

JPMorgan Chase chief Jamie Dimon claims that future generations might realistically expect a three-and-a-half-day working week.

And if that sounds ambitious, Microsoft co-founder Bill Gates has taken things even further, raising the possibility that AI could leave us working just two or three days a week.

With predictions like that floating around, it would be completely understandable if we started to believe that the five-day working week was living on borrowed time.

But here's some rock-solid advice. Avoid rushing to make plans for your four-day weekend anytime soon.

Let's face it, there's absolutely no shortage of people pining for a three-day working week. No matter how much they love their jobs, most people would jump at the chance.

Yet there's a stack of reasons why the four-day weekend is likely to remain a workplace fantasy for a very long time to come.

Perhaps the biggest stumbling block of all is the small matter of what happens to all that extra productivity.

Just because AI allows employees to get more done in less time does not mean employers will pass on that efficiency by rewarding staff with extra days away from work.

That's where history comes in handy.

You might recall that computers, email and smartphones were all supposed to make our working lives easier – much easier.

For sure, most would agree that those advances in technology made plenty of tasks easier.

But, at the same time, we also ended up more connected, more contactable and, in many cases, working longer hours.

The question is: Is there any reason to believe AI will be different?

In fact, businesses spending millions of dollars on AI are likely to expect a return on their investment through higher profits, increased output and lower costs.

If that's not enough to cast doubt on the three-day working week, consider the mathematics of making it happen.

Moving from a traditional five-day working week to three days means cutting working hours by 40%. That's the easy bit.

But to get five days of work done in three, employees would need to become roughly 67% more productive for every hour they work.

Even with AI doing some of the heavy-lifting, that's a massive productivity increase to expect.

Despite years of discussion, trials and enthusiastic predictions, the five-day working week remains firmly entrenched across much of the economy.

A small number of organizations have made four days work. Many others have found the practicalities considerably more difficult than the promises suggested.

For now, the three-day working week looks less like the future of work and more like another case of corporate hot air.

The technology might eventually make it possible.

Convincing the boss to give us four days off every week is another matter altogether.
