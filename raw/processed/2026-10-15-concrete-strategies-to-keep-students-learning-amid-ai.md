# Concrete strategies to keep students learning amid AI

## Thinking beyond the final paper

If research assignments are meant to help students learn how to find, evaluate, and use information to build their own understanding, then we need to design assignments with those objectives in mind. That means thinking beyond the final paper and asking what students should be able to do for themselves by the time they finish.

AI has made it easier than ever to produce something that looks like research. But finding an answer and knowing why that answer is credible are two different things. Students still need opportunities to read closely, weigh evidence, recognize different perspectives, develop arguments, and explain how they came to know what they know to be true.

The answer isn’t necessarily to abandon research papers or to ban AI from every stage of the process. Instead, we can design assignments that make the learning visible, build students’ research skills, and give them meaningful reasons to do the work themselves.

Here are four ideas to consider, along with concrete strategies instructors can adapt to their own courses.

## Gain buy-in: Help students understand why research matters

Students are more likely to invest in research when they understand what they’re gaining from it. Rather than presenting a research paper as a series of requirements to complete, explain how each stage contributes to their ability to think independently and make informed decisions.

Make critical thinking concrete. Ask students to consider how they decide whether to trust information in their everyday lives and how those decisions compare with the standards used in research. Teach them that critical thinking extends far beyond the classroom, showing up in how they approach their first jobs, navigate relationships, and make informed decisions throughout their lives.

Connect assignments to questions that matter. When possible, let students investigate questions related to their interests or fields of study. Help them move from a broad topic to a question that requires investigation rather than a quick answer.

Explain the purpose of each requirement. Don’t assume students understand why they need scholarly sources or multiple drafts. Explain what each requirement helps them practice and how it contributes to the quality of their research.

Discuss the limits of AI-generated answers. Ask students to consider what might be missing when a tool presents a confident summary of a complicated issue.

## Build skills: Teach students how to research

We often assign research projects without explicitly teaching all the skills students need to complete them. Students may know how to find information online but struggle to read scholarly articles, evaluate the credibility of a source, or synthesize multiple arguments.

Incorporate research instruction into class sessions. Integrate the teaching of course content with the practice of reading, evaluating, and discussing sources. Use materials relevant to your course so students can develop research skills, all while saving valuable class time and streamlining your efforts.

Collaborate with a subject librarian. Ask your librarian to help students identify appropriate databases, develop search strategies, evaluate sources, and navigate unfamiliar research tools.

Require students to engage directly with their sources. Have students submit PDFs of selected sources with annotations identifying important passages, explaining why the evidence matters, and connecting ideas to their developing arguments. Require page numbers for citations.

Teach students how to synthesize information. Ask students to organize their sources in a synthesis matrix. Have them compare authors’ central claims, supporting evidence, methods and conclusions.

## Focus on process and growth

Research is an ongoing process of asking questions, exploring ideas, and refining conclusions. Students may need to revise their questions, reconsider their assumptions, or change their arguments as they encounter new sources. Assignments should accommodate that development instead of focusing only on the finished paper.

Divide the assignment into checkpoints. Require students to submit a research question, a preliminary search, an annotated bibliography, a working argument, an outline, and/or a draft before completing the final project. Set deadlines that give students enough time to use feedback at each stage.

Build revision into the grading structure. Allow students to revise early submissions without penalty, or grade preliminary work primarily for completion and thoughtful engagement. Make the final evaluation reflect the quality of the research and the extent to which students have used feedback throughout the process. Include rubric criteria for refining a research question, improving source selection, strengthening the relationship between claims and evidence, and responding to criticism.

Use low-stakes practice before major deadlines. Have students write short source analyses, develop claims, or compare two interpretations before these skills are assessed in a substantial assignment. Provide feedback early enough for students to learn from mistakes rather than discover them after submitting the final paper.

Build discussion into the research process. Schedule peer workshops or short instructor conferences at key stages. Ask students to explain their research questions, describe the evidence they have found, and identify where they remain uncertain. Use these conversations to help students clarify their reasoning before they commit to a final argument.

Allow flexibility in the final format when appropriate. Depending on the learning objectives, students might communicate their findings through a traditional paper, presentation, poster, digital exhibit or another format.

## Expect accountability: Make students’ learning visible

Providing students with support and opportunities to improve is only part of the process. Instructors also need ways to assess whether students have developed the skills the assignment is intended to teach.

Identify which tasks students must complete independently. Specify the parts of the assignment that require students’ own reading, analysis, and reasoning. For example, students might independently develop an initial interpretation of a source or outline the evidence for an argument before using AI to explore additional perspectives.

Include in-class reading and writing. Set aside class time for students to annotate a passage, evaluate a source, draft a claim, or explain how evidence supports an interpretation. These activities reinforce essential skills and help instructors identify misunderstandings while there is still time to address them.

Assess research knowledge beyond the paper. Include questions on a midterm or final exam that require students to draw on their research. Ask them to compare the conclusions of two sources, explain a counterargument, or describe how their interpretation changed after they conducted research.

Incorporate firsthand investigation when it fits the assignment. Consider whether students can gather or examine evidence through interviews, observations, site visits, experiments, or primary-source materials.

Require a final reflection. Ask students to identify a significant research decision, explain how they evaluated the evidence behind it, and describe how their thinking developed. If they used AI, have them discuss a specific instance in which they verified, revised, rejected or built upon its output. Evaluate the quality of their reflection rather than asking for a generic statement about ethical AI use.

Use the right platforms for your purposes. Platforms such as Google Docs and Box enable the preservation of version history to check the evolution of the paper throughout its stages.

## Start with one change – and a cohort

Redesigning a research assignment doesn’t have to mean starting over. Begin by identifying what you most want students to learn, whether it’s the ability to evaluate evidence, read scholarly sources closely, synthesize different perspectives, construct an original argument, or understand how knowledge is created in your field.

The goal isn’t to make research more difficult just because AI makes it easier to complete certain tasks. It’s to make sure that the essential work remains part of the assignment, and that students receive the guidance and practice they need to do it well.
