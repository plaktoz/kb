# Can an AI-guided diet beat healthy eating when taking metformin?

Can an AI-guided diet deliver better blood sugar control than conventional healthy eating? A randomized trial puts personalized nutrition to the test in adults starting metformin, with results that raise questions about the added value of algorithm-based dietary advice.

Study: Metabolic and gut microbiome responses to personalized nutrition during metformin therapy in prediabetes and early‑stage type 2 diabetes: a randomized clinical trial. Image Credit: Butusova Elena / Shutterstock

In a recent study published in the journal Communications Medicine, researchers conducted a randomized controlled trial (RCT) to evaluate whether a machine-learning-guided personalized diet improves metabolic outcomes compared with a standard healthy diet during metformin initiation. The study specifically analyzed data from 138 adults with prediabetes or early-stage type 2 diabetes (T2D) over six months of follow-up.

The research was led by scientists from the Garvan Institute of Medical Research and Weizmann Institute of Science, in collaboration with UNSW Sydney, the Victor Chang Cardiac Research Institute, St Vincent's Hospital, the University of Sydney, Western Sydney University, the German Cancer Research Center and Mohamed bin Zayed University of Artificial Intelligence.

The study found that both dietary regimens produced comparable reductions in glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), body weight, visceral fat, and blood pressure, with no statistically significant difference in glycemic outcomes between the two approaches.

The personalized diet produced a slightly greater reduction in short-term glycemic variability (GV), but the clinical significance of this difference remains uncertain. Both interventions led to declines in gut microbial richness, while changes in microbial diversity and short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) differed between groups, though the study did not find significant differences between the diets.

Together, these findings suggest that conventional low-fat, high-fiber dietary guidance can deliver similar metabolic improvements to algorithm-guided nutrition when paired with metformin, though larger studies are needed to confirm these results.

## Background

Type 2 diabetes (T2D) and prediabetes are highly prevalent metabolic disorders associated with increased risks of macrovascular and microvascular complications, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD), and premature mortality.

Decades of research have established behavioral modifications (specifically sleep, diet, and physical activity) as the primary lifestyle intervention for prediabetes, whereas metformin currently serves as a widely used first-line oral glucose-lowering medication for T2D and may be prescribed to selected individuals at high risk of progressing from prediabetes.

Metformin is one of the most widely prescribed drugs globally, yet some patients do not achieve recommended glycemic targets with the medication alone.

For example, metformin monotherapy yielded less than a 0.1% HbA1c reduction after six months in the landmark Diabetes Prevention Program (DPP), where participants received limited dietary support. Previous clinical trials have also rarely examined how concurrently initiating metformin may interact with algorithm-guided nutrition.

Earlier research using the same algorithm reported greater improvements in blood glucose control than a Mediterranean-style diet, but the effects of combining personalized nutrition with newly initiated metformin remained unclear.

Metformin and dietary changes can also alter the gut microbiome, which plays a role in glucose regulation. Researchers are still investigating how these microbial changes relate to improvements in metabolic health.

## About the study

The present study aimed to address this knowledge gap and inform future personalized medicine and diabetes-associated public health recommendations by recruiting 151 adults (aged 20–70 years; HbA1c ≤ 8.0%) at the Garvan Institute of Medical Research in Sydney, Australia (the PREDICT trial; NCT03558867).

Of the 151 participants randomized, 138 began treatment. The average age was 55 years; nearly two-thirds were women; three-quarters had prediabetes; and most were overweight or obese. These participants comprised the intention-to-treat (ITT) cohort.

Before treatment, the researchers assessed participants' glucose levels, body composition, liver fat, and gut microbiome. Participants were then assigned to one of two dietary interventions alongside extended-release metformin (1.5 g/day).

The two groups followed: 1. A personalized postprandial glucose-targeting (PPT; n = 66) diet using a machine-learning algorithm that combined clinical, lifestyle, glucose-monitoring, and gut microbiome data to predict responses to meals selected from more than 5,700 foods, and 2. A low-fat, high-fiber standard-of-care (SOC; n = 72) diet based on Australian guidelines. Participants with a body mass index (BMI) > 25 kg/m² were prescribed a 75% daily energy target (100% otherwise), and both groups received structured dietary support.

The researchers assessed six-month changes in HbA1c (the primary outcome), glucose variability, body composition, and gut microbial characteristics. Microbiome findings were also compared with data from approximately 10,000 adults without diagnosed T2D in the Human Phenotype Project.

The researchers originally planned to recruit 264 participants, but the COVID-19 pandemic curtailed recruitment, potentially reducing the study's ability to detect differences between the dietary interventions.

## Study findings

The study’s summary statistics revealed high median metformin adherence (97.5%) and 91,100 logged meals across the six-month intervention. Primary analyses showed similar, significant HbA1c reductions in both the PPT (-0.26%) and SOC (-0.29%) arms, with no meaningful difference between the diets. Follow-up measurements at 12 months also showed no significant difference, though fewer participants provided data at this stage.

Both groups demonstrated comparable reductions in body weight (-4.6 kg vs. -4.4 kg), visceral fat, and systolic blood pressure. Liver steatosis also decreased in the PPT group, but the researchers found no significant difference between the dietary approaches.

The PPT group experienced a slightly greater reduction in glucose variability, but the investigators considered the effect unlikely to be clinically meaningful. Carbohydrate intake also decreased in the PPT group while increasing in SOC, making it difficult to determine whether the improvement reflected personalized dietary recommendations or lower carbohydrate consumption. Metformin safety assessment identified mild-to-moderate gastrointestinal intolerance as the most frequent adverse event (11 PPT vs. 18 SOC participants).

An exploratory analysis of stool samples showed that species richness declined in both groups, Shannon diversity dropped significantly in PPT, and fecal acetate and butyrate fell significantly in SOC, along with reductions in several butyrate-producing bacterial species. These changes were observed within the groups, but differences between the diets were not statistically significant.

Exploratory analyses identified increases in several members of the bacterial phylum Bacteroidota. Changes in Phocaeicola vulgatus and Agathobaculum butyriciproducens were associated with better glycemic outcomes, but these correlations did not establish causality. Akkermansia muciniphila increased in the personalized-diet group, while changes in microbial richness, diversity, and SCFAs were not significantly associated with improvements in the measured metabolic outcomes.

## Conclusions

This trial found that algorithm-guided postprandial nutrition offered no significant additional improvements in blood glucose control or cardiometabolic health compared with standard dietary guidance when both were initiated alongside metformin. The findings also show that improved glycemia can occur alongside declines in microbial richness, diversity, and certain SCFAs, indicating that these gut microbiome measures do not necessarily improve alongside metabolic outcomes.

These findings tentatively support accessible, guideline-based nutrition alongside metformin therapy, but the smaller-than-planned sample size, absence of metformin-only and diet-only comparison groups, and predominantly female, well-educated study population limit broader conclusions. Future research should examine how dietary patterns, metformin treatment, and changes in gut microbial communities interact to influence metabolic outcomes.
