# Recent Reading

Scott shares eight books he found interesting, offering brief reflections on each.

## Books Covered

**1. *Envy: A Theory of Social Behaviour* — Helmut Schoeck**
Schoeck argues envy is corrosive to society, suppressing individual achievement. Notably, envy targets nearby peers more than distant elites — as seen in the Cultural Revolution, where local teachers and small landowners bore the brunt of populist malice. Scott notes this pattern appears in modern right-wing populism as well.

**2. *Infinite Powers* — Steven Strogatz**
A history of calculus. Scott praises Strogatz's work generally but finds the book's repeated attempts to justify calculus's importance unnecessary for its likely audience.

**3. *The Fragility of Goodness* — Martha Nussbaum**
Nussbaum contends that genuine moral dilemmas exist — situations offering no good outcome. Greek tragedies embodied this. Philosophers from Plato through Bentham have sought frameworks to eliminate such dilemmas, but Nussbaum challenges that project.

**4. *Who We Are and How We Got Here* — David Reich**
Ancient DNA research reveals human prehistory involved far more migration and mixing than assumed. The notion that populations stayed put after initial migrations is, Reich's genomic evidence suggests, largely wrong.

**5. *The Myth of Sisyphus* — Albert Camus**
Camus opens with the claim that suicide is the only serious philosophical question. He proposes embracing absurdity through experiential abundance. Scott finds this psychologically unconvincing, arguing humans are fundamentally goal-directed and need reformable — not abandoned — notions of meaning.

**6. *The Perfectionists* — Simon Winchester**
A tour through the history of precision engineering. A standout anecdote contrasts Rolls-Royce's handcrafted quality with Ford's Model T — Ford's interchangeable parts actually demanded greater precision, since no hand-fitting was possible.

**7. *The Marginal Revolution* — Tyler Cowen**
Cowen traces the history of marginalism in economics — the insight that decisions hinge on incremental rather than average costs. Scott notes Cowen's argument that this mode of thinking is losing ground to econometrics within the discipline.

**8. *Catch-22* — Joseph Heller**
Scott calls it possibly "the funniest book I've ever read." He shares several short passages illustrating its satirical wit. The novel reinforces his view that fiction isn't merely entertainment — it generates a distinctive kind of thinking.
