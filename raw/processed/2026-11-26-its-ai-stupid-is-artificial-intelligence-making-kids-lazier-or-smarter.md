# It’s AI, stupid: is artificial intelligence making kids lazier or smarter?

ChatGPT was first released during my final year of university. Having discovered it earlier than most, it became a sort of esoteric cheat code. A little hungover? Not to worry, simply pop your essay question into the chat box and Bob's your uncle — a little robot brain would scramble to action, pumping out lines of unremarkable yet passable academia.

I grew increasingly lazy whenever I deemed an essay topic uninteresting, yet refrained from using it often, mainly due to a sense of unease. It felt like entering a bicycle race with training wheels fitted; I didn't like it thinking for me.

Things are different today. In a survey of over 2,000 UK children for Safer Internet Day 2026, nearly all children aged eight to 17 (97 per cent) are using AI tools.

The Higher Education Policy Institute's 2026 survey of UK undergraduates found that 94 per cent use generative AI to support assessed work. One respondent reported: "I'm not using my brain at all."

The share of secondary teachers reporting a pupil using AI without permission rose from 15 per cent in 2023 to 58 per cent in 2026, according to Teacher Tapp.

So is AI making young people lazier? The answer is nuanced, according to Rose Luckin, professor emerita at UCL and a leading expert on AI in education.

"It makes them lazier when it does the thinking for them, and sharper when it makes them explain their thinking."

The problem, she argues, lies less with students than with the work they are set. "It's not necessarily about being lazy. It's about what we're asking of them. We have to design assignments where it's not about the AI doing it for the student."

In the same way that a few sweaty hours of regular exertion helps us to carry the shopping without a thumping chest, regular mental strain keeps our brain from going soft.

A 2026 study in China of more than 26,000 secondary students over two and a half years found their homework got faster and better with AI, but exam scores, where AI was not used, fell by up to 24 per cent. The more students used AI, the bigger the fall. "What they produce can increase in quality," says Luckin, "but what they actually learn, and therefore can do when the tool is taken away, decreases."

## AI as a 'sparring partner'

The evidence isn't all bad. When AI is not relied on for delegation, and is instead used as a kind of "sparring partner", the results trend the opposite way. A 2025 Harvard physics study found that with a purpose-built AI tutor designed to give one step at a time, students learned more than twice as much in less time.

So, effortful learning reshapes the brain positively; AI can either accommodate that, or simply replace its function. In other words, people who use AI in order to be lazy get lazier brains, while people who use it to give their brain a workout get sharper.

The danger, Luckin warns, is that the tools "might also make you feel a bit cleverer than you really are".

The catch-22 for young employees is that in order to verify and prompt AI well, they usually need to start with entry-level jobs, which are disappearing. Online job adverts in London fell 64 per cent from the May 2022 peak to February 2026, and the remaining roles favour experienced candidates.

London now has the highest youth unemployment rate in the country — nearly one in four economically active 16 to 24-year-olds were out of work early this year.

"How do you build the expertise if that first rung of the ladder is being taken away?" asks Luckin.

Daniel Reast, senior researcher at Centre for London and co-author of its Held Back report, is blunt: London's professional sectors "cannot automate the bottom rung of the career ladder and still expect experienced workers to appear further up it". Only 17 per cent of the capital's 16 to 18-year-old leavers moved into sustained work or an apprenticeship in 2022-23, compared with 25 per cent in Greater Manchester.

Reast cautions that "it is too early to put a credible percentage on AI's contribution", with hiring costs and a weak economy still the bigger cause. But AI, he says, is becoming "an additional and potentially structural pressure".

Alan Milburn's wide-reaching report released last week hopes to tackle the patchwork of issues causing the UK to have the highest proportion of Neet (not in education, employment or training) young people in Europe apart from Romania.

## Keeping up with the machines

Where that leaves us in 10 or 20 years is hard to call.

"What they do need to be really good at in the future is learning," says Luckin, "because we cannot predict with any accuracy exactly what knowledge, what capabilities are going to be the thing in three years, five years, 10 years."

So, what can be done? Luckin's advice to students is simple: "Think what you think the answer is first," then use AI "to challenge your thinking".

Above all: "Don't hand off the thinking to the AI." Daniel Susskind, author of What Should My Children Do?, proposes that parents should "teach both, test both": teach children to work both with AI and without it, and examine both. The basics come first: reading, writing, maths, independent thinking.

Employers have a part to play too. Where AI removes routine tasks, Reast says, they should "replace their training value through supervised projects, mentoring and clear routes for progression". That means reversing a long decline: employer investment in training in London fell by more than 45 per cent in real terms between 2011 and 2024.

"London does not lack talented young people or successful, growing industries," says Reast.

"What it lacks is a reliable first step between the two." For the time being, as machines keep getting smarter, the best we can do is try to make sure we do, too.
