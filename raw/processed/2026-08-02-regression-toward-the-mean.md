# Regression Toward the Mean: An Introduction with Examples

Regression to the mean is a statistical phenomenon first identified by Sir Francis Galton. It holds that in complex systems involving chance, extreme outcomes tend to be followed by more moderate ones.

## Key Examples

**Employee Performance:** A manager enrolls low-performing employees in a training program and sees improvement — but the gains may reflect regression, not the program's effectiveness. As Bevelin explains, performance measurements contain both a true component and a random error component.

**Sports (Ski Jumping):** Kahneman noted commentators inventing psychological narratives to explain what was simply statistical regression between first and second jumps.

**Military Feedback:** An Israeli Air Force officer believed praise hurt performance and criticism helped — Kahneman demonstrated this was regression at work, not feedback effects.

**Depression Treatment:** Headlines crediting energy drinks or cat-hugging for improving depressed children's moods likely reflect regression, since extreme groups naturally trend toward average over time.

## Imperfect Correlation

Regression to the mean occurs whenever correlation between two variables is less than perfect. Kahneman's rule: "Whenever the correlation between two scores is imperfect, there will be regression to the mean."

## Solution

Introduce a control group to distinguish genuine improvement from natural regression. When that's impossible, compare against historical benchmarks cautiously.

## Takeaway

Prioritize track records over single-event outcomes, and recognize that extreme results influenced by chance will tend to normalize over time.
