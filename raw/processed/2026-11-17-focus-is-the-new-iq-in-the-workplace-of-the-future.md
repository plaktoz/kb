# Focus is the new IQ in the workplace of the future

Don’t worry about AI taking your job. Worry about losing your focus and your ability to learn. Louise Opprud explains why deep focus could become one of the most important skills in tomorrow’s workplace.

As artificial intelligence takes over more of our tasks at work, the digital workplace is putting our own intelligence under pressure. According to Louise Opprud, founder of Work Life Lab, this is one of the main problems in a labour market that has become focused on technology at the expense of people. She has been working on the future of work for fifteen years.

When our working day is constantly interrupted, it becomes harder to concentrate on increasingly complex tasks.

“When we don’t have a space where we can concentrate, the brain can neither learn new things nor tackle the most demanding tasks. Research shows that we lose up to 40 per cent of our productive time when the working day is fragmented by distractions. And as AI takes over routine tasks, it is precisely the complex, creative and analytical tasks that remain for us – tasks that require deep concentration,” says Louise Opprud.

The more AI produces, the more important the human element becomes: asking the right questions, evaluating the answers and taking responsibility for them.

## The ability to keep learning

The World Economic Forum predicts that 39 per cent of our current skills will have changed or become obsolete by 2030. Former Google CEO Eric Schmidt goes even further, estimating that the next generation of AI could take over 90 per cent of the tasks we currently perform at work. He expects this technology to arrive around 2030–32.

According to Louise Opprud, this changes how work needs to be organised. So which skills will matter most? The ability to learn new skills and think creatively, she says.

“As work tasks are taken over by algorithms, the workplace must increasingly become a place where people learn new things and develop their skills. And the skills that will be most highly valued will be linked to the top line: innovation, creativity and new product development,” says Louise Opprud.

There are strong indications that IDA’s members already view AI as a benefit. Fifty-two per cent say that AI has increased their job satisfaction, either to some extent or to a large extent, increased their job satisfaction, and members more often feel that their creativity, professional skills and critical thinking are being strengthened rather than weakened. But reaping these benefits requires that we have the capacity to engage with what AI delivers.

## ‘Brain fry’ and ‘workslop’: the hidden costs of AI at work

AI may not take your job, but it could take something else: your focus and your ability to think critically.

Two new terms have emerged: ‘brain fry’ and ‘workslop’. The first describes a brain that is overloaded with input. The second describes AI-generated work that looks finished and well-thought-out, but doesn’t hold up when you scratch beneath the surface. ‘Ask the chatbot’ has even come second in Djøf’s annual poll of the most irritating workplace phrases.

So while we save time producing material, we have to put more effort into critically evaluating what we receive. That requires the very concentration that Louise Opprud believes our working culture undermines.

“When we focus deeply and enter a state of flow, where we are completely absorbed in something, our creativity, problem-solving and learning all improve significantly – some studies show an increase in creativity of up to 700 per cent. Our best thinking happens when we focus on one task at a time. But we’ve created exactly the opposite: we’re interrupted every other minute, jumping from meeting to meeting and juggling a swarm of Teams chats, pop-ups and messages whilst trying to carry out our most important work,” says Louise Opprud.

## The most important work gets done once the children are in bed

“In 2026, the most important work often doesn’t take place during working hours at all. Many managers and employees tell me that when they really need to concentrate, they have to be at home in peace – in the evening, once the children have been put to bed, or at the weekend. The working day has become a time for coordination, while the thinking gets done at home,” says Louise Opprud.

Louise Opprud points out that the brain is at its least effective in the evening when it comes to demanding tasks. Working at the weekend also leaves you without a chance to recover, while higher stress levels reduce both mental performance and the ability to focus.

“Unless you’re one of the roughly 16 per cent of the population who are night owls, the evening is a poor time for thinking. For the rest of us, the brain is at its sharpest during the first seven hours of our day. When we put off the most difficult tasks until the evening, we’re scheduling them for the time of day when, biologically speaking, the brain finds it hardest to perform,” says Louise Opprud.

## The most important skills in the age of AI

- Analytical thinking
- Creative thinking
- Technological literacy

According to the World Economic Forum, these skills are already among the most sought-after, and demand for them is expected to grow particularly strongly in the coming years.

## AI can also tackle the difficult tasks. So what’s left for you?

If this all feels a little overwhelming, that’s hardly surprising. The fact that you’ve read this far is actually an excellent sign that you can stay focused – and you’ll need to.

Because AI isn’t just taking on the easy tasks. In September, OpenAI claimed to have solved the Navier–Stokes problem, one of mathematics’ seven Millennium Problems, using thousands of AI agents. The proposed solution has not yet been accepted and has sparked controversy amongst mathematicians. But the controversy itself is telling: a proof produced by AI in a matter of days may require months of concentrated expert work to verify.

“The more AI produces, the more important the human element becomes: asking the right questions, assessing the answers and taking responsibility for them. As Daniel Kahneman describes in Thinking, Fast and Slow, the brain has two ways of thinking: a fast, intuitive mode for easy tasks and a slow, focused mode for more difficult ones. It is this slow thinking that AI leaves to us – and it requires a new digital working culture,” says Louise Opprud.

Perhaps one of the most important skills of the future will be the ability to make time for deep concentration, rather than to reply quickly to emails or Teams messages.

We experienced this first-hand when we tried to get hold of Louise Opprud. Instead of a quick reply, we received an auto-reply:

“The nature of my work requires me to spend most of my time in deep research mode, with customers in creative meetings, or giving talks. I therefore don’t have the ability to answer my emails every day. Instead, I focus on email a couple of times a week – so I will get back to you, but probably not straight away :-)”

According to Louise Opprud, in many workplaces there is a prevailing narrative that a good colleague is one who replies quickly. And who wouldn’t want to be a good colleague? But when we know the cost of an ‘always-on’ culture, we need to rethink accessibility and focus time in a new way.

“Without focus, there is no learning, no innovation and no complex thinking – and so we lose our relevance in a future that is making new demands faster than ever. We need to focus less on technology and more on how work is organised. We need to question our working culture and experiment with new ways of working that create better conditions for immersion, learning and creativity,” concludes Louise Opprud.
