# Supporting well-being, together: Insights from Brown's staff development day

Brown's 2026 Staff Development Day featured an opening keynote by Dr. Jud Brewer, Professor of Behavioral and Social Sciences and Director of Research and Innovation at the Brown Mindfulness Center. Drawing on neuroscience and clinical research, Brewer explored how stress and anxiety can become learned habits and how awareness and curiosity can help change them.

Centered on the theme "We Are Brown: Forward Together," Brown's 32nd annual Staff Development Day brought nearly 1,700 staff members together for a day of workshops, conversations, volunteer opportunities and community building.

## The brain is always learning

Brewer explained that the brain is remarkably efficient at reinforcing behaviors that appear to solve problems or relieve discomfort, whether or not those behaviors are ultimately helpful. Anxiety often follows this pattern: when uncertainty arises, worrying can create the feeling that something productive is happening. The situation itself may not have changed, but the mind has become occupied, offering temporary relief from not knowing. Over time, the brain begins to associate worry with safety, reinforcing the habit each time the cycle repeats.

"The thing that I never learned in medical school was that anxiety could actually be driven like a habit," Brewer said. Rather than focusing on why people experience anxiety, he began asking how anxiety becomes self-reinforcing, and what helps the brain learn a different response.

Because many of life's greatest sources of stress and anxiety cannot be immediately solved or controlled, repeatedly trying to think one's way out of anxiety may simply reinforce the habit rather than change it. Under stress, the brain becomes less able to rely on reflective thinking, making willpower an unreliable strategy for changing deeply ingrained habits.

## Awareness as a starting point

Brewer offered a different starting point: active, present-moment awareness as a way of gathering new information. Before habits can change, they first have to be seen. He encouraged the audience to become curious about their experience — what thoughts arise, what emotions or physical sensations accompany them, and whether the mind is responding in a way that is actually helpful or simply repeating a familiar pattern.

Asked what feels better, worrying or curiosity, Brewer said: "To our brains, it's a no-brainer. Curiosity feels much better." Yet most people have spent a lifetime strengthening habits of worry rather than curiosity. Learning to meet difficult experiences with curiosity instead of automatic reaction takes practice, but creates the conditions for new learning and behavior change.

## Supporting well-being in the workplace

Choosing a keynote centered on the science of stress, anxiety and habit formation reflected the idea that supporting well-being is not separate from professional development, but an integral part of it — part of a broader shift toward human-centered approaches to leadership and organizational development.

Brewer concluded with a hopeful reminder: if the brain is constantly learning, then change remains possible. Every moment of awareness offers an opportunity to reinforce a different pattern, respond in a different way, and begin writing a different story.
