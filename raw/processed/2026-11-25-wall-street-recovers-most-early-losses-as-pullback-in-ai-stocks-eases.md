# Wall Street recovers most early losses as pullback in AI stocks eases

## AI stocks retreated for a second day following Monday's relief rally

Wall Street stocks recovered most of their steep early losses as a pullback in AI stocks eased.

The S&P 500 fell 0.3 per cent after being down as much as 3.2 per cent earlier Wednesday. The Dow Jones Industrial Average rose 164 points, or 0.3 per cent. The Nasdaq composite fell 0.6 per cent.

In Toronto, the S&P/TSX composite index closed 217 points higher, rising 0.64 per cent.

The market pared its losses even as a report raised more worries about high inflation and as U.S. President Donald Trump plans to escalate his trade war with major trading partners.

"The trade wars aren't over yet," Greg Taylor, portfolio manager at Vancouver-based PenderFund Capital Management, told CBC News on Wednesday.

"It was starting to seem like Trump was backing off, and ready to make some trade deals. This headline shows that we may have gotten ahead of ourselves with those beliefs."

On Wednesday, the Trump administration proposed new tariffs on China and dozens of other countries that they view as harming American industries.

Taylor said such a move would stoke inflation, adding more stress to consumers.

AI stocks retreated for a second day following Monday's relief rally over the surprise reinstatement of Jensen Huang as Nvidia's CEO. Stocks have generally been falling since setting an all-time high last month as worries grow that AI stock prices have grown unjustifiably high.

The market turnaround came as some of the biggest tech companies bounced back from steep drops. Nvidia, which was down more than nine per cent at one point, closed with a 3.9 per cent loss.

Broadcom, which had dropped more than six per cent, ended just 0.1 per cent lower, while Microsoft came back from a more than three per cent slide to finish down 1.2 per cent.

There were some gainers among tech stocks: Meta climbed 3.4 per cent, and Salesforce rose two per cent.

Gains in financial services stocks also helped temper the market's losses. Visa rose 1.8 per cent, Mastercard gained 1.9 per cent and American Express rose 1.2 per cent.

Anthropic bounced back from an early drop to close with a 1.1 per cent gain. Still, since soaring to $115 US per share on the day it became the first major frontier lab to go public, Anthropic shares have tumbled by about 28 per cent.

Roughly three quarters of the stocks in the S&P 500 rose, although the pullback in technology stocks kept the index in the red.

Technology companies, particularly those focused on AI, have large market values that give them a dominant influence over whether major indexes rise or fall. Nvidia is among the most valuable companies on Wall Street and its surge on Monday led a tech sector rally. It is now one of the biggest weights pushing the market lower.

The S&P 500 index is down 5.2 per cent for November, after losing some ground in October and September.

"At this point, between the AI angst and the newly announced tariffs, it doesn't look like the Santa Claus Rally is in store for us this year," said Chris Zaccarelli, chief investment officer for Northlight Asset Management, in a research note.

Questions about Nvidia's corporate governance, as well as the influence of a U.S. president who owns millions of dollars' worth of shares in the company, have added to concerns surrounding the artificial intelligence sector. Relief over Huang's return as CEO is fading because of worries that AI might not produce enough profit to justify the large investments and stock values.

That dynamic is running up against stubbornly high inflation. The latest update on inflation showed that prices rose at a more aggressive 3.8 per cent rate in October from the same period last year, and followed a 3.6 per cent rate in September. The data is from the Federal Reserve's preferred measure of inflation, the personal consumption expenditures price index.

Aside from potentially stifling economic growth, high inflation could mean higher interest rates, with the Fed likely to raise its benchmark interest rate at its December policy meeting to try to tame inflation. The rate of inflation is well above the central bank's target of two per cent.

The economy has been resilient, though, even as inflation squeezes households and businesses and consumers grow increasingly anxious about spending and finances. The government said that the economy grew even faster in the July-September quarter than previously estimated, with growth revised upward to a 4.3 per cent annual rate, the fastest in three years.

Much of the spending that is driving the economy, though, has been from large tech firms building out AI infrastructure and from upper-income households. There are signs poorer Americans are relying more on credit to sustain their spending.

Higher oil prices, fueled by the U.S. war with Iran and its impact on the world's oil supply, have also contributed to inflation. The price of a barrel of Brent crude, the international standard, rose 3.5 per cent to settle at $94.61 US on Wednesday. The price has swung between $72 and $114 US since the United States and Israel attacked Iran in February. Energy companies mostly gained ground Wednesday, including a 1.1 per cent rise for Chevron.

Bank of Canada governor Tiff Macklem said yesterday high energy prices are expected to continue to pinch consumers at the fuel pump and beyond in 2027.

The wide-ranging and often uncertain U.S. trade conflict with much of the world also added to rising costs. The U.S. plans to hike tariffs on China, the European Union and other trading partners after the U.S. Supreme Court struck down broader tariffs that were set last year.

Businesses tend to pass along some or all of the cost of tariffs to consumers. Anger over stubbornly high prices was a factor in pushing voters to punish Republicans by handing majorities in the House and the Senate to Democrats.

Treasury yields fell in the bond market. The yield on the 10-year Treasury fell to 4.87 per cent from 4.91 per cent late Tuesday.
