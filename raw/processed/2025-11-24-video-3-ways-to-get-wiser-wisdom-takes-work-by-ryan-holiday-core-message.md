# 3 ways to get wiser: WISDOM TAKES WORK by Ryan Holiday | Core Message

Animated core message from Ryan Holiday's book "Wisdom Takes Work," presenting three practical methods for building genuine wisdom: working with what you read, working to see your thinking, and working to be wrong.

## Introduction [00:00:00]

**Narrator:** People go to school for 25 years and have very little wisdom to show for it. That's because wisdom requires a certain type of work, work that most either don't know to do or aren't willing to do. As Ryan Holiday explains in his new book, it's not enough to learn new ideas. You need to interrogate them for value, connect them to what you already know, and apply them to your life.

Then, after some time, you need to reflect on their usefulness. But this takes time and energy that most of us simply don't invest. But what good is wisdom anyway? Why bother putting in the work?

Wisdom is worth the work because it plants the seeds of personal freedom. Wisdom gives you a deep understanding of yourself and the human condition, helping you let go of the destructive habits that trap most people, like the hedonic treadmill or the comparison trap.

It helps you navigate the internal forces trying to hold you back, especially self-sabotage in the form of procrastination. Wisdom also frees you from unnecessary anxiety, worry, and chronic stress by allowing you to realize that this too shall pass and that you need less than you think to be happy. That's why as people grow older and wiser, they often become happier despite their declining health.

Earn the freedom that wisdom provides by putting in the work in three distinct ways starting today.

## Way 1: Work with What You Read [00:01:19]

**Narrator:** First, work with what you read. Read more books. Even in the age of AI, books offer something unique. Books contain years of lived experience, carefully refined, and take years to thoughtfully put together. Put simply, writing a good book requires refined judgment. And what is wisdom if not exactly that?

So, here's my challenge to you. Starting tomorrow, trade half your phone and TV time for reading great books. Focus on three book categories. First, read about the human condition. Start with The Diary of Anne Frank, The Gulag Archipelago by Aleksandr Solzhenitsyn, and When Breath Becomes Air by Paul Kalanithi. These books showcase the remarkable strength people can summon in the darkest of circumstances.

Second, read timeless philosophical books that present different approaches to living. Pick up Meditations by Marcus Aurelius, The Bhagavad Gita, or Walden by Henry David Thoreau. Each provides a distinct perspective on living meaningfully. Third, explore books that deepen self-knowledge. Books like The Untethered Soul by Michael A. Singer, Man's Search for Meaning by Viktor E. Frankl, and Awareness by Anthony de Mello.

Now, if you simply flip through the pages of those books, you failed. What separates casual readers from truly wise ones can be summed up in one word, extraction. You must deliberately extract what matters.

Here's how Ryan Holiday does it. As he reads, he makes notes in the margins. Then, after finishing a book, he waits 1 to 2 weeks before reviewing his notes. This delay is crucial because it filters out ideas that would have little impact on his life or work. He extracts only the most memorable insights onto 4 by 6 index cards. One idea per card. A great book might yield 20 to 30 cards.

Now, you might prefer a digital notes system like Notion or Obsidian, and that's okay. Just ensure you have one idea per note. Ryan Holiday's note cards include three things, the idea itself as a direct quote or short summary, the theme that connects it to other ideas, like Stoicism or apprenticeship, and a personal note on why it matters and how he might use it.

Now, if you follow a similar method of extraction, you'll burn useful ideas into your mind and begin applying them in your life.

## Way 2: Work to See Your Thinking [00:03:44]

**Narrator:** The next way you must work to be wiser is to work to see your thinking.

In December 1941, just days after the attack on Pearl Harbor, Japan was rapidly taking the Philippines and other Pacific Islands, and the United States now had to fight a two-front war with supply lines that stretched thousands of miles. The US Army's Chief of Staff, George Marshall, called in General Dwight D. Eisenhower into his office and asked, "What should our approach be?"

Most officers would have started brainstorming aloud, but Eisenhower said, "Give me a few hours." He found a desk in the war plans division, grabbed some paper, and started writing. After pouring his ideas onto the page, he'd pause and push himself.

Are these risks worth taking? What are the trade-offs? What am I missing? Like Eisenhower, wise people aren't afraid to ask for time to get their thinking straight, even when the pressure is intense.

And to get their thinking right, they know they must write. Writing out your thoughts allows you to see them clearly and spot flaws, biases, or gaps in logic. Joan Didion once said, "I don't know what I think until I write it down." Writing for her was an exploration, a way to discover what her mind truly held once it hit the page.

Whenever possible, work to understand what you think on a topic before you open your mouth.

## Way 3: Work to Be Wrong [00:05:03]

**Narrator:** The third way you can work to be wise is to work to be wrong.

Most people use AI to be proven right. AI chatbots know this, so they're quick to affirm whatever ideas you throw at them. Earlier this year, I heard a story on The Daily podcast about a man who became convinced by ChatGPT that he was on the verge of the greatest mathematical proof the world had ever seen. He wasn't a mathematician, so he showed his friends the chat history to see what they thought.

"It seems promising," they said. Encouraged, he emailed his AI-assisted work to mathematicians around the world. Not a single one responded. After several weeks, he did something he should have done from the start. He asked another AI chatbot if he might be wrong.

In seconds, the other chatbot shattered his illusion and saved him years of chasing a dead end. Use AI differently than most people and actively push it to point out what's wrong with your approach. This could be a career path you've been on for years, a business strategy you haven't stopped to rethink, or an assumption you've never challenged.

Develop thick skin and learn to take criticism without being demoralized. Those who cannot admit they're wrong can never become wiser.

## Conclusion [00:06:16]

**Narrator:** In the end, pursuing wisdom is a lot like walking toward the horizon. You never reach it, but you periodically look back and see how far you've traveled. The further you go, the calmer, more curious, and happier you'll be.

As Epictetus said, "Wisdom is getting in fewer fights, letting more things go, and focusing more on what you control."
