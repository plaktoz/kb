# Is UD all in on AI?

At the University of Dayton, artificial intelligence rules can shift from one classroom to the next. A student may be encouraged to use AI for one assignment and prohibited from using it in another, even as every first-year student is now required to learn how the technology works.

This fall, first-year students will complete new instruction on AI fundamentals and human dignity through asynchronous modules embedded in existing courses. Meghan Henning, senior assistant provost for undergraduate curriculum and student success, said students in the College of Arts and Sciences, School of Business Administration, and School of Education and Health Sciences will learn AI fundamentals primarily through Coursera, an online platform that hosts courses developed by universities and companies, in Introduction to the University. Engineering students will receive similar instruction in EGR 103 Engineering Innovation through IBM SkillsBuild, a free online training platform focused on technology and workforce skills.

Human dignity content will be incorporated into HUM 102 and ASI 102, covering AI as a writing and research tool, what it means to be human in an AI-driven world and the technology’s social justice implications. Students will also learn how AI systems use training data, how to write prompts and how to evaluate AI-generated responses for accuracy.

“We don’t just want students who can operate these tools,” Henning said. “We want students who can ask the harder questions: To what end? Who benefits? Who is harmed? What is gained, and what is lost?”

Those questions are harder to answer as generative AI becomes more prevalent in academic settings. UD’s Academic Honor Code requires instructor permission for AI use but does not set one uniform policy for how AI may be used across courses. Instead, the code states that instructor-permitted student AI use “will vary widely from course to course.” Students are expected to check with each instructor to find out what is allowed, meaning a student could use FlyerGPT for one project, be prohibited from using AI for another and be allowed to use it only for brainstorming somewhere else.

“How that plays out in any given degree program will vary,” Henning said. “All of our instructors are providing clear guidance about how AI is or is not to be engaged in individual courses. Some instructors will fold the tools into discussions and assignments while others will use them more sparingly.”

Ryan Allen, executive director of UD’s Learning Teaching Center and Center for Online Learning, told Flyer News in previous coverage that faculty members sometimes felt “stuck between a rock and a hard place” because they lacked a reliable way to discourage AI misuse while worrying that the technology could deprive students of learning intended by an assignment. UD has grappled with that uncertainty since generative AI first became widely available. In 2023, then-Provost Paul Benson argued that the university should be prudent as academic policy was developed around a technology changing as quickly as AI. Since then, UD has significantly expanded its use of AI while many questions about where to draw the line remain.

Henning said the university considers that issue through academic integrity and the purpose of learning rather than through a blanket use-or-ban rule. “Legitimate use amplifies a student’s own intellectual work,” Henning said. “Corrosive use replaces it.”

## To help students learn, AI has to do more than give an answer

Research on AI in education paints a more complicated picture than simply calling the technology beneficial or harmful. A 2026 review by researchers at Hunan University of Science and Technology in China of 35 experimental and quasi-experimental studies involving more than 4,000 participants found ChatGPT had an overall positive effect on learning outcomes, but instructional method and the way AI was incorporated into learning affected the results. Other research has raised concerns about students turning to AI for answers without enough skepticism.

A 2025 Harvard University study found students using a thoughtfully designed AI tutor learned more in less time than students in an active-learning classroom. The researchers also cautioned that ordinary chatbots are not necessarily tutors and can simply give students answers without requiring the effort an instructor intended. The mixed findings leave colleges in unfamiliar territory, since students may need experience with AI for the workplace while still needing time without it to practice writing, reasoning and problem-solving themselves.

Jason Eckert, executive director of Career Services and acting assistant provost for experiential learning, said employers have moved beyond simply wanting graduates to recognize what AI is. “Awareness of AI is no longer enough,” Eckert said. Employers, including members of UD’s Employer Advisory Council, expect graduates to be AI literate and comfortable using AI to augment their work.

“While employers expect applicants to be familiar with AI platforms, they also know that there are many platforms, and that it is impossible to be an expert in all AI tools,” Eckert said. Students are instead being asked to demonstrate skills such as prompt engineering and knowledge of machine-learning fundamentals in addition to familiarity with specific platforms.

Eckert said he hopes students graduate with concrete examples of using AI in coursework, jobs or internships rather than only saying they know how ChatGPT works. At the same time, AI skills are only one part of what employers are seeking.

“Employers really want to hire well-rounded graduates with exceptional communication and ethical reasoning skills,” Eckert said. He said those expectations also include the ability to write, solve complex problems, and consider the common good.

## FlyerGPT puts a price on AI use

UD does not only instruct students about AI. It also gives them access to several models through FlyerGPT, a secure university platform introduced in February 2025 and expanded since then. Students receive a $20 monthly usage limit within FlyerGPT, while faculty and staff receive $25. Users who need more for coursework, research or university work can request a temporary increase.

The amount is not money placed into a student’s account. It functions as a monthly ceiling on the computing costs created by the AI models they use. The arrangement gives students access to models that might otherwise require separate subscriptions, but where UD’s money for FlyerGPT ultimately comes from is less evident. UD’s publicly available information does not identify which university account funds FlyerGPT.

The price of AI is not only financial. Generative AI depends on data centers filled with computing equipment that consumes electricity and requires cooling. The International Energy Agency projects worldwide data-center electricity consumption will grow from roughly 485 terawatt-hours in 2025 to around 950 terawatt-hours in 2030.

Those figures cover the global data-center industry, not FlyerGPT alone, and the environmental cost of a request can vary depending on the model, hardware, data center and source of electricity. UD’s own AI strategy includes sustainability among the considerations that should guide responsible AI use, but the university has not publicly released an estimate of the electricity, water use or carbon emissions connected specifically to FlyerGPT. A 2025 student reflection published through the Hanley Sustainability Institute also examined AI’s benefits alongside concerns about the electricity and water required by the data centers supporting it.

## UD expands from using AI to building with it

UD’s involvement with AI now stretches beyond classroom use. In September, the university announced it will help lead a $1.4 million Gates Foundation grant project developing AI tools for public schools. Meredith Wronowski, a UD associate professor of educational administration, is working with Bryan VanGronigen of the University of Delaware and IT firm Toboggan Labs on an AI system that reviews school improvement plans and produces feedback.

“Leading school improvement efforts is challenging work,” Wronowski said. “Too often, school leaders get bogged down in paperwork or lose momentum when key staff leave. This technology holds promise to clear those obstacles so educators can keep their focus on supporting students.”

The work is intended to reduce the administrative demands placed on education officials. UD computer science faculty members G. Michael Poor and Nicholas Stiffler are also involved in a related idea that would preserve information about a school’s past decisions and improvement efforts when educators or administrators leave. The grant supports undergraduate and graduate research as well.

The project gives UD’s involvement with AI another dimension. Students are learning how to use it, employers expect them to understand it, faculty members decide where it belongs in class, and university researchers are exploring new uses for it. Henning said one reason UD made AI literacy part of every first-year student’s education is that students arrive with widely different levels of exposure to the technology.

“If we left AI literacy to chance, we’d be widening a gap,” Henning said.

The first students completing the requirement have only begun college, leaving its impact difficult to gauge. Henning said UD plans to gather student and faculty feedback and alter the curriculum as technology and student needs change.

For now, UD is venturing further into a realm it approached much more prudently only a few years ago, with AI now sitting inside the curriculum, the tools supplied to students, the expectations employers place on graduates and the research faculty members are helping conduct.
