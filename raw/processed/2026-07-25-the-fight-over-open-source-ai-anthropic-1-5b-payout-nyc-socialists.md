# The Fight Over Open Source AI, Anthropic's $1.5B Payout, NYC Socialists: Evictions = Violence?

## Segment 1: Open Source AI & Chinese Models

China's Moonshot AI released KIMI K3, an open-source model described as performing on par with leading American models at roughly 50% lower cost — echoing the earlier "Deepseek moment." The White House reportedly considered banning Chinese open-source models, with conflicting signals from different officials.

Sacks stated there is "no decision by the White House to ban open-source models" and called a potential ban "a tragic mistake." He argued that if distillation is a genuine concern, Anthropic should be blocking Chinese access to *their* models rather than restricting Americans from using Chinese ones. He accused Anthropic of regulatory capture, noting they grew from ~$10B to over $70B ARR in one year and don't need government protection.

Chamath explained distillation as firing queries at a model en masse, using outputs to train a competing model. He argued KYC (Know Your Customer) requirements would curb distillation but would slow Anthropic's growth — implying the company tolerates distillation for commercial reasons. He framed the regulatory push as "a valuation preservation game by the closed frontier labs," warning that government intervention would raise AI costs for American enterprises and ultimately hurt broader markets.

Friedberg drew a parallel to early internet history: open-source browsers and servers (Firefox, Apache) democratized the web and diffused value broadly, rather than concentrating it among a few gatekeepers. He argued open-source AI would similarly spread economic benefit widely.

Sacks pushed back mildly, contending that China hasn't fully caught up and that both open and closed models will thrive given market size — but agreed government intervention would be harmful.

## Segment 2: Anthropic's $1.5B Copyright Settlement

Anthropic settled a copyright lawsuit for $1.5 billion — described as the largest such settlement in U.S. history — after allegedly downloading ~7 million books from pirated sources to train Claude. Authors receive ~$3,000 per book; lawyers receive ~$101 million.

Sacks highlighted the hypocrisy: Anthropic and OpenAI argue their own training on third-party content is fair use, yet simultaneously claim Chinese distillation of *their* outputs constitutes IP theft. He noted Anthropic's own blog coining "industrial-scale distillation attacks" never actually used the phrase "IP theft" — because doing so would undermine their fair use defense in ongoing lawsuits.

Friedberg distinguished between copying software weights (actual IP theft) versus learning from model outputs (more analogous to benchmarking or reading a book's reviews). He questioned whether copyright law will ultimately restrict AI training on publicly available outputs.

Calacanis argued content creators should organize collectively — as the music industry does — to negotiate licensing terms before being steamrolled.

## Segment 3: Google & Tesla Earnings / CapEx

Google reported blowout results but was down ~7% due to going free-cash-flow negative for the first time since its IPO, driven by massive CapEx investment (~$195–205B forecast). Google Cloud is on a ~$100B annual run rate.

Chamath noted Google's ~32% average return on invested capital over 25 years and said the CapEx build-out is warranted. Friedberg called GCP well-positioned to capture enterprise AI value, model-agnostic, and potentially the world's lowest-cost AI infrastructure in a worst case.

Tesla's CapEx surged 142% year-over-year; SpaceX shares fell ~30% from their IPO-day close.

## Segment 4: NYC Socialism Corner

NYC Mayor Zoran Mamdani introduced tenant-protection measures including banning simultaneous credit checks and income requirements, recognizing tenant unions, and a rent freeze. An activist at the hearing referred to "the violence of evictions."

Friedberg delivered an extended argument rooted in Founding-era property rights philosophy, warning that eroding private property rights leads historically from anarchy to tyranny. Sacks added that inability to evict disruptive tenants harms other residents — particularly working-class tenants — more than landlords.

Chamath and Calacanis argued the straightforward fix is permitting reform to increase housing supply, citing Austin as a data point where deregulated permitting drove down rents.
