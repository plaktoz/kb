# Student Learning and Skill Development in the Age of AI

In asynchronous online learning, effective course design is ultimately about helping students develop knowledge, skills, and professional judgment that transfer beyond the classroom. This is particularly important for adult learners.

Many online students balance coursework with career, family, and other commitments. They are motivated by practical outcomes (e.g., developing expertise, solving workplace problems, earning credentials, and advancing professionally). As a result, successful online learning experiences are typically designed around application, reflection, analysis, and decision-making rather than passive content consumption.

For instructional design teams and faculty, this raises familiar questions:

Are students practicing the skills we want them to learn?

Are they applying concepts in authentic contexts?

Can they explain their reasoning and decisions?

Do assessments provide meaningful evidence of learning?

Until recently, the primary challenge was designing activities that encouraged these behaviors in an asynchronous environment where instructors cannot observe students working in real time. Now, generative AI has introduced a new layer of complexity.

## AI Changes the Learning Equation

Generative AI tools can explain concepts, summarize readings, generate examples, brainstorm ideas, provide feedback, and even produce polished drafts of assignments within seconds. And that’s the short list. (Insert deep breath here)

These capabilities create exciting opportunities. AI can help students overcome barriers, explore new perspectives, receive immediate guidance, and engage more deeply with course content. At the same time, AI can also make it more difficult to determine whether students are developing the underlying knowledge and skills a course is intended to cultivate.

Result: A tool that supports learning in one context may inadvertently bypass learning in another.

## Student Use of AI: Potential Affordances and Slippage

This does not mean AI should be avoided. It means course design requires greater intentionality. The most important question is no longer simply, “Can students use AI?” Instead, faculty must determine:

What thinking, practice, judgment, and decision-making should students continue to perform themselves?

## Designing for Learning Rather Than Tool Use

When incorporating AI into a course, the focus should remain on student learning outcomes rather than on the technology itself.

A useful design principle is that students should not merely consume AI-generated content. They should be asked to engage with it, critique it, improve it, test it, apply it, or explain it. In other words, AI use should make learning more visible, not less.

Faculty may find it helpful to consider the following questions when creating a new course, revising an existing course, or evaluating a proposed AI-supported activity.

## Questions for Reflection

What thinking, practice, or decision-making should students be able/required perform themselves?

Does the activity require students to analyze, apply, explain, create, or evaluate rather than simply accept an AI-produced response?

Could AI use unintentionally remove productive struggle that is important to learning?

How, in your course/discipline, are students developing the knowledge needed to evaluate AI output rather than defer to it?

What process or evidence could students provide to make their reasoning and learning visible?

Are students being asked to use AI because it is relevant to the discipline or profession, or only because the tool is available?

While specific tools may change, the fundamental goal of higher education remains the same: helping students develop the knowledge, skills, judgment, and habits of mind they need beyond the classroom.

Meaningful AI integration doesn’t start with identifying where a tool can be used, but by defining what students should learn, what they should be able to do independently, and how that learning will be demonstrated.

When those questions remain at the center of course design, AI becomes one more instructional tool to consider rather than the driving force behind instructional decisions.
