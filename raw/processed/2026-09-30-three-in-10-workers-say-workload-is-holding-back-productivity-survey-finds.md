# Three in 10 workers say workload is holding back productivity, survey finds

Poor management and skills gaps are also cited, as experts urge employers to examine job design and access to development

Workplace pressures have a harmful effect on employee productivity, research has suggested.

Nearly three in 10 (28 per cent) employees cited workload pressures as affecting productivity, while 25 per cent pointed to poor management or decision making, according to a survey of more than 2,000 UK workers by global professional services firm PwC.

This compared with just over one in 10 (13 per cent) who cited a lack of skills or training.

Ben Willmott, head of public policy at the CIPD, warned that workplace pressure could make it harder for employees to perform effectively.

"If it [pressure] becomes excessive and puts people under sustained stress, it can affect their cognitive capacity and their ability to prioritise and make decisions," he explained.

Jac Jones, HR business partner at AdviserPlus, added that while a lack of skills and training could lead to productivity issues, a heavy workload was usually the main cause.

"Someone may be experienced, capable and well trained but, if they're consistently overloaded or exhausted, they're unlikely to be working at their best," she said.

## Gaps in skills development

However, the survey also highlighted gaps in access to skills development.

Three in five workers (60 per cent) were confident they could learn new skills, but only half (50 per cent) of these said they had access to the learning and development resources they needed.

Fewer than half (48 per cent) also understood which skills they would need in the future.

In the research, one in five (20 per cent) Gen Z workers cited a lack of skills or training as a productivity barrier, compared with 13 per cent of millennials and 11 per cent of Gen X workers.

Sarah Moore, head of workforce transformation consulting at PwC UK, said employers needed to give workers greater clarity about future skills requirements and opportunities to develop.

"As digital and human skills become increasingly interconnected, this will require a renewed focus on workforce planning, job design and supporting early-career talent to build long-term capability," she explained.

## Creating the right work environment

Jones said employers should also consider whether workers had the right environment to use their skills effectively.

"Organisations can invest in more training, but without the right environment, tools and support for people to put those skills into practice, it will only go so far," he added.

Teresa Rose, founder of HR consultancy ConsultHer, said employers should examine how work was organised before assuming additional training was needed.

She advised organisations to assess "workload, workflows, priorities, decision rights, role clarity and cumulative change," adding: "Training people harder will not fix badly designed work."

Willmott said line managers could help reduce workplace pressure, pointing to CIPD research showing employees with better managers were less likely to report excessive pressure at work.

He said managers could help by providing "constructive feedback," supporting employees to perform well and helping with their learning and development.

"They are also more likely to report higher levels of motivation and lower intention to leave," he added.
