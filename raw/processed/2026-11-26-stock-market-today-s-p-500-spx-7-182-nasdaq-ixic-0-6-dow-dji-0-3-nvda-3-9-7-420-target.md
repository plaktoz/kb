# Stock Market Today: S&P 500 SPX 7,182, Nasdaq IXIC -0.6%, Dow DJI +0.3%, NVDA -3.9%; 7,420 Target

## A 97.8% December hike bet pins the S&P 500 near 7,182; reclaiming 7,340 opens 7,420

## Key Points

S&P 500 SPX fell 3.2% intraday below 7,000, then closed down just 0.25% at 7,182.78 into Thanksgiving.

Core PCE ran 3.3% against a 3.2% forecast, pushing December Fed rate-hike odds up to 97.8%.

Breadth flipped: 370 S&P 500 stocks rose against 128 decliners and the equal-weight RSP gained 0.83%.

Wall Street is dark on Thursday, November 26. The NYSE and Nasdaq are closed for Thanksgiving, the bond market is shut, and CME equity index futures trade only until 1:00 p.m. ET before reopening at 6:00 p.m. ET with no settlement prices published for the day. The last cash print investors have to work with is Wednesday's, and it was one of the strangest pre-holiday sessions in years.

The S&P 500 (SPX) closed at 7,182.78, down 18.31 points or 0.25%, after falling as much as 3.2% in the morning. The Nasdaq Composite (IXIC) finished at 23,585.80, down 147.74 points or 0.62%, after a drawdown that reached 4.6% at the lows. The Dow Jones Industrial Average (DJI) did something neither of the others managed: it closed green, up 164.25 points or 0.33% at 50,182.95, wiping out an intraday hole of more than 1,100 points. The Russell 2000 added 0.71% to 2,704.06, and the Nasdaq-100 slipped 0.64% to 26,046.90.

Strip the headline numbers away and the session reads differently. A cap-weighted index lost a quarter of a percent while 370 of its members rose and only 128 fell. The equal-weight S&P 500 ETF (RSP) gained 0.83%. The S&P MidCap 400 jumped 1.23% to 3,577.34, the best of any major U.S. benchmark. Eight of eleven sectors closed higher. The losses lived almost entirely inside the AI complex, where Nvidia (NVDA) dropped 3.9% to $149.30, Oracle (ORCL) gave up 5.13%, Micron (MU) fell 4.53% and Lam Research (LRCX) slid 5.68%.

That split is the story of the week. The market absorbed a hotter core PCE print, a near-certain Fed rate hike, a fresh round of proposed tariffs on 14 trading partners, a credit-rating warning on the world's most important chipmaker and an Anthropic (ANT) stock that dipped under its IPO price, and the average stock still finished higher. Sellers ran out of ammunition by lunch. What they left behind was a market that no longer needs AI leadership to hold its ground but has not yet figured out how to go up without it.

For the year, the S&P 500 now sits 4.9% above its 2025 close, the Dow is up 4.4% and the Nasdaq is clinging to a 1.5% gain. Since November 11, when the S&P 500 closed at 7,619.38 and sat within 2.6% of its October record, the index has lost 5.7%. The Nasdaq has shed 7.4% over the same stretch. Friday's abbreviated session, which closes at 1:00 p.m. ET with bonds following at 2:00 p.m., is the first real test of whether Wednesday's reversal was a bottom or a breather.

Intraday Structure: Gap Down, a Sub-7,000 Flush and an Afternoon Reclaim

The day broke in three distinct pieces. The opening hour was liquidation. Stocks gapped down hard at 9:30 a.m. as traders digested the October PCE report, a proposed Section 301 tariff package and a second straight morning of heavy selling in AI names. The S&P 500 fell through 7,000 soon after the open, and the low of the day came in at the 3.2% mark, which translates to the 6,970 area from Tuesday's 7,201.09 close.

The Nasdaq took the worse beating. At its 4.6% low it traded near 22,640, a full 1,090 points under Tuesday's finish at 23,733.54. The Dow, heavy with industrials, financials and healthcare, was down more than 1,100 points before lunch, putting it in the 48,900 area. Volatility exploded alongside. The VIX traded as high as 34.95 in the early going, above 30 for most of the morning, after sitting at 40.03 on the prior Friday.

The middle of the session was a grind. Bids started to appear in materials, industrials and small caps first, the parts of the market that had been dumped indiscriminately during the November slide but carry little direct AI exposure. Treasury yields were falling the entire time, which gave rate-sensitive groups such as real estate and homebuilders a tailwind. Dream Finders Homes (DFH) finished up 9.24% and RH ripped 8.67%, a tell that the bid was coming from domestic, rate-driven sectors rather than chips.

The afternoon was the reclaim. The Nasdaq-100 rallied more than 4% off its low in a few hours. The S&P 500 climbed back through 7,100 and then through 7,150, finishing 3.0% above its intraday trough at 7,182.78. Nvidia, which had been down 9.1% at its worst, cut the damage to 3.9%. Anthropic, down as much as 9.8%, closed up 1.1%. Dell (DELL), which had been off 10.3% intraday after its earnings, closed up 0.6%.

That kind of path matters for positioning. A 3.2% intraday drop that closes down 0.25% leaves a long lower wick on the daily chart, the classic reversal candle that technicians treat as a sign that forced sellers have been cleared. It does not, by itself, flip the trend. The S&P 500 remains below the 7,340.53 close of November 17 and well under the 7,416.59 close of November 16, the two closes that mark where the latest leg lower began. The VIX still closed at 28.29, up 7.32% on the day, which says the options market did not buy the recovery as anything more than a relief move.

Core PCE at 3.3% and a 4.3% GDP Revision Lock In a December Hike

The macro catalyst for the morning flush was the October Personal Consumption Expenditures release. Headline PCE rose 0.3% month over month and 3.8% year over year, up from 3.7% and in line with forecasts. The core reading, the Fed's preferred inflation gauge that strips out food and energy, also climbed 0.3% on the month and hit 3.3% on a 12-month basis against a 3.2% consensus. That is a tenth hotter than expected and a full 130 basis points above the Fed's 2% target.

The same morning, the third-quarter GDP estimate was revised up sharply, to 4.3% annualized from 3.4%. An economy growing above 4% with core inflation accelerating is not one that invites rate relief. The Beige Book, released in the afternoon, showed 8 of 12 Federal Reserve districts reporting growth, reinforcing the picture of an economy that is running hot rather than rolling over.

The market response in fed funds futures was decisive. Pricing for a 25 basis point rate hike at the December meeting, now less than two weeks away, climbed to 97.8%. Prediction markets carried similar odds in the 93% to 94% range. Bets on a 50 basis point move, which had crept into pricing earlier in November, have faded entirely. The debate has shifted from whether the Fed hikes to how many more hikes follow in 2027.

Here is where the session gets interesting. A hot inflation print paired with near-certain tightening should be a negative for equities, and for the first two hours it was. By the close, the market had treated the data as a known quantity. Hike odds above 97% leave little room for a hawkish surprise on the decision itself. The risk now sits in the forward guidance and the dot plot rather than in the hike, and that is a problem for mid-December rather than for Black Friday.

The data calendar into the long weekend is thin. Jobless claims for the week were not part of Wednesday's flow, and Friday morning brings only the advance goods trade balance plus retail and wholesale inventories at 8:30 a.m. ET. None of those releases carries the weight to reprice the December decision. The next real inflation input arrives with the November CPI, which lands after the holiday stretch. Until then, 3.3% core PCE is the number every rate trader is anchored to, and any equity rally that tries to price in an end to tightening is fighting that figure.

Treasury Yields Fall to 4.85% Even as Hike Odds Hit 97.8%

The bond market did not trade the way the inflation data suggested it would. The 10-year Treasury yield fell 6 basis points to 4.85%, an 11-week low, on the same day core PCE came in hot and GDP was revised up by 90 basis points. The 30-year dropped 6 basis points to 5.24%. The 2-year, the maturity most tied to Fed policy, slipped 2 basis points to 4.61%. The 2s10s spread compressed to 24 basis points.

That move tells you what bond investors are actually worried about. A rate hike in December is priced. What is not priced is whether a slowdown in AI-driven capital spending drags growth lower in 2027 and forces the Fed to reverse course. The long end rallying while the front end holds near 4.61% is a flattening trade, and flattening into a tightening cycle is the textbook signal that the market expects policy to overshoot. Long-term Treasury funds pulled in $2.40 billion on the day.

The 10-year at 4.85% is a meaningful level for equities. Earlier in November, the 10-year was trading near 5.10% to 5.15%, and every tick higher in that zone pressured long-duration growth stocks. A 25 to 30 basis point decline from those highs takes real pressure off equity valuations, particularly for the rate-sensitive cohorts that led Wednesday's rally. Real estate gained 0.92%, homebuilders caught a bid and the S&P MidCap 400, which carries heavier exposure to financing costs, outperformed every other major benchmark.

Credit held up as well. The investment-grade bond ETF (LQD) gained 0.48% and the high-yield ETF (HYG) added 0.12%. There was no widening in spreads to match the morning panic in equities, a sign that the credit market did not view Wednesday's sell-off as a systemic event. The exception was Nvidia itself, where a ratings action put the chipmaker's AA rating on CreditWatch Negative, a story covered in its own section below.

The dollar index eased 0.10% to 103.71, slipping from the 22-month high above 104.20 it touched on Monday. The dollar is still up 6% since early November. Against the yen, the dollar held at 159.14, with 160.00 acting as a ceiling where intervention risk from Tokyo rises. The euro firmed to $1.0973 from a recent low of $1.0881.

Section 301 Tariffs on 14 Partners Land Milder Than Feared

The second shock of the morning came from trade policy. The U.S. Trade Representative proposed a new round of Section 301 tariffs on 14 trading partners, with rates of up to 20% on China and 15% on the European Union and Japan. The administration's argument is that industrial subsidies in those economies have created excess capacity that hurts American producers. The headline alone was enough to deepen the gap down at the open.

The details turned out to matter more than the headline. The new duties will not stack on top of the tariffs imposed in July, which caps the incremental burden on importers and limits the pass-through to consumer prices. By midday, the reading on trading desks had shifted from escalation to a manageable adjustment. The added cost is likely small, though it provides a marginal lift to core goods inflation heading into 2027, which is the last thing a Fed staring at 3.3% core PCE wants to hear.

The market's verdict showed up in the sectors most exposed to trade. Materials led the entire S&P 500 with a 2.07% gain. Industrials added 0.83%. CNH Industrial (CNH) jumped 8.00% and Deere (DE) rose 4.44%, both farm and construction equipment names that would be first in line for retaliation risk. Knife River (KNF) gained 7.77%. If investors genuinely believed Wednesday's tariff package was the opening shot of a broader trade war, those are the stocks that would have been sold, and instead they ripped.

International markets told the same story on a one-day delay. Mexico's IPC rallied 2.99% to 71,372.25 on Wednesday, the best performer among major global indices, and the peso held its ground with USD/MXN at 19.60. Brazil's Ibovespa gained 1.58% to 166,684.61. Canada's TSX added 0.64% to 34,179.99, a one-week closing high, helped by a commodity rally. None of those economies is on the 14-country list, and capital rotated toward them.

The tariff overhang has not disappeared. The proposal still has to move through a comment period, and the list of targeted products has not been finalized. Asian exporters, particularly in Japan, South Korea and Taiwan, carry the heaviest exposure. Thursday's rebound across Asia and Europe, covered later in this analysis, was driven in part by the relief that the proposal was less severe than feared. That relief is conditional. Any widening of the product list or acceleration of the timeline before the December Fed meeting would reopen the wound, and it would land on a market where core goods inflation is already a live concern.

Nvidia at $149.30: CEO Saga, CreditWatch and $2.3 Trillion Gone

Nvidia closed Wednesday at $149.30, down 3.9%, the worst performer in the Dow. At its intraday low the stock was down 9.1%, trading in the $141 area. It was the second straight losing session following the reinstatement of Jensen Huang as chief executive, and it leaves the stock down 27% in November alone and $2.3 trillion below its October 6 all-time high of $243.37. Each 1% move in the shares is now worth $37 billion in market value.

The governance saga is the reason this stock is trading like a small cap. Huang resigned on November 12 following reports of personal conduct inconsistent with the company's code of conduct, including a relationship with an alleged Chinese intelligence asset. CFO Colette Kress became interim CEO. On November 17, the board said it did not intend to reinstate him, even as a public campaign for his return gathered momentum. On November 23, the board reversed itself and voted unanimously to reinstate Huang as President, CEO and board director. The stock rallied more than 30% earlier in the week on that news before giving back ground on Tuesday and Wednesday.

Wednesday added a new problem. S&P Global placed Nvidia's AA credit rating on CreditWatch Negative, citing weaknesses in management and board oversight. A credit action on a company with Nvidia's balance sheet is not about solvency. It is a formal statement that the board's handling of the CEO crisis raised governance questions serious enough to warrant a potential downgrade.

The fundamental picture has not deteriorated at the same pace as the stock. Nvidia's most recent quarter, reported on November 17, showed revenue of $115.8 billion, more than double the year-ago figure, with net income of $2.70 per share against estimates of $2.48. The gap between the operating numbers and the share price is where the debate lives. Bulls see a 27% monthly drawdown on a company that just doubled revenue. Bears see a real governance discount and an October high that looks like a cycle peak.

The chart is the cleanest way to frame it. Nvidia was at $186.64 on November 6, before the board review began, and $156.14 on November 9 after the first sell-off. Wednesday's $149.30 close sits below both. The intraday reversal from $141 shows buyers are willing to step in below $145, but the stock needs to reclaim $156 to repair the damage from the first leg and $186 to erase the scandal entirely. Until it does, Nvidia is a drag on the Nasdaq rather than an engine, and the broad market has to find leadership elsewhere.

The AI Complex Splits: Memory and Oracle Smoked While Meta Ripped

Nvidia was not the only AI name under pressure. The technology sector was the worst of all eleven S&P 500 groups, down 0.83%, and the losses concentrated in the parts of the trade most tied to data-center capital spending. Oracle (ORCL) dropped 5.13%. Lam Research (LRCX) fell 5.68%. Sandisk (SNDK) lost 5.57% and Micron (MU) gave up 4.53% as memory stocks took a second straight hit. The VanEck Semiconductor ETF (SMH) fell 1.01% while the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX) lost 0.50%.

The hyperscalers split down the middle. Amazon (AMZN) dropped 3.12% and Alphabet (GOOGL) lost 2.13%. Meta Platforms (META) went the other way, climbing 3.40% and bucking the sell-off entirely. Meta's AI spending is tied more closely to its own ad business than to selling capacity to third parties, and the market is starting to reward that distinction. Companies whose AI revenue depends on other companies continuing to spend are being marked down. Companies using AI to defend their own franchise are holding up.

Hardware with tangible orders also found support. Dell's record $54.94 billion in quarterly revenue and $132.3 billion AI backlog could not prevent a 10.3% intraday drop, but the stock closed up 0.60%. HP Inc. (HPQ) was the best performer in the S&P 500. The pattern is consistent: concrete bookings are being paid for, while valuation built on future spending is being questioned.

Fund flows confirm the rotation within tech rather than an exit from it. The technology sector pulled in $2.30 billion on Wednesday. Disruptive-tech funds lost $1.35 billion and thematic multi-sector funds bled $1.54 billion. Money is leaving the speculative AI wrappers and moving into broad sector exposure, where it gets diluted across companies with real earnings.

Outside the U.S., the AI unwind hit even harder on Wednesday. South Korea's Kospi collapsed 6.16% to 4,697.51 as Samsung dropped 9.87% and SK Hynix fell 10.32%. Taiwan's Taiex sank 4.80% to 38,195.43 with MediaTek down 10%. Japan's Nikkei lost 2.78% to 57,011.62 as SoftBank plunged 14.83%. Those memory and AI-supply-chain names led the 2026 AI rally and are now repricing at speed.

The circular-financing worry is the thread connecting all of it. AI developers raise capital, spend it on chips and cloud capacity from the same vendors that invested in them, and those vendors book the spending as revenue. When confidence in the end demand wobbles, the entire loop gets marked down together. Wednesday's afternoon recovery showed dip buyers are still willing to defend the group at the lows, but the leaders that carried the S&P 500 into its October record are now its biggest weight.

Anthropic at $82.79 Hugs Its $83 IPO Price

The most-watched new stock on the market spent Wednesday testing its own floor. Anthropic (ANT), which priced its IPO at $83 per share and began trading last Thursday in the largest public offering on record, fell as much as 9.8% in the morning, slicing through its issue price. It rallied 12% off the session low to close up 1.07% at $82.79, still a fraction under the $83 offer and down 0.04% since its debut.

An IPO trading at or below its offer price within a week of listing is a signal worth reading. The book was reported seven times oversubscribed before pricing, so demand at $83 was never the question. The question is what happens when the broader AI tape turns. Anthropic came public in a month where Nvidia is down 27% and the Kospi is down 16%. It has spent its first five sessions absorbing that pressure rather than riding a debut pop.

There is a technical dimension. Underwriters typically defend the offer price in the early weeks of trading through a stabilization mechanism, and $83 has functioned as a hard floor at the close even as intraday trades went well below it. A decisive close below that level, particularly on heavy volume, would tell the market that stabilization support is being overwhelmed. Wednesday's reversal from a 9.8% drawdown to a green close is the opposite signal.

The read-through to the broader market is significant. Anthropic is a pure-play AI developer, the kind of company whose valuation depends entirely on continued enterprise and consumer adoption of large language models. Unlike chip and memory suppliers, its revenue is not directly tied to the data-center buildout cycle. If the stock can hold $83 while hardware names unwind, it supports the thesis that the market is separating AI applications from AI infrastructure rather than abandoning the theme wholesale.

For Friday's half-session, Anthropic's $83 level is one of the cleaner sentiment gauges on the board. A close above it on Black Friday would likely pull other AI software names along. A close below $80 would put the stabilization floor in question and reopen the debate about whether the record offering priced at the top of the cycle.

For a stock that had been treated as a low-growth PC vendor, an 8-cent beat on earnings and a $600 million revenue beat are the kind of numbers that force a rerating.

The stock still fell 10.3% intraday before closing up 0.60%. The backlog is enormous, but the market is asking whether AI server orders at mid-single-digit margins translate into enough profit to justify the multiple. Wednesday's close says the question remains open.

Deere (DE) rallied 4.44% despite an earnings miss and a weaker fiscal 2027 profit forecast. That is a classic bad-news-priced-in reaction from a stock that had been sold hard into the print, helped along by the tariff relief that lifted every farm and equipment name. Breadth Flips: 370 Advancers, Midcaps Up 1.23% and Equal Weight Beats Cap Weight

The single most important number from Wednesday is not an index level. It is the 370-to-128 advance-decline ratio inside the S&P 500. On a day when the cap-weighted index lost 0.25%, 2.9 stocks rose for every one that fell. That is the widest gap between breadth and headline performance in weeks, and it runs in the opposite direction from the pattern that defined most of 2026.

For the bulk of the AI rally, the S&P 500 rose on the backs of a handful of megacaps while the average stock lagged. November reversed that dynamic on the way down, when broad selling hit every sector. On November 17, all 11 sectors finished red and decliners beat advancers by 3.76 to 1 on the NYSE. Wednesday flipped it back. The equal-weight S&P 500 (RSP) gained 0.83%, more than a full percentage point better than the cap-weighted index.

Size factors told the same story. The S&P MidCap 400 rose 1.23% to 3,577.34, the strongest major average of the day. The S&P SmallCap 600 gained 0.81% to 1,657.97 and the Russell 2000 added 0.71% to 2,704.06. All three outperformed the S&P 500 by more than 95 basis points. Midcaps carry the heaviest weighting in industrials, financials and materials, the exact sectors that led the afternoon reclaim, and they carry the least AI exposure of any domestic benchmark.

The question for the rest of the year is whether that broadening is durable or just a one-day rotation into the holiday. There are reasons to take it seriously. Falling yields help small and midcaps more than megacaps because smaller companies carry more floating-rate debt, and tariff relief helps domestic industrials.

There are also reasons for caution. Small-cap blend funds actually saw $887.1 million in outflows on Wednesday, while large-cap blend funds pulled in $6.57 billion. That suggests the breadth improvement was driven by short covering and tactical rotation inside existing positions rather than by new money committing to the small-cap trade. A Fed that is about to hike is not a natural friend of leveraged small companies either, regardless of where the 10-year trades.

The practical read is that the market has stopped falling as a single block. On a day when AI leaders were down between 3% and 6%, the average stock went up. If that pattern holds through Friday's half-session and into the first week of December, it marks the point where the November correction stops being a market-wide liquidation and becomes a sector-specific reset. That distinction matters for how deep the S&P 500 can go from here.

Sector Scorecard: Materials Lead at 2.07%, Technology Finishes Last

Eight of eleven S&P 500 sectors closed higher on Wednesday, and the spread between the best and worst was 290 basis points. Materials led at 2.07%, driven by the tariff relief and a bounce in industrial metals and construction aggregates. Real estate gained 0.92% on the yield decline. Communication services rose 0.89%, carried by Meta's 3.40% rally and Charter's 8.39% jump, which more than offset the 2.13% loss in Alphabet.

Industrials added 0.83% behind the farm and construction equipment rally in Deere and CNH plus a 7.77% move in Knife River. Energy rose 0.74%, a modest gain given that Brent crude jumped 3.51% on the day, which says investors are skeptical that a geopolitically driven oil spike translates into sustained earnings. Financials gained 0.71%, recovering a portion of the November 17 rout when the group fell 1.87% and led all laggards on concerns about how much damage an AI spending pullback would inflict on the broader economy.

Utilities rose 0.48% and consumer staples added 0.46%, the defensive groups participating but not leading. That is a constructive detail. On a genuine risk-off day, staples and utilities lead while cyclicals lag. On Wednesday, the cyclicals led and the defensives trailed, which is how a recovery rally looks rather than a flight to safety.

The three red sectors tell the rest of the story. Consumer discretionary slipped 0.20%, dragged by Amazon's 3.12% loss and Urban Outfitters' 10.33% drop, though RH, Dream Finders Homes and Avis Budget all gained more than 8% within the group. Health care lost 0.56%, extending a weak stretch for insurers and pharma services, with GoodRx's 20.67% collapse the worst move in the sector. Technology finished last at 0.83% lower, with every major chip and memory name in the red and the State Street Technology Select Sector SPDR (XLK) closing near a 1% loss.

The sector rotation has a clear logic. Every group with direct exposure to the AI capital-spending cycle underperformed. Every group with exposure to falling rates, tariff relief or domestic cyclical demand outperformed. The rotation is not about defensiveness. It is about distance from the data-center trade.

Technology's weight in the index is why the S&P 500 can fall on a day when eight sectors rise. For the S&P 500 to reclaim 7,340 and push toward 7,420, either technology has to stop bleeding or the broadening has to accelerate enough to overwhelm it. Wednesday showed the second path is possible. It also showed how much heavy lifting it requires.

Fund flows into long-term Treasuries, the VIX holding above 28 and gold bouncing off a 15-month low all describe the same thing: a market that recovered on the surface on Wednesday while keeping its hedges firmly in place underneath.

Thursday, with U.S. cash markets closed, flipped the script. Wall Street's late reversal carried into Asia. The Nikkei rose 0.9% to 57,499.16, with SoftBank recovering 7.3% and Kioxia ripping 12.6% after announcing an AI memory partnership with Nvidia. Taiwan's Taiex snapped back 2.5% even as TSMC slipped 0.2%. The Kospi edged up 0.3% to 4,711.21, a muted bounce after Wednesday's collapse, with SK Hynix up 2.2% and Samsung up 0.4%. The Hang Seng gained 1.2% to 22,226.88, ending its losing streak. The ASX 200 added 0.8% to 8,763.80. The Shanghai Composite barely moved, closing at 3,476.88.

The monthly damage in Asia is severe. Japan is down 8% in November, South Korea 16% and Taiwan 15%. Asia-Pacific shares outside Japan have lost 9% for the month even after Thursday's 0.6% gain. Those markets carry the heaviest concentration of AI supply-chain exposure in the world, and they have absorbed the worst of the repricing.

Europe ran harder. Germany's DAX rose 1.9% in early trade to 25,180.12 and extended the gain past 2% by mid-morning. France's CAC 40 gained 1.8% to 8,126.47. Britain's FTSE 100 climbed 1.6% to 10,591.83. European indices are less exposed to AI hardware and more exposed to industrials, banks and exporters, which benefited from the read that the U.S. tariff proposal was less aggressive than feared.

The global handoff matters for Friday. When U.S. traders return for the abbreviated session, they will be stepping into a market where Asia and Europe have already priced a relief rally on top of Wednesday's afternoon recovery. That raises the bar for Black Friday. A flat or weak open would mean the U.S. is lagging a global bounce that started on its own tape.

Black Friday Half-Session: Futures Up 0.5%, With 7,200, 7,340 and 7,420 in Play

S&P 500 futures rose 0.5% on Thursday during the shortened CME session, pointing to a 35-point gain at Friday's open, which would put the index near 7,218. Nasdaq futures climbed 0.4% to 0.5% and Dow futures rose 0.2%. The cash market opens at 9:30 a.m. ET on Friday and closes at 1:00 p.m. ET, with bonds shutting at 2:00 p.m. Volume will be thin, which tends to exaggerate moves in both directions.

The first level to watch is 7,200. The S&P 500 closed Tuesday at 7,201.09 and Wednesday at 7,182.78, so 7,200 is the line separating a bounce from a full recovery of the week's losses. Futures defending that level through Thursday is the first box checked. A cash open above 7,200 that holds through the first hour would confirm Wednesday's reversal candle.

Above 7,200, the next resistance is 7,340, the November 17 close and the level where the S&P 500 broke down into the latest leg of the sell-off. Clearing 7,340 would reverse the entire move since the Nvidia earnings report. The upside target beyond that is 7,420, just above the 7,416.59 close of November 16. A move to 7,420 represents a 3.3% gain from Wednesday's close and would put the S&P 500 back above the level it held before AI stocks broke down in force. That target is reachable in a market where breadth has already flipped positive, Treasury yields are falling and the December hike is fully priced.

The downside markers are just as clear. Wednesday's intraday low near 6,970 is the line in the sand. A close below 7,000 would invalidate the reversal and open a retest of that low, with no November support beneath it. For the Nasdaq-100, 25,000 is the equivalent floor, and the composite needs to hold 23,000 to keep Wednesday's structure intact.

The verdict on Wednesday's session is mixed with a bullish tilt. The headline indices closed red on the S&P 500 and Nasdaq, technology was the worst sector and Nvidia remains broken below $156. But 370 of 500 S&P stocks rose, midcaps led by more than 1%, eight of eleven sectors finished green and the index recovered 3.0% from its intraday low on a day that delivered hot core PCE, proposed tariffs, a 97.8% hike probability and a credit warning on the market's most important chip stock. That is how selling exhausts itself. As long as the S&P 500 holds 7,000 and Friday's half-session reclaims 7,200, the path of least resistance runs toward 7,340 and then the 7,420 target, with broad participation doing the work that AI leadership no longer can.
