# Big Tech's AI Bond Splurge Is Squeezing Out Weaker Corporate Borrowers

Treasury yields have climbed to multi-year highs, with the 30-year above 5.3%, just as hyperscalers flood bond markets with over $200 billion in AI-related debt. The combination is squeezing CCC-rated borrowers, whose default rates are climbing toward 2% as refinancing gets more expensive.

Big Tech's AI borrowing spree is no longer just a tech story. It is now a credit-market test, and the weakest borrowers are taking the hardest hit.

The 30-year Treasury yield was hovering around 5.25% in early September, close to levels investors haven't had to live with since before the financial crisis. That's bad news if you need to refinance. It's worse if your rating starts with a C.

CCC-rated companies, the riskiest tier of junk borrowers, don't have the luxury Meta, Amazon or Oracle have. PIMCO said in an August 31 credit note that coupons on CCC bonds maturing in 2027 and 2028 could roughly double if those companies refinance at today's index yields. That is real money. For a weak retailer, media group or telecom borrower with thin cash flow, the difference between a low 2021 coupon and today's market can be the difference between buying time and losing control of the balance sheet.

The awkward part is that the strongest borrowers are now demanding the market's attention. Reuters reported in August, citing BNP Paribas data, that AI hyperscalers had issued about $220 billion of debt in 2026 through August 10, compared with $12.5 billion in the comparable period last year. Fortune also reported that S&P Global counted $225 billion of issuance from hyperscalers and related companies such as Nvidia, a 973.7% jump from the prior year period.

That is not normal refinancing. It is the AI buildout arriving in the bond market with size, urgency and long maturities. Morgan Stanley has put global AI-related debt issuance near $236 billion as of May 31 and forecast nearly $570 billion for all of 2026. You can believe the AI infrastructure cycle is real and still see the pressure it puts on everyone else trying to borrow.

## The best borrowers are taking up the room

Meta sold $30 billion of bonds in a six-part deal in October 2025, Reuters reported, after warning that its capital spending would be a lot larger in 2026. Oracle followed with a $25 billion, eight-part bond sale on February 2, as part of a broader plan to raise $45 billion to $50 billion for Oracle Cloud Infrastructure capacity.

Meta can carry that debt. Oracle can still reach investors. Alphabet, Amazon and Microsoft are not suddenly distressed borrowers. But fixed-income portfolios are not bottomless, and repeated long-dated supply from the same handful of companies changes the price investors demand. Reuters reported that Alphabet's August bond deal was well received, but still required a concession of roughly 10 to 15 basis points versus its existing debt. Earlier AI deals cleared more easily. Now investors want paid.

Oracle is different. Its balance sheet is more exposed to the AI infrastructure bet than Microsoft's or Alphabet's, and credit investors have treated it that way. Bloomberg reported in December 2025 that the yield on Oracle's 5.2% notes due 2035 rose to 5.9%, above the 5.69% average yield on higher-rated junk bonds, while five-year credit default swaps jumped to levels last seen in 2009. Oracle remained investment grade, but the market was sending a blunt message: big AI spending is not free just because the borrower has a famous name.

## Treasury can smooth the market, not save weak borrowers

The Treasury Department has noticed the strain in the long end. On August 19, it said it would at least double the size of liquidity-support buybacks for 10-to-20-year and 20-to-30-year nominal securities, lifting the per-operation amount from $2 billion to at least $4 billion starting September 9 and running through November 4.

That helps market plumbing. It is not a rate cut.

If you are a CCC-rated issuer facing a maturity wall, Treasury buybacks do not make your earnings stronger or your lender base more patient. They may help keep long-dated government bonds trading cleanly, but they do not erase the fact that investors can now buy debt from some of the most profitable companies on earth at yields that looked impossible a few years ago.

S&P Global said earlier this year that U.S. maturities for B-minus and lower-rated debt would rise from $56.6 billion in 2026 to $215 billion in 2028. That date is close enough to matter. Many weaker companies used the cheap-money years to push out maturities, but those maturities did not disappear. They moved forward on the calendar.

None of this means the AI buildout is about to crack. The larger point is more practical. When Big Tech turns into one of the bond market's biggest borrowers, weaker companies no longer compete only with each other. They compete with Meta's data centers, Oracle's cloud backlog, Amazon's next campus and the U.S. Treasury's own funding needs. The strongest names will still get funded. The weaker ones will pay up, sell assets or default.

That is where this story lives: not in whether AI companies can raise money, but in who gets pushed aside while they do it.
