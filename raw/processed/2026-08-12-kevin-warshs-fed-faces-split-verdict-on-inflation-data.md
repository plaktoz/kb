# Kevin Warsh's Fed Faces Split Verdict on Inflation Data

July's consumer price index (CPI) report drew conflicting interpretations from economists, as the same data was read as a sign of disinflation, a case against current Fed policy, and grounds to question the numbers themselves.

## The Case for Disinflation

Former Fed economist Claudia Sahm said the report continued to build the case that inflation is moving in the right direction: "Implications for the Fed: good news. Disinflation, even as the Fed's been on hold so far this year, supports its stance." She noted supercore inflation (non-housing core services) rose just 0.2% in July, well below earlier-year pace, calling the narrowing breadth of price increases "the best news in today's report." The share of the CPI basket with prices rising 5%+ fell in July and is back to average levels seen in 1997-2005, when inflation was close to 2%.

The CPI rose 3.4% year-over-year in July, in line with estimates and down from June's 3.5%, while core CPI eased to 2.5% annually from 2.6%. Sahm cautioned two months of data isn't enough to confirm the trend.

Market strategist David Rosenberg echoed that read: core CPI is running at a 1.6% annualized rate over the three months to July, versus 2.7% a year earlier. Inflation showed up last month mainly in computers, airfares, toys, appliances and used cars, but "didn't show up anywhere" else.

## Skepticism Over What the Data Captured

Economist Peter Schiff called the print "misleading," arguing the way energy prices are captured created a lag effect — oil and gasoline started July depressed before rebounding sharply, so July CPI still reflects May's oil price collapse rather than July's rebound.

Charlie Bilello, chief market strategist at Creative Planning, said inflation has run well above the Fed's 2% target since January 2020 — a 4.0% annualized rate, now 13% above a 2% inflation trend — calling it "a massive failure of monetary policy."

## The Fed is Too Tight

James Thorne of Wellington Altus argued the Fed's benchmark rate remains above neutral even as rate-sensitive sectors like housing and business investment show strain, calling the case for staying restrictive "institutional inertia dressed up as vigilance."

## What it Means for Markets

Economist Mohamed El-Erian tied the report to bond market moves, saying elevated Treasury yields have less to do with inflation or Fed policy and more to do with the heavy supply of upcoming government and corporate debt.
