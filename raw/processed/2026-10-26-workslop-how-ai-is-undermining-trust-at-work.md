# Workslop: How AI Is Undermining Trust at Work

Imagine opening a document sent by a colleague at four in the afternoon. The presentation is impeccable. The headings are clear, the formatting is professional, and the text looks carefully prepared. But after a few paragraphs, you notice something strange: despite all those words, there is no argument, conclusion, or concrete thinking. It is pages of content that look like work but do not actually move the work forward.

Now you have two options: talk to your colleague about the quality of the material or quietly redo everything yourself. The second option seems easier. You avoid confrontation and solve the problem. The price, however, can be nearly two hours of rework to fix a single document.

This situation has a name: workslop, one of the silent problems emerging as artificial intelligence becomes more widely adopted in the workplace.

The term describes AI-generated content that looks well-produced at first glance but lacks the substance needed to create value. And its impact goes far beyond the time wasted correcting reports, presentations, and emails. When this kind of content spreads throughout an organization, it begins to undermine something much harder to recover: trust between people.

## What Is Workslop, and Why Has It Become a Workplace Problem?

The term workslop was popularized by research from BetterUp Labs in partnership with Stanford’s Social Media Lab, led by psychologist Kate Niederhoffer and researcher Jeff Hancock.

The definition is straightforward: work generated by artificial intelligence that masquerades as good work but lacks the substance needed to move a task forward.

The word masquerades is essential to understanding the problem. Low-quality work has always existed, but its flaws are usually easier to identify. Workslop is different: it presents the appearance of a professional deliverable without the reasoning, accuracy, or contribution that should support it.

It might be a visually appealing presentation with no useful conclusions. A four-paragraph email that could have been handled in a single sentence. A research summary that sounds convincing until someone decides to check the sources. Or a report full of corporate jargon that does not help anyone make a decision.

The problem is not simply using artificial intelligence to produce content. It is confusing the appearance of productivity with the actual creation of value.

According to the cited study, 40% of surveyed workers said they had received workslop in the month before the survey. This phenomenon should therefore not be treated merely as an individual failure or an isolated case of technology misuse. It points to a broader challenge in how companies are integrating AI into their workflows.

## The Hidden Cost of AI: Productivity Lost to Rework

The promise of generative AI is compelling: produce more in less time, automate repetitive tasks, and free people to focus on higher-value activities.

But there is a difference between generating content quickly and completing work well.

A Workday study cited in this context indicates that 37% of the productivity gains associated with AI are offset by rework created by the tools themselves. Other research cited in the discussion suggests that approximately two-thirds of workers spend six hours or more per week correcting AI-generated outputs.

This is where the term botsitting comes in: the need to continually supervise, verify, and correct the work of AI systems, as if caring for a machine that cannot yet operate reliably without oversight.

The Harvard Business Review has also been cited as a source for an estimate of approximately $186 in lost productivity per employee per month. In an organization with thousands of professionals, this kind of cost can add up quickly.

These figures reveal a contradiction: the technology that is supposed to save time can also create new tasks involving review, validation, and correction.

When one person uses AI to prepare a document but someone else has to spend nearly two hours checking it, the work has not disappeared. It has simply changed hands.

The real productivity gain is not how quickly content is produced, but the value it delivers once it is produced.

And that is only the first layer of the problem.

## The Real Impact of Workslop Is the Loss of Trust Between Colleagues

If workslop caused nothing more than rework, it would already be a significant problem. But its deeper effects emerge in the way people begin to see one another.

Picture a work meeting. A colleague presents an interesting idea, makes a precise observation, or shares a well-structured analysis. In the past, you would probably assume those words reflected that person’s knowledge, experience, and judgment.

Now a doubt may arise: Is this really what you think, or is it just what artificial intelligence produced for you?

That question changes the relationship between the person producing information and the person receiving it.

For a long time, professional communication carried a kind of implicit credit. When someone presented an analysis, the team generally associated that content with the author’s ability to exercise judgment. Professional reputation was tied to what a person delivered.

Workslop makes that relationship harder to establish. A sophisticated report may have been produced without sufficient research. A presentation may sound convincing without reflecting a genuine understanding of the problem. An idea may seem original even though no one has examined whether it makes sense in that particular context.

As a result, distrust is no longer directed solely at the tool. It begins to affect the person who used it.

According to the BetterUp Labs and Stanford Social Media Lab research cited earlier, more than half of the participants who received workslop reported feeling frustrated, more than a third said they felt confused, and approximately one in five said they felt offended. Nearly half also came to view the people who sent this kind of content as less capable, less trustworthy, or less creative, and became less willing to work with them again.

These reactions reveal an important dimension of the problem. Frustration and confusion may result from an inefficient process. Feeling offended, however, suggests that the experience can also be interpreted as a breach of expectations and trust.

Artificial intelligence is supposed to make collaboration easier. When it is used to produce content without substance, it can instead lead people to question the quality, authorship, and even the honesty of their colleagues’ work.

That is why workslop is not just a tax on productivity. It is a tax on trust.

Lost time can be recovered. Rebuilding credibility in a professional relationship is much harder.

## Knowledge Decay: How Misusing AI Can Weaken Organizational Knowledge

Workslop’s impact does not end with the person who sends a document and the person who has to fix it. When this behavior becomes routine, it can affect an organization’s ability to create, share, and preserve knowledge.

In an article published by the Harvard Business Review in June 2026, researchers Matthias Holweg of Oxford and Thomas Davenport of Babson discussed this risk under the concept of knowledge decay.

The mechanism is relatively simple.

One person outsources part of their own reasoning to artificial intelligence and forwards an apparently well-developed result. The recipient realizes they can also turn to the tool to complete their part of the work. Instead of deepening the original analysis, they produce another automated piece of content and pass it along.

The next person does the same.

At every stage, the material may look professional, but errors, generalizations, and gaps in reasoning can accumulate. Gradually, the organization may lose the ability to distinguish what it truly knows from what it has merely produced.

That is the danger of knowledge decay: it is not just about receiving incorrect information, but about weakening the human processes that allow people to identify errors, build arguments, and develop reliable knowledge.

The issue is also related to how language models work. They generate responses based on learned patterns and the prediction of word sequences. Although they can produce useful and sophisticated results, this does not guarantee that every statement is true or that a response is appropriate to a specific context.

A more advanced model may improve the quality of many outputs, but it does not eliminate the need for human verification.

That is why botsitting and botshitting represent such different behaviors. In botsitting, a person supervises the AI’s output, checks the information, and makes the necessary corrections. In botshitting, content is forwarded without responsible review, even when the person sending it cannot explain or defend the conclusions.

When oversight is not valued and speed is the only measure of performance, people may become less inclined to feel responsible for the material they produce with the help of technology.

The consequence is not just a bad document. It is an organization that may begin to confuse information volume with knowledge.

## Why Human Specificity Has Become an Advantage in the Age of AI

In an environment where anyone can generate texts, reports, and presentations in minutes, producing something that merely looks professional is no longer a differentiator in itself.

The question becomes: what does this work contain that demonstrates genuine knowledge, experience, and judgment?

The answer lies, to a large extent, in specificity.

Artificial intelligence can easily produce generic text. It can organize information, suggest structures, summarize concepts, and offer different approaches to solving a problem. But the value of work also depends on the ability to connect those possibilities to concrete facts, particular circumstances, and real decisions.

A relevant report does more than present market trends. It explains what a particular trend means for that company, at that moment, given its specific challenges.

A good presentation does more than display well-designed charts. It helps answer an important question, supports a recommendation with evidence, and makes clear which decisions need to be made.

A high-quality analysis is not merely fluent. It shows that someone investigated the problem, evaluated the information, and took responsibility for the conclusion.

That is what makes human authorship so important.

In 2026, Adam Mosseri, head of Instagram, also drew attention to the growing scarcity of authenticity and the changing perception of what it means to create. The question is no longer only whether someone can produce something, but whether they can produce something that carries an original contribution of their own.

This reflection applies directly to professional work. If artificial intelligence can generate a first draft of almost any kind of content, the human differentiator must appear in the selection of information, the interpretation of facts, the context, and the judgment that shape the final result.

A simple test can help: how many concrete elements in your work demonstrate that you truly understand the subject?

It might be a piece of data you verified, a conversation that changed your interpretation, an experience you have had, or a decision grounded in circumstances the tool does not know on its own.

AI can help structure and communicate these contributions. What it should not do is replace the responsibility to make them.

## How to Avoid Workslop: Three Practices for Professionals and Companies

Combating workslop does not require abandoning artificial intelligence. It requires changing how we define quality, productivity, and responsibility.

The solution begins when companies stop treating technology adoption as a goal in itself and start evaluating the actual value it creates.

## 1. Set Quality Standards for AI-Generated Content

Before forwarding a document created with the help of artificial intelligence, check whether it is ready for someone else to use.

A minimum quality standard should include four elements:

Verifiable sources: Relevant information should be supported by sources that can be checked.

Explicit uncertainty: Limitations, doubts, and points that still need confirmation should be identified rather than presented as established facts.

Human accountability: Someone must read, understand, and validate the content before sending it.

A clear purpose: The document should answer a question, support a decision, or fulfill a specific task.

These criteria do not need to turn every deliverable into a bureaucratic process. On the contrary, they can reduce rework by clarifying what it means to deliver something that is ready to use.

The central rule is simple: do not forward content just because it looks complete. Send it when you can explain why it matters and have sufficient confidence in what it says.

## 2. If You Lead a Team, Reward Results — Not the Volume of AI Use

Companies also need to examine the incentives they create.

If leaders measure AI success by the number of tools adopted, the amount of content produced, or the volume of interactions with AI systems, they risk encouraging technology use rather than actual value creation.

A team can produce more reports, presentations, and messages without making better decisions or solving problems more efficiently.

That is why leaders need to define what a good outcome looks like for each activity. Evaluation should consider information accuracy, content usefulness, decision quality, total time spent — including review and correction — and the actual impact on the work.

It is also important to recognize oversight as a legitimate part of the process. Checking sources, identifying errors, and improving an AI-generated response are not secondary tasks. They are activities that help turn an automated output into reliable work.

The goal should not be to maximize the use of artificial intelligence. It should be to use technology to improve performance without compromising people’s quality of work, autonomy, or accountability.

## 3. Preserve Your Authorship and Make Your Judgment Visible

When content production becomes fast and accessible, authorship takes on a different dimension.

Signing a document is not enough. You need to demonstrate that you made a genuine intellectual contribution to its creation.

That means adding context, questioning assumptions, checking claims, and explaining why a particular conclusion makes sense. It also means taking responsibility for the limitations of what you produce, acknowledging AI assistance when appropriate, and not presenting as your own knowledge something you have not understood or validated.

Artificial intelligence can expand your ability to research, organize ideas, and communicate information. But the contribution that distinguishes your work must remain recognizable.

In a world of automated content at scale, authenticity does not depend on rejecting all technological assistance. It depends on preserving what makes a contribution genuinely yours: experience, critical thinking, judgment, and responsibility.

## The Future of Work Depends on How We Use Artificial Intelligence

Let’s return to the opening scene: it is four in the afternoon, and you have just received that impeccable document that says nothing.

You may be the person receiving workslop and spending time correcting someone else’s work. But you may also be the person producing it, confident that polished formatting and fluent text will be enough to convince others that the task is complete.

The difference between these two positions is not always obvious. In many cases, the same person occupies both roles at different times.

We cannot eliminate every AI error or prevent superficial content from circulating in the workplace. But we can decide how to use these tools, what quality standards to demand, and what responsibility to take for the work we send.

Before forwarding your next report, email, or presentation, ask yourself one question: Is there something here that only I could have contributed?

It might be a specific interpretation, a properly verified piece of data, relevant experience, or a recommendation that demonstrates a genuine understanding of the problem.

If the answer is no, perhaps the document still needs work.

Artificial intelligence can provide the structure, accelerate research, and help organize ideas. But producing a result that truly matters requires more than appearance, fluency, and speed.

Because when polished content becomes abundant, what distinguishes good work is not the mask. It is the thinking behind it.

And trust begins when we take responsibility for what we deliver.
