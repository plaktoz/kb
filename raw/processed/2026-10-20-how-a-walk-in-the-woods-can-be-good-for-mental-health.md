# How a walk in the woods can be good for mental health

Ever wonder why a walk through the woods can be such a mood booster? Research from the University of New Hampshire shows that it can be as simple as the sounds people experience in nature, people's awareness of biodiversity—the many species living in the woods—and educational signage sharing information about the variety of animals, birds and trees.

"Previous nature-health research had a major limitation: it could tell us that biodiversity is good for mental health but couldn't tell land managers, park planners or health care providers what to actually do with that information," said Lauren Ferguson, assistant professor of recreation management and policy. "The research lacked specificity about which aspects of biodiversity matter most and how to enhance the mental health benefits of nature visits."

The study, recently published in *Landscape and Urban Planning*, surveyed visitors to College Woods—a 230-acre (93-hectare) natural area bordering the UNH campus in Durham—between June 2023 and May 2024. Researchers surveyed visitors both before and after their visits and also measured biodiversity using wildlife cameras, bird surveys and vegetation records.

Overall, visitors reported more positive emotions, fewer negative sentiments and greater feelings of restoration, including relaxation and recovery from mental fatigue, after spending time in the woods. On average, participants' self-reported restoration scores increased by nearly 30% following their visits.

Additionally, researchers found that visitors' overall perceptions of biodiversity were stronger predictors of mental well-being than the actual number of bird and mammal species researchers measured. The pleasantness of sounds visitors experienced, such as birdsong, rustling leaves and flowing water, was another strong predictor.

As part of the study, educational signs were placed near the trail entrance during designated periods and removed during others to explore whether helping visitors recognize biodiversity could influence their experiences. The sign, titled "Meet the neighbors!", highlighted the forest's wildlife, including more than 20 mammals, 50 birds and 110 plants and trees. It encouraged visitors to look and listen to the different species.

Visitors who encountered the sign perceived slightly greater biodiversity than those who did not. The sign was also positively associated with a good mood, although it did not show a meaningful relationship with reductions in negative emotions or increased restoration.

"We think the signs may help people become more aware of the biodiversity around them and notice species they might otherwise miss," Ferguson said. "That can create a stronger, more mindful connection with nature and turn a casual walk in the woods into a more restorative experience."

The study also found that season influenced visitors' experiences. Participants reported greater feelings of restoration during spring and summer, but improvements in mood were observed regardless of season.

By shedding light on what makes time in nature beneficial, the findings offer insights for park managers, urban planners and others responsible for maintaining public green space.

"Our research shows that educational signs about biodiversity were linked to a boost in positive mood during forest visits," Ferguson said. "Biodiversity education signs are a simple, inexpensive tool that forest managers can use to enhance those benefits."

Co-authors on the study include Remington Moll, associate professor of natural resources and the environment; Laura Kloepper, associate professor of biological sciences; Anna Frisbie, graduate student; and Karina Sanchez, assistant professor of biology from California State University, Dominguez Hills.
