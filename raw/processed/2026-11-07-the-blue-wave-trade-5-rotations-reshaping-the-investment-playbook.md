# The Blue Wave trade: 5 rotations reshaping the investment playbook

## A Blue Wave, but not necessarily a red flag for equities

The Democrats’ sweep of both chambers of Congress has triggered a significant repricing across financial markets.

On 4 November, US equities rallied even as parts of the semiconductor and data-centre trade came under pressure. Oil prices fell sharply, Treasury yields declined and the dollar weakened. Healthcare, clean energy and travel-related businesses attracted renewed interest, while defence stocks faced selling pressure.

The market appears to be weighing two competing forces: the prospect of tighter scrutiny of AI infrastructure and constraints on President Trump’s fiscal agenda, against the benefits of lower energy prices, reduced inflation pressures and potentially easier financial conditions.

Importantly, Democrats have won Congress, not the White House. President Trump retains veto power and considerable authority over trade and foreign policy. The election changes the balance of power, but it does not guarantee sweeping policy reversals.

For investors, the opportunity may be less about predicting political outcomes and more about identifying where market leadership could shift.

Here are five rotations worth watching, including potential beneficiaries and businesses facing new headwinds.

## 1. AI rotation: From building capacity to delivering returns

The AI trade is increasingly dividing between companies building infrastructure and those monetising it.

Opposition to data-centre expansion has gained political momentum, particularly around electricity costs, water consumption and local infrastructure. Greater scrutiny could delay projects and increase construction costs.

That creates potential headwinds for data-centre operators, power equipment suppliers and semiconductor companies whose growth expectations depend heavily on rapid infrastructure expansion.

Meanwhile, established hyperscalers with existing computing capacity, strong cash flows and diversified revenue streams could prove more resilient.

China technology offers another angle, with lower expectations, domestic policy support and growing AI capabilities providing a different source of potential returns.

### Potential beneficiaries

Microsoft (MSFT): Existing cloud infrastructure, enterprise AI adoption and recurring software revenues.

Alphabet (GOOGL): AI monetisation across search, advertising and cloud services, supported by substantial operating cash flow.

Amazon (AMZN): AWS cloud services and AI capabilities, with potential benefits if existing computing capacity becomes more valuable.

Meta Platforms (META): AI-driven advertising efficiency and monetisation, although capital spending remains substantial.

Alibaba (BABA / 9988.HK): China cloud and AI exposure, with potential support from domestic policy and improving technology adoption.

Tencent (0700.HK): AI applications across advertising, gaming and digital services.

### Stocks facing potential headwinds

Data-centre operators: CoreWeave (CRWV), Applied Digital (APLD), IREN (IREN) and Cipher Digital (CIFR) could face project delays, tighter oversight or increased funding costs.

Power infrastructure: Bloom Energy (BE), Vertiv (VRT) and GE Vernova (GEV) could face slower order conversion if new data-centre projects are postponed.

Semiconductors: Nvidia (NVDA), AMD (AMD) and Broadcom (AVGO) could be vulnerable if infrastructure spending slows or customers become more selective about capital expenditure.

Memory suppliers: Micron Technology (MU), SK hynix (000660.KS) and Samsung Electronics (005930.KS) could face pressure if AI server demand forecasts are revised lower.

Risks to the view: Regulatory restrictions could remain limited, while AI demand may continue growing faster than available capacity. Scarcity could support pricing for chipmakers, memory suppliers and existing infrastructure providers. Power demand also extends beyond AI, meaning these companies are not uniformly exposed to data-centre construction.

Investor takeaway: This is not necessarily the end of the AI investment cycle. The market may simply become more selective, rewarding companies with visible AI revenues and sustainable cash flows rather than infrastructure spending alone.

## 2. Policy rotation: Healthcare and clean energy return to focus

A Democratic Congress could shift the policy debate towards healthcare affordability, insurance coverage and renewable energy.

Healthcare providers and insurers could benefit if proposed coverage reductions are delayed or softened. Clean-energy companies may also regain investor interest if existing incentives become more difficult to reverse.

Conversely, businesses that benefited from Republican policy priorities could face greater uncertainty.

### Potential beneficiaries

HCA Healthcare (HCA) and Tenet Healthcare (THC): Broader health insurance coverage could reduce uncompensated hospital care and support revenues.

Universal Health Services (UHS): Hospital and behavioural healthcare exposure, potentially benefiting from more stable insurance coverage.

Centene (CNC) and Molina Healthcare (MOH): Exposure to Medicaid and government-sponsored insurance programmes.

First Solar (FSLR): US solar manufacturing and potential support from renewable-energy incentives.

Enphase Energy (ENPH) and Sunrun (RUN): Residential solar and energy-storage exposure, where policy support could improve demand.

NextEra Energy (NEE): Renewable generation and utility investments, with possible benefits from improved policy visibility.

### Stocks facing potential headwinds

Traditional energy producers: Exxon Mobil (XOM) and Chevron (CVX) could encounter renewed regulatory scrutiny and potentially less supportive fossil-fuel policies, although their earnings remain far more sensitive to oil and gas prices.

Risks to the view: Healthcare policy changes may remain gridlocked, while reimbursement pressure and rising medical costs could hurt insurers and hospitals. Clean energy faces subsidy uncertainty, project delays, financing costs and competition. Oil majors could also benefit from other factors, including stronger demand or supply constraints.

Investor takeaway: Policy-sensitive sectors may experience a re-rating, but investors should distinguish between changes in political sentiment and changes that can meaningfully improve earnings.

## 3. Fiscal and geopolitical rotation: From defence to the consumer dividend

A Democratic-controlled Congress could complicate further fiscal expansion, including President Trump’s proposed increase in defence spending. It may also increase scrutiny of the Iran conflict and trade policies.

The sharp decline in oil prices suggests markets are assigning greater weight to the possibility of geopolitical restraint.

If energy prices remain lower, they could ease inflation, reduce business costs and improve consumer purchasing power. Airlines, travel companies and selected retailers could be among the beneficiaries.

### Potential beneficiaries

Delta Air Lines (DAL) and United Airlines (UAL): Lower jet-fuel prices could improve airline margins.

International Airlines Group (IAG.L): Exposure to European and transatlantic travel, with potential fuel-cost relief.

Booking Holdings (BKNG) and Expedia (EXPE): Online travel platforms that could benefit from resilient travel demand and improved affordability.

Marriott International (MAR): Hotel demand and travel spending exposure.

Walmart (WMT) and Target (TGT): Potential relief from lower transport, energy and imported-goods costs if tariff pressures ease.

### Stocks facing potential headwinds

Defence contractors: Lockheed Martin (LMT), Northrop Grumman (NOC), RTX (RTX) and L3Harris Technologies (LHX) could face pressure if expected defence-budget increases are scaled back or delayed.

Energy producers: Exxon Mobil (XOM), Chevron (CVX) and ConocoPhillips (COP) could face earnings pressure if oil prices remain lower.

Risks to the view: Congress cannot unilaterally end the Iran conflict or reverse tariffs. Geopolitical escalation could push oil prices higher again, while defence spending could remain elevated because of broader national-security priorities. Lower oil prices caused by weakening global demand would also be less favourable for airlines and consumer stocks.

Investor takeaway: Markets could begin rewarding companies that benefit from lower energy costs rather than those positioned for further fiscal and geopolitical escalation. But the distinction between lower oil prices driven by increased supply and those driven by weaker demand remains critical.

## 4. Yield rotation: From financing pressures to valuation support

Lower Treasury yields could be another important consequence of the post-election repricing.

Political gridlock may constrain additional fiscal stimulus, while cheaper oil could ease inflation pressure. That combination could support rate-sensitive assets and improve financing conditions.

Property companies, utilities and gold miners offer different ways to gain exposure to this theme.

Gold’s recent rebound has been supported by a weaker dollar, lower yields, short covering and the broader market recovery. The longer-term outlook could remain constructive if lower energy prices eventually give the Federal Reserve greater flexibility to support growth.

### Potential beneficiaries

Prologis (PLD): Logistics property exposure, with valuations and financing economics potentially supported by lower yields.

Realty Income (O): Income-producing properties, with sensitivity to interest rates and funding costs.

Digital Realty Trust (DLR): Data-centre real estate exposure that could benefit from lower borrowing costs, although AI infrastructure risks remain.

NextEra Energy (NEE) and Duke Energy (DUK): Capital-intensive utilities that could benefit from lower financing costs.

Newmont (NEM) and Agnico Eagle Mines (AEM): Gold mining exposure, with potential benefits from stronger precious-metal prices.

Wheaton Precious Metals (WPM): Precious-metals streaming exposure, offering a different operating model from traditional miners.

### Stocks facing potential headwinds

Banks: JPMorgan Chase (JPM), Bank of America (BAC) and Wells Fargo (WFC) could face pressure on net interest income if falling yields compress lending margins, particularly if deposit costs adjust more slowly.

Life insurers: MetLife (MET) and Prudential Financial (PRU) could face pressure on reinvestment yields if interest rates remain lower for an extended period.

Risks to the view: Sticky inflation, renewed energy-price increases or fiscal uncertainty could push yields higher again. Lower yields driven by recession fears could hurt property demand and bank credit quality. Banks may also benefit from stronger loan demand or capital-market activity, while miners remain exposed to commodity prices and operating costs.

Investor takeaway: The direction of yields matters, but so does the reason they are falling. Lower yields alongside resilient growth could favour property and utilities, while a growth scare would create a more challenging backdrop.

## 5. Dollar rotation: From US concentration to global diversification

A weaker US dollar and lower Treasury yields could improve financial conditions outside the United States.

Emerging markets may benefit from reduced dollar funding pressure, stronger capital flows and potentially more supportive equity valuations.

Asia presents several opportunities. India could benefit from cheaper oil and domestic consumption, China offers selective technology and policy exposure, and Taiwan retains structural semiconductor advantages despite near-term AI spending concerns.

European equities could also attract interest as investors reassess their heavy exposure to US markets.

### Stocks and UCITS ETFs to watch

Alibaba (BABA / 9988.HK) and Tencent (0700.HK): Chinese technology businesses with domestic demand and AI exposure.

iShares MSCI India UCITS ETF (IIND): Broad Indian equity exposure.

iShares Core MSCI EM IMI UCITS ETF (EIMI): Diversified emerging-market equity exposure.

iShares Core MSCI Europe UCITS ETF (IMEU): Broad European equity exposure.

iShares MSCI China UCITS ETF (ICHN): Diversified Chinese equity exposure.

### Stocks facing potential headwinds

US-focused regional banks: Banks with limited international earnings exposure may benefit less directly from a weaker dollar and could face pressure if lower yields compress interest margins.

US-dominated equity portfolios: Concentrated exposure to large US technology stocks could lag if international market leadership broadens. This is primarily a portfolio concentration risk rather than a negative fundamental view on any individual company.

Risks to the view: Persistent US inflation or a hawkish Fed could support the dollar. Emerging markets remain vulnerable to capital outflows, domestic political risks and global growth shocks. India faces valuation risks, China continues to confront structural challenges, and Europe remains sensitive to weak growth and energy costs.

Investor takeaway: Diversification outside the US could become increasingly relevant, but investors should focus on regional fundamentals and valuations rather than relying on currency movements alone.

## What could derail the Blue Wave trade?

The post-election moves have created opportunities, but markets may be moving faster than the underlying policy changes.

Geopolitical escalation: Renewed tensions involving Iran could reverse the oil-price decline, revive inflation concerns and pressure airlines, consumer stocks and rate-sensitive sectors.

Policy gridlock: Democrats control Congress, but President Trump retains veto and executive powers. Healthcare, clean-energy and fiscal expectations may not translate into meaningful legislative change.

Sticky inflation or a hawkish Fed: Higher Treasury yields and a stronger dollar could challenge property, utilities, precious metals and emerging-market equities.

AI spending uncertainty: A broader slowdown in AI investment could affect data-centre operators, semiconductor companies, memory suppliers and hyperscalers. Conversely, resilient AI demand could lead to a recovery in infrastructure-related stocks.

Slower global growth: Falling oil prices and yields may reflect deteriorating demand rather than improving financial conditions. Earnings weakness could then overwhelm the benefits.

Crowded rotation trades: Rapid moves into healthcare, clean energy, travel or defensive sectors could leave valuations vulnerable if expectations become too optimistic.

## The bottom line: A rotation, not necessarily a retreat

The Blue Wave has changed the political equation, but the bigger investment story may be the broadening of market leadership.

AI remains an important structural growth theme, but investors may increasingly reward profitability over capital expenditure. Healthcare and clean energy could regain policy relevance, lower oil prices may benefit consumers more than energy producers, declining yields could support rate-sensitive assets, and a softer dollar may create opportunities outside the US.

The message for portfolios is not to abandon yesterday’s winners, but to ensure they are not the only drivers of tomorrow’s returns.

Investors may want to review where their portfolios are concentrated, which businesses depend on favourable policy outcomes, and whether their exposure is balanced across different earnings and macroeconomic drivers.

The next phase of the market may reward diversification, earnings resilience and selectivity more than simply adding to the most crowded trades.
