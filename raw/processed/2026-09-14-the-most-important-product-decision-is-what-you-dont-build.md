# The most important product decision is what you don't build

The hardest — and most valuable — product decisions involve removing or refusing to build things, not shipping more features.

Two recurring examples from financial services illustrate this: the "document hub" and the "notifications centre." Both start as simple requests and balloon into complex, costly platforms that ultimately serve the organisation more than the user.

A practical fix: show stakeholders running costs over years, not just build costs. That reframes the conversation.

For genuine communication needs, address each use case individually rather than constructing a one-size-fits-all platform — asking whether it "makes the boat go faster."

On deletion, Gerry McGovern found that removing 80–90% of site content improved sales and reduced support load. A Nature study shows people are cognitively biased toward adding rather than subtracting.

On AI and agents, the push back against "endless execution" enthusiasm: agents are better deployed for pruning and maintenance than for generating yet more features.

The Jobs 1997 product matrix closes the argument: four products, everything else cancelled — not as an end state, but as a focusing mechanism.
