# Shane Parrish on Mental Models, Decision Making, Charlie Munger, Farnam Street, And More

An interview with Shane Parrish, curator of the Farnam Street blog, on mental models, decision making, and lifelong learning.

Parrish started Farnam Street during his MBA out of frustration that the program emphasized regurgitating material over critical thinking. He began revisiting original research (like Kahneman and Tversky's papers) for his own learning, and the site grew into a community focused on continuous learning and applying mental models across disciplines. Farnam Street's mission: help people make better decisions, discover connections across disciplines, and explore how to live a good life.

On investing role models Buffett and Munger: their edge isn't inventing new ideas but taking other people's ideas (psychology, competitive strategy) and applying them more consistently and rigorously than the originators — in effect institutionalizing debiasing that even Kahneman himself admits he can't fully apply to his own decisions.

On reading: Parrish deliberately reduced his book count after realizing that reading 140+ books a year (racing to finish) meant less synthesis. "It's not about how many books you read but what you get out of the books you read." His technique: summarize each chapter in his own words, apply lessons by looking backward and forward, margin notes for connections, and letting a finished book sit before doing a final review pass.

On the Feynman Technique: explaining a concept on paper as if teaching someone with no background reveals gaps in understanding — if you can't explain it simply, you probably don't understand it as well as you think.

On multidisciplinary thinking: mental models are tools in a toolbox; having many (and knowing how to use them) improves decision quality broadly. Second-order thinking — "and then what?" — is highlighted as an underused discipline: many decisions stop at the first-order effect and never consider the ripple effects that follow (e.g., a cost-saving software purchase whose savings ultimately get competed away).

On investment process: improving decision-making incrementally compounds over time. Decision journals — documenting the variables considered and expected range of outcomes in advance — expose the reasoning process to challenge and feedback, which is more valuable than any single correct call.

On mental models generally: models are reductions, not reality itself ("all models are false but some are useful," quoting George Box). Core, durable models — social proof, incentives, margin of safety — apply broadly; domain-specific models should be layered on top depending on one's field.
