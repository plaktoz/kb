# Eating more ultra-processed foods may raise prostate cancer risk by 30%

Ultra-processed foods (UPFs) now supply nearly 60% of the calories consumed by adults in the United States. New research from Florida Atlantic University's Charles E. Schmidt College of Medicine suggests men who consume more UPFs may have an increased risk of developing prostate cancer, the most commonly diagnosed cancer in men and the second-leading cause of cancer death among men in the U.S.

The study, published in The American Journal of Medicine, analyzed nationally representative data from 17,024 U.S. men ages 18 and older who participated in NHANES between 2003 and 2023. Researchers analyzed two 24-hour dietary recalls per participant, classified foods using the NOVA system, and separated participants into four groups by share of daily calories from UPFs, from under 20% to over 44%.

Compared with men in the lowest UPF consumption group, those in the three higher intake groups had a 29% greater risk of prostate cancer; men in the two highest consumption groups had a 30% greater risk. After adjusting for age, race and ethnicity, smoking, and poverty status, the increased risk ranged from 24% to 31%. Men in the single highest intake group showed a possible 34% increase, though that result was not statistically significant, possibly reflecting the smaller number of cases in that group.

"Among adult men in this nationally representative U.S. population, those who consumed higher amounts of UPFs had significantly higher risks of the subsequent development of prostate cancer," said senior author Charles H. Hennekens, M.D. "These findings add to emerging evidence that UPFs have major adverse health effects and underscore the need for large-scale observational studies and randomized trials to adequately test the hypothesis."

Previous research has linked greater UPF consumption to higher risks of obesity, metabolic disease, cardiovascular disease and premature death. The authors compare the UPF evidence trajectory to tobacco, where decades passed between early evidence of harm and policies aimed at reducing use. The American Cancer Society estimates more than 333,000 men in the U.S. will be diagnosed with prostate cancer in 2026 and more than 36,000 will die from the disease.
