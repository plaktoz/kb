# Stanford scientists discover immune cells that explode like microscopic bombs

Stanford researchers have identified a previously unknown immune cell that destroys nearby cells by exploding, disappearing within minutes and leaving virtually no trace. The discovery was made in planarian flatworms, published in Cell, and the cells were named "ruptoblasts."

Postdoctoral researcher Chew Chai first noticed the cells while testing whether flatworms can distinguish their own tissue from another individual's. Fusing worms lengthwise with parts of different worms produced "Frankenstein" worms that rejected foreign tissue — resembling how a human immune system rejects a transplanted organ, but through a very different cellular mechanism: "It's this huge inflammatory response. Like there's a fire and an alarm goes off, and the cells just blow up," Chai said.

The hormone activin triggers the response: rising activin levels preceded chronic inflammation in fused worms, and injecting activin into healthy worms produced the same effect. Using live cell microscopy and flow cytometry, Chai found a small group of cells that burst open, released substances that killed nearby cells, and vanished within five minutes — a process the team named "ruptosis."

Ruptosis is far faster than other known explosive cell death: comparable mammalian or bacterial processes unfold over hours, while ruptosis happens within seconds to minutes, turning each ruptoblast into a highly localized weapon. Tested against E. coli bacteria, human kidney cells, and mouse blood cells, ruptoblasts destroyed all three target types, with damage staying tightly contained near the explosion rather than spreading.

Unlike T cells and neutrophils, which are blood cells produced in bone marrow, ruptoblasts are glandular cells that appear to intensify normal secretion systems, with a rapid calcium surge from the endoplasmic reticulum driving the explosion. Searching other species, researchers found ruptoblasts only in basal bilaterians like flatworms, suggesting the mechanism originated early in animal evolution — and may have been lost in vertebrates, which cannot easily repair the surrounding tissue damage the way flatworms' abundant stem cells can. Senior author Bo Wang said the findings show how much remains unknown about immune mechanisms in understudied organisms, and could offer clues for targeted treatments against infections and tumors.
