# 71% Of Workers Feel Pressure To Be 'Always On.' Is It Worth It?

A recent survey indicates 71% of U.S. employees feel pressured to be "always on" outside work hours, but 69% find it hasn't significantly boosted their careers. Only 8% attribute a promotion to it, despite spending 1.7 hours weekly on after-hours communications. While some perceive it as building manager trust or visibility, this constant availability often deteriorates work-life balance, heightens stress, and fuels burnout, contributing to a "resource-loss spiral." Many workers fear setting boundaries could jeopardize their career. The article advises that true career advancement should derive from value creation, not merely endless availability, encouraging employees to establish deliberate boundaries to safeguard their well-being and professional standing.

Answering emails at 2 a.m., constantly taking work calls at the dinner table and responding to urgent Slack messages while on vacation can feel like proof that you're fully committed to your job. A new Software Finder survey of 1,006 U.S. employees found that 71% feel pressure to stay reachable outside normal working hours. Yet 69% say being available after hours has not meaningfully improved their opportunities for career advancement.

The career payoff appears limited for most workers. Only 8% say after-hours availability directly contributed to a promotion and 12% credit it with a raise or bonus. Still, nearly half worry that setting boundaries could hurt their career. Meanwhile, the average worker spends 1.7 hours a week checking work communications after hours—the equivalent of more than two full workweeks a year.

Being responsive can sometimes strengthen manager trust or increase visibility with leadership. But if constant availability is hurting your work-life balance without leading to meaningful career advancement, it's worth questioning whether being "always on" is really helping you get ahead.

## Why Being Always On Can Seem Worth It

There are reasons employees keep making themselves available after hours. Thirty percent of workers said being reachable strengthened trust with their manager, and 22% said it increased their visibility with leadership. Those are real advantages, especially in workplaces where responsiveness is closely tied to how commitment is judged.

For some workers, that extra availability does appear to pay off. Among the subgroup who describe themselves as constantly available, 57% said it had improved their career, while 17% credited it with a promotion and 24% with a raise or bonus. But those findings also suggest the payoff isn't automatic. Being known as dependable can increase your visibility without necessarily changing your title, compensation or access to better opportunities.

## How Being Always On Can Affect Your Career Outlook

Constant work encroachment can affect more than your evenings and weekends. A 2026 study of 3,753 workers published in Humanities and Social Sciences Communications found that work-leisure imbalance was associated with higher job dissatisfaction. That dissatisfaction, in turn, was associated with a greater likelihood of perceiving limited career development prospects.

The pattern was especially pronounced among professional, technical and managerial workers. The researchers describe chronic work encroachment as part of a "resource-loss spiral," where ongoing demands are associated with job dissatisfaction and a more negative view of future career prospects.

## How Being Always On Erodes Work-Life Balance

The downside becomes harder to ignore when being always on starts eating into recovery time. In the survey, 40% of workers said it had worsened their work-life balance, 38% said it increased their stress and 32% said it worsened burnout. Nearly three-quarters also said they check work communications while on vacation.

A 2026 systematic review published in Frontiers in Psychology found a similar pattern across 17 studies. Extended work-related availability was generally associated with more work-family conflict and poorer health outcomes, including higher stress, burnout and reduced mental well-being. The researchers described constant connectivity as a "persistent boundary-crossing demand" that can interfere with recovery and blur the line between work and personal time.

## Why Employees Are Afraid To Set Work Boundaries

The pressure to stay available doesn't disappear just because the payoff is uncertain. Nearly half of workers in the Software Finder survey said they worry that setting boundaries around after-hours communication could hurt their career, including 11% who worry about it frequently.

That helps explain why so many employees keep answering messages long after the workday ends. For some, responsiveness has become part of how commitment is judged, especially in cultures where speed and accessibility are rewarded informally. Once being always on becomes the norm, not responding can start to feel like you're not meeting expectations.

## How To Set Work Boundaries Without Hurting Your Reputation

It is possible to be responsive while still setting healthy work boundaries. All it takes is a more deliberate approach to mapping out your availability and determining what requires your attention after hours. Doing so can help protect your work-life balance without requiring you to become completely unreachable.

The 2026 Frontiers in Psychology review found that job autonomy, schedule control and healthier boundary norms can reduce some of the strain associated with an always-on culture. The goal is to interrupt the pattern where every exception becomes an expectation and after-hours work steadily takes up more of the time you need to recover.

If after-hours communication is common in your workplace, be consistent about when you respond. You don't have to ignore every message, but you also don't have to train everyone around you to expect an immediate response at all hours.

## Career Advancement Requires More Than Constant Availability

If you want to know whether being available after hours is helping your career, look at what you're getting in return. Are you gaining more authority, better assignments or a clearer path to promotion? Or are you simply becoming the person everyone knows will answer at any hour?

Pay attention to what truly drives career advancement in your organization and whether your extra availability is contributing to those outcomes. If your time commitment keeps increasing while your title, compensation and opportunities stay the same, being dependable may be benefiting the company more than your career. Career advancement should come from the value you create, not from proving how many sleepless nights you sacrifice in the name of commitment.
