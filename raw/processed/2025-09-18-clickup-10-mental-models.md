# 10 Mental Models to Help You Make Better Decisions

Mental models are structured cognitive frameworks — simplified representations of reality — that let people process complex information and make informed decisions rather than relying on subconscious or biased snap judgments. Charlie Munger, Warren Buffett's partner and an early proponent, argued that facts only make sense once they hang together in a "latticework of theory," meaning multiple mental models must be consciously developed and applied.

## The ten models

1. **Bayes' theorem** — predicts the probability of an event using relevant historical data and situational factors rather than assumption, providing a mathematical alternative to biased guessing.
2. **Opportunity cost** — every decision has a cost measured in the value of the abandoned alternatives; evaluating it enables unbiased comparison between options.
3. **Redundancy** — keeping extra capacity of a critical resource (staff, equipment) as a buffer so a single point of failure doesn't derail an outcome.
4. **Backward chaining** — starting planning from the end goal and working backward through the steps required, which keeps focus on the outcome rather than getting lost in sequential detail.
5. **Pareto principle (80/20 rule)** — roughly 80% of effects come from 20% of causes, so resources should concentrate on the highest-value 20%.
6. **Law of diminishing returns** — returns on continued investment in one area eventually shrink toward zero or negative, signaling when to redirect effort toward new opportunities.
7. **Margin of safety** — building a buffer into plans (engineering tolerances, project deadlines, financial estimates) so efforts can absorb setbacks without failing outright.
8. **Confirmation bias** — the tendency to interpret situations in ways that support existing beliefs while discounting contradicting evidence; countered by intellectual humility and data-driven decision-making.
9. **Preferential attachment** — the tendency for resources to flow toward efforts already succeeding, amplifying their lead; the article recommends balancing this against the Pareto principle by still testing other opportunities before fully committing resources to current winners.
10. **Hanlon's razor** — "never attribute to malice what could be explained by carelessness," a check against over-interpreting others' inaction (e.g., a stalled sales lead) as intentional or personal.
