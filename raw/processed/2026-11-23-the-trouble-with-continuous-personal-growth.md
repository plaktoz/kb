# The Trouble With Continuous Personal Growth

For many people, continual growth doesn’t come naturally. Lots of things get in the way, such as the difficulty of finding time to work on new skills and a tendency to build on what you do well rather than try something new. Data from a self-assessment taken by 550 people reveals that there are two obstacles in particular: finding time to pause and reflect, and not encountering enough unfamiliar roles and situations. Centering around these two traits makes renewal and continued adaptation possible.

For four decades, I have studied work and working lives. During that time, I have watched assumptions that once seemed secure begin to unravel: that work happens in one location; that expertise accumulates steadily; that a career unfolds within one organization; that working life has a beginning, middle, and end.

The pandemic, longer lives, and even artificial intelligence have each accelerated changes. In so doing, they have made one capacity more important than ever: the ability to keep adapting. Not simply by working harder or acquiring the next skill, but by renewing how we think, learn, and contribute over time.

But cultivating the ability to embrace renewal doesn’t always come easily. That is why I have been investigating the idea of a working life being akin to a woven object. As I explore in my new book, Living the 100-Year Life: How to Build a Career That Lasts and a Life You Love, our lives are held together by eight “threads” that support both work productivity and personal nurture. Four of those threads concern how we contribute: They are our abilities for Mastery, Knowing, Cooperation, and Amplification. The other four threads are abilities that sustain us over time: Friendship, Intimacy, Calm, and Adventure. The point is not to make every thread equally strong but to notice which are supporting us, which need rejuvenation, and which may be becoming fragile.

## Three Insights Into Responding to Change

In advance of the book’s release, I posted a thread strength diagnostic online at the book’s website. The diagnostic has been completed by more than 550 people to self-assess the strengths and weaknesses of the eight threads in their own lives. The data in those 550 responses offers a modest but revealing glimpse of how people are responding to their new realities and which of their own abilities are, by their own judgment, sustaining them or failing them.

First Insight: We Are Adapting

The first part of the story is encouraging. Three threads stand out as particularly strong among the diagnostic takers: Mastery, which is our capacity to deepen expertise and build distinctive capabilities; Knowing, our capacity to understand ourselves, others, and the changing world around us; and Amplification, which is how we use technologies such as AI to extend our thinking and judgment.

Their self-reported strengths in these threads suggest that many people are not simply reacting to change. They are building expertise, reflecting on the direction of their lives, and learning how to work intelligently alongside new technologies and in modern workplace configurations, such as hybrid schedules.

Three-quarters of self-assessors said they are experimenting with new tools to analyze information, test ideas, and make decisions.

The details of the data give a clearer sense of this:

In terms of Mastery, 80% said they feel absorbed when deepening their expertise, 77% said they believe they have developed distinctive capabilities that bring value, and 71% said they can see how their skills might develop further.

For the thread of Knowing, 89% said they are deeply curious about themselves and the world, 91% reported reflecting on the experiences shaping their values and who they are becoming, and 83% said they are learning to engage with perspectives different from their own.

For the thread of Amplification, 83% reported that they are developing the judgment to distinguish between which types of work can be supported by technology and which types rely more on human judgment. Over three-quarters — 77% — said they are experimenting with new tools to analyze information, test ideas, and make decisions.

Taken together, these are not the responses of people blind to change or waiting to be told what to do.

Second Insight: Capability Is Not the Same as Renewal

Many of us have curiosity, judgment, and valuable expertise. But insight does not automatically become a practice, and experimentation does not automatically become transformation.

Threads that appear to be strong can still be losing vitality. The diagnostic’s results suggest that many people are taking steps toward building capability without consistently renewing it. Regarding Mastery, 72% said they make intentional efforts to develop their skills but not yet as a consistent habit; 39% reported that their development has slowed even though they are still performing well; and 38% said they tend to build on what they already do well rather than stretch into something new.

The same pattern appears in Knowing. Over a third of respondents, 37%, said they intend to reflect on their values and direction but often do not follow through. In regard to Amplification, 74% said their use of technology to support thinking and decision-making is inconsistent. Almost half said they default to easy or habitual uses rather than using technology to stretch their thinking.

Third Insight: There Are Two Obstacles to Renewal

The diagnostic cannot tell us precisely why finding the consistency and follow-through that renewal requires is difficult. But the data points toward two threads that play distinctive roles in a long working life: Calm, which gives us the steadiness and space to notice what experience is teaching us, and Adventure, which takes us beyond familiar roles, routines, and assumptions.

Calm helps us pause and reflect. Adventure helps us encounter what is new. When either is underdeveloped, it becomes harder to turn established capability into continued growth and more difficult to encounter the fresh insight that can evolve into practice.

Across the sample of diagnostic takers, Calm was the weakest of the eight threads; there was deep agreement with the statement “I know what helps me sustain my energy, but I often don’t act on it.” For 18% of respondents, their answers around Calm ranked the thread as being “fragile” for them, meaning it is under strain or underdeveloped and may need deliberate care. This may help explain why insight does not always turn into action: People need a certain degree of calm for change to settle in. When working lives are organized around constant response, there is little time or steadiness to reflect on what experience is teaching us. It can feel impossible to notice when established expertise is becoming too narrow or to decide what to do differently.

Reports around the Adventure thread point to a second obstacle. Few people described Adventure in a way that would designate it as fragile in their lives, but 73% reported that the thread needs renewal — the highest proportion of any thread. To me, this says that while we are functioning well in the present, many of us also recognize that our working lives contain too little that is genuinely unfamiliar. There is a need to embrace adventure, whether it be a project beyond our usual field, a work period or experience where we are no longer the expert, or a willingness to make a lateral move. These are all valuable circumstances to proactively seek out.

These findings do not provide a complete explanation of why we can be working hard but still have a nagging feeling that we’re stuck. But they suggest that renewal may be held back by two absences: too little space to reflect, and too little opportunity to step beyond what is already known and valued.

## Building Renewal Into a Working Life

Over the years that I have studied work, I have seen working lives reshaped in ways few of us anticipated: by globalization and new technologies, by the pandemic, by artificial intelligence, and by longer lives. There will be more change to come, some of it as unexpected as the pandemic itself.

That is why renewal matters. Each of us needs to keep asking: What am I still learning? What am I doing simply because I am already good at it? What small experience might open up a different possibility?

Renewal cannot rest with individuals alone. Organizations have a responsibility to make it possible. That means protecting time for reflection, valuing lateral moves and unfamiliar projects, and ensuring that technology expands people’s judgment rather than simply increasing their output.

None of us can know exactly how work will change over the coming decades. But we can build working lives that balance capability with renewal, contributing what we have learned while continuing to make room for reflection, curiosity, and new experiences. A long working life should not be a test of how long we can continue doing what we already know. It can be a continuing process of becoming.
