# How an MIT research project became a global programming language

A group of researchers began exchanging frustrated emails around 2009 about the limitations of programming languages built for scientists — tools that were rigid, slow, and required complete rewrites to improve performance. Those conversations led to a research project at MIT aimed at building a better solution: Julia, a high-performance, easy-to-use language designed for scientific research, data analysis, and modeling complex systems.

The project grew into the Julia Lab at MIT, which eventually became the company JuliaHub. Today, Julia is free and open-source with over 1 million users across thousands of companies and universities worldwide. It has been applied to an extraordinary range of problems — from modeling semiconductor behavior and neural networks to imaging black holes.

Julia's core technical advantage is "just-in-time compilation," which adapts code compilation based on data types, making it faster and more flexible than comparable numerical programming languages.

JuliaHub co-founder and CEO Viral Shah describes the founding motivation: scientists and engineers aren't programmers, and building multidisciplinary applications is difficult. The team wanted a language that let researchers "express their ideas at a high level and also get great software performance."

The co-founders include Shah, MIT mathematics professor Alan Edelman, Jeff Bezanson (SM '12, PhD '15), and former MIT research scientist Stefan Karpinski. They announced Julia via a blog post in 2012 and were surprised by the immediate interest from other researchers who shared their frustrations.

Julia has since been used to accelerate Moderna's Covid-19 vaccine development, create an aircraft collision-avoidance system that ran roughly 50 times faster than its Python predecessor, and help Meta engineers develop an improved audio codec for WhatsApp's billions of users.

In 2025, JuliaHub launched Dyad, an AI platform to help engineering teams develop complex physical systems such as rockets, heat pumps, and satellites. Dyad 3.0, released in April 2026, enables autonomous AI agents to work through physics simulations, safety analyses, and quality controls. A key feature is its physics compiler, which enforces physical laws — something general AI systems often fail to do. Shah says this capability could reduce product engineering design times "by orders of magnitude, leading to months of work being accomplished in hours."

Edelman, who teaches a Julia course drawing students from nearly every MIT department, notes that students regularly arrive already using the language. He observes that Julia's design encourages more generalized problem-solving, and that users often find it genuinely enjoyable to work with — not just fast.
