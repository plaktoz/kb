# 4 Approaches to Keep Students from Offloading Their Thinking to AI

One in 10 American teenagers say they complete all or most of their schoolwork with the help of artificial intelligence chatbots. These are the findings of a recent survey by the Pew Research Center among students aged 13–17.

The output generated by AI can be so convincing that teachers are not always able to distinguish it from work written by a human. In a study conducted among nearly 300 teachers, experienced staff correctly identified AI-generated essays in only 37.8% of cases. This study was carried out two years ago. Imagine how much easier it has become for students to tailor their answers to the required tone and format of an assignment using the latest AI models.

If a student can delegate to a neural network the intellectual work that the assignment was intended to encourage, the task itself becomes a mere formality. But I’m convinced that banning the use of AI isn’t the solution. Even when the answer is available instantly, an assignment can be structured in such a way that the students still have to do the bulk of the work themselves.

Below are four approaches to designing this kind of assignment that my curriculum team and I have developed for the network of schools I founded.

## Make the lesson topic more personal for the student

Every teacher knows this: If students are not interested in the topic, then they will only learn enough to get a passing score on a test. And if you can get any answer within a second from an AI, then why go deeper?

Therefore, I always encourage our teachers to frame the lesson topic around the student; I call this technique “placing the student inside the story.”

AI can easily answer “Why did Caesar cross the Rubicon?” But it can’t deal with a task in which the student is the protagonist: “Imagine you were in Caesar’s shoes. What would you do?” To answer this question correctly, the student will have to work out the situation himself: think about what was happening and what each option would lead to.

AI could also answer on Caesar’s behalf, but then the teacher can ask the student to explain the reasoning behind their decision.

## Add an oral presentation to the assignment

For decades, the main way to assess how a student thinks has been through essays; it was believed that by developing an argument on paper, they demonstrate their reasoning and understanding. But, as I mentioned at the start, AI has made great strides in essay writing.

This does not mean I am suggesting we ban AI while carrying out a task. It can be useful — for example, to draw up a plan or gather material. However, in my opinion, real learning takes place during discussions and presentations, when students explain their conclusions aloud.

This is where tricky questions come in handy. For example: Why did you use this argument, or which fact had the greatest influence on your conclusion? When answering such questions, the student is not simply repeating material they have learnt, but is linking facts and arguments, drawing on memory.

The aim of the oral defense is not to catch students using AI, but to give them the opportunity to demonstrate they understand their own work.

Since students have been using AI to prepare for class, I’ve noticed time and again that those who worked through the material on their own answer the teacher’s or the class’s questions with ease, whereas those who simply read back the generated text find it noticeably more difficult.

## Creating something that can be used

Previously, in our programming classes, students started with the simplest practice programs: For example, they would write a few lines of code to display the phrase Hello, World! on the screen. Such tasks introduced students to the programming language, but it took quite a while for them to get to something that could actually be used. What’s more, writing code can easily be delegated to AI.

We’ve changed the assignment: Now students use AI to create a working prototype, such as a video service with several features. I’ve noticed that in this format, they start adding things to their product that nobody asked for, simply because they want their creation to work better.

This is where I see one of the most interesting possibilities for generative AI in education. Now students can produce a first version more quickly and learn by developing it further. And, most importantly, basic skills do not become any less important — they now have a clear purpose. Students are more likely to study a new subject not because it is on the syllabus and is compulsory, but because without it they will not be able to bring their idea to fruition.

## Setting micro-tasks during the lesson

Instead of one large task, which is easy to delegate to AI, teachers can set lots of short tasks during the lesson. A small task relating to what is happening in the lesson is too fiddly to give to AI. It’s easier to think it through yourself than to explain the context to AI.

For example, you could ask a student to suggest the next step in solving a problem, to respond to a classmate’s argument and say whether she agrees or if there is a better option. A student can also be asked to predict the outcome of an experiment, or, after a new fact comes up, decide whether the initial answer still holds. Each of these questions requires a modest but independent intellectual effort.

Such micro-tasks do not make the use of AI impossible — nor is that the aim. They prevent students from handing all the work over to the machine and disengaging from the process.

No matter how technology develops, one thing will always be up to us. Coming up with a question that children will want to answer for themselves. A machine can suggest anything, but only a person can still spark a student’s interest.
