# Fed's Warsh under pressure to clarify message in highly anticipated Jackson Hole speech

Federal Reserve chairman Kevin Warsh made clear four weeks ago that he wants to use his speech at the Kansas City Fed's annual symposium in Jackson Hole, Wyoming, to take on big, lofty ideas rather than tactical details of near-term policy meetings. Events have not cooperated.

Warsh is under intense pressure to give a clearer message Friday at 10am ET than he has so far on the current inflation landscape, the prospects for near-term interest rate increases, and the relationship between the Fed and the Treasury. Markets are skittish, the Treasury has undertaken controversial interventions in bond and currency markets, and investors have grown restless with Warsh's tendency toward vague, high-altitude rhetoric.

Traditionally, the Fed chair uses the Jackson Hole speech to deliver a particularly important and long-range message — a notion Warsh has endorsed. But it will be hard for him to stay so high-altitude given extraordinary cross-pressures in markets since his late-July press conference: after that FOMC meeting, the bond market sold off, sending long-term rates soaring, with commentators complaining Warsh seemed unwilling to back his promises of price stability with concrete steps. The U.S. Treasury has twice intervened in global markets, first using a Fed facility to prop up the Japanese yen, then acting to support prices of long-term Treasury bonds — raising serious questions about the Fed's willingness to raise rates if needed and its role in cooperating with the Treasury on future market interventions.

"To regain market confidence we think Warsh will have to bluntly state that the FOMC will raise policy rates if inflation as measured by core PCE does not move steadily downwards," Steve Englander and John Davies at Standard Chartered Bank wrote in a note.

Warsh is determined not to offer "forward guidance" about future Fed moves, but economists and traders are thirsting for more concrete engagement with the moment's essential debates: does he believe inflation that has reaccelerated over 2026 is temporary, or does the Fed need to raise rates to finally hit its 2% target after six consecutive years of overshoot? And is the Fed willing to cooperate further with the Treasury on bond and currency market interventions, and what are the limits?

Before becoming chairman, Warsh spoke of crafting a new Treasury-Fed accord updating the 1951 agreement that clarified responsibilities between the two institutions on bond issuance and interest rates — now would be an opportune time to start filling in details, as the Treasury undertakes more interventionist policies. With the world's bond markets wobbling, Warsh's credibility is on the line in an early test of his leadership.
