# Apps, Agents, and Aggregation

The article opens with Steve Jobs' famous iPhone introduction, describing "three revolutionary products" combined into one device, and asks whether readers are truly grasping today's parallel shift: that AI-driven messaging and natural interfaces are replacing traditional computing, and that "pre-built UI — write once, run everywhere, for everyone — is dead."

## Messaging and Jobs to Be Done

Thompson revisits his 2014 thesis that messaging was mobile's "killer app," published just before Facebook acquired WhatsApp. He contrasts this with Zuckerberg's earlier, less successful "Facebook Home" concept, which tried to make Facebook the primary interface on Android devices. Thompson had rebutted this in a 2013 piece arguing that phones succeed because they serve many discrete "jobs to be done," not just social connection — illustrated by his own home screen having only four "people" apps out of 151 total. Today that number has grown to 689 apps, though the core set (messaging, social media, and now AI chatbots) has stayed consistent.

## A Computer for AI

Thompson describes building a custom app via Meta's "Muse" agent, which organized his saved Instagram recipes. He emphasizes that agents work by pairing AI with actual deterministic computers — each Muse user reportedly gets a virtual machine with real computing specs. This, he argues, is what makes agents practically usable for average people, not just developers.

## From Discovery to Inspiration

The recipe app exemplifies "the infinite app" — UI generated on demand for a single user's need, disposable because creation is now cheap. Thompson connects this to his earlier writing on generative UI and argues Meta's hardware (glasses, devices) exist primarily as "Muse delivery mechanisms." He argues the internet's old problem of discovery is being replaced by a new scarcity: human "volition" or inspiration to act, with agents becoming the new gatekeepers between users and every underlying app or service.

## A Platform for Aggregation

Thompson examines Microsoft's newly unveiled Copilot "super app," which bundles chat, coding, and a persistent enterprise agent called "Autopilot." He quotes Satya Nadella's framing of Copilot as a "new OS for work." Thompson connects this to his 2020 argument that Teams functioned as Microsoft's "OS for SaaS," suggesting Copilot is a natural evolution — an attempt to own the entire interface layer and force other software to integrate on Microsoft's terms.

## The Distributors

Thompson closes by predicting that once people experience agent-driven computing, traditional apps and enterprise UI will feel outdated. He references Salesforce's Dreamforce strategy of charging extra to integrate with agents like Claude, calling it audacious given the trajectory toward computer-using AI. The critical competitive battle, he argues, will be over which company becomes users' primary agent — since agents gain value (and lock-in) through accumulated context and access. Thompson concludes that Meta and Microsoft hold early advantages due to their existing massive distribution among consumers and enterprise employees, respectively.
