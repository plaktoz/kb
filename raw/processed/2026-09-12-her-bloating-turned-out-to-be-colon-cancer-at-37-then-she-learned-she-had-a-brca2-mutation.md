# Her Bloating Turned Out to Be Colon Cancer at 37. Then She Learned She Had a BRCA2 Mutation

Marisa Stachelski was 37 when she began experiencing worsening GI issues in summer 2022, including severe bloating she described as looking "almost like a five-months-pregnant kind of look." After dietary changes failed to help, she developed pain so severe she was "keeled over" on her couch.

A dietitian-ordered stool test revealed traces of blood, which Stachelski then began visibly noticing herself. A gastroenterologist performed a colonoscopy and found a cancerous polyp. Due to holiday timing, she waited weeks for oncology follow-up — spending that time wrapping gifts for her young children while uncertain about her future.

In early 2023, she received a stage 1 colon cancer diagnosis. Surgery removed the tumor, and because it was caught early, no further treatment was needed.

**Genetic Testing Reveals BRCA2 Mutation**

Her oncologist recommended genetic testing, which Stachelski viewed as routine. Two weeks later, she learned she carried a BRCA2 mutation — significantly elevating lifetime risk for breast, ovarian, fallopian tube, pancreatic, and prostate cancers. She had no known family history of breast or ovarian cancer on her mother's side.

Her father had died of pancreatic cancer 14 years earlier but was never genetically tested. After reaching out to paternal cousins, she discovered one her age had been diagnosed with stage 3 colon cancer just two months prior.

Sara Pirzadeh-Miller, director of the Cancer Genetics program at UT Southwestern's Simmons Cancer Center, noted that genetic testing should be considered for people with early-onset cancers, multiple cancers, strong family history, or known hereditary mutations. She emphasized that "a genetic counselor can help determine whether testing is appropriate."

Pirzadeh-Miller also clarified that BRCA2 mutations alone don't alter colon cancer screening recommendations — personal and family history of colon cancer remain the primary factors.

**Risk-Reduction Decisions**

After three months of research, Stachelski decided ongoing surveillance would be too stressful, saying she "couldn't live life on six-month increments." In June 2023, she underwent a nipple-sparing double mastectomy requiring three procedures.

Rather than a full hysterectomy, she enrolled in a Mount Sinai clinical trial in September 2024 focused on emerging research suggesting ovarian cancers may originate in the fallopian tubes. She had her fallopian tubes removed while delaying ovary removal to avoid early menopause at nearly 38. Current guidelines indicate she should have her ovaries removed by age 45.

**Ongoing Monitoring**

Seven months after her colon cancer diagnosis, precancerous stomach tissue was found and removed. She later learned her paternal grandmother had died of stomach cancer. She now has annual follow-ups with breast and plastic surgeons, biennial colonoscopies and endoscopies, annual dermatology screenings for melanoma, and is enrolling in a pancreatic cancer screening trial.

Stachelski relies on talk therapy to process her experiences, including grief over lost fertility and body changes.

**Advocacy and Outreach**

During her diagnosis, Stachelski began documenting her journey on Instagram. A pivotal moment came when a high school acquaintance told her the posts inspired her to get a mammogram, which detected early-stage breast cancer. Her husband suggested a podcast; her first episode, recorded at her kitchen counter, received 110 downloads within a day.

Launched in August 2025, her podcast *BRCA and Beyond* features medical experts, survivors, and previvors. She now works full-time on the podcast and partners with organizations including BRCA Strong, Lynch Syndrome Awareness, the National Society of Genetic Counselors, and FORCE.

"The cancer risk took my fertility, but I created my own baby," she said of the podcast.
