# Competitive Intelligence Analysis in the AI Era

## The Data Exhaustion Problem

Every morning, thousands of analysts across finance, consulting, and corporate strategy wake up and do the same thing.

They pull the same earnings transcripts from the same providers. They scrape the same news feeds. They query the same government databases. They subscribe to the same alternative data vendors.

Then they feed all of it into AI models and call the output “analysis.” The models themselves are not that different to each other.

Here’s the problem: when everyone is eating from the same buffet, nobody leaves with a unique meal.

## How We Got Here

The data vendor ecosystem was built for a world where processing was the bottleneck. It made perfect sense. Collecting raw data was relatively easy; making sense of it was hard. So an entire industry emerged to package, clean, and distribute information at scale. Bloomberg terminals. Refinitiv feeds. Crunchbase. PitchBook. Statista. S&P Capital IQ. Thousands of people hired to crunch data, working at specialised providers, each carving out a slice of the information market.

For decades the system worked. Access to these platforms was expensive enough to create tiers. If you could afford Bloomberg, you had an advantage over someone who couldn’t. In most cases the data itself wasn’t exclusive, and even if it were, it was only for a short amount of time, until the original news propagated through the wire. The speed and format of delivery though, created an edge.

AI collapsed that entire logic. When a model can ingest, parse, and synthesise data in seconds rather than hours, the processing advantage disappears. What remains is the data itself. And if the data is the same data everyone else has, the advantage is zero.

## The Crowding Effect in Competitive Intelligence Analysis

There’s a well-documented phenomenon in quantitative finance called factor crowding. When too many funds discover the same signal and trade on it, the signal degrades. The profit opportunity gets arbitraged away precisely because everyone sees the same thing at the same time.

We’re seeing the analytical equivalent of factor crowding across industries. AI has made it trivially easy to extract patterns from standard data sets. But when every firm’s AI is extracting the same patterns from the same data, the “insight” becomes consensus. And consensus, by definition, is not an edge.

Consider competitive intelligence. A consulting firm feeds public earnings calls, SEC filings, and news articles into their AI platform and produces a competitive landscape report. Their client receives it on Monday. By Tuesday, three other firms have produced near-identical reports for competing clients, using the same inputs and similar models. By Wednesday, all competitors have the same information. This was actually the situation a few months ago; today, one needs to replace Monday, Tuesday and Wednesday with 08:00, 08:30 and 09:00 of the same day.

Clients pay for differentiated insight and receive a commodity.

## Secondary Data Is a Depreciating Asset

I use the term “secondary data” deliberately. It’s information that has already been collected, processed, and distributed by someone else. By the time you receive it, it has already passed through multiple hands. Its informational value has been partially extracted at each step.

This doesn’t mean secondary data is useless. It’s essential for baseline understanding. You need to know what the consensus view is before you can develop a differentiated one. But relying on it exclusively is like trying to win a race using only the same car, engine, and tyres as everyone else. You’re competing entirely on driver skill, and even that gap narrows when AI is the driver.

The depreciation curve for secondary data is accelerating. Information that once held its value for weeks or months now becomes consensus within hours. AI agents monitoring the same feeds, extracting the same signals, distributing the same summaries; the cycle compresses until the time between data availability and consensus convergence approaches zero.

## Where the Pressure Builds

Data vendors are starting to feel this. When clients realise that everyone has access to the same information, the willingness to pay premium prices erodes. Why pay six figures for a data feed that your competitor also has, when the outputs you generate from it are functionally identical?

And no, raising prices and blocking LLM access to your content cannot be justified unless your secondary data content is truly unique and significant. Even then, most search-enabled LLMs are smart enough to search for similar content and fill-in the gaps.

The vendors who will thrive are the ones who can credibly claim exclusivity. Not exclusivity of format or delivery speed, as those advantages are gone, but exclusivity of content. Information that originates with them. Data collected through proprietary methods. Intelligence gathered at the source rather than repackaged from public records.

The rest face a race to the bottom on pricing, or irrelevance.

## The Implication for Strategy

If your organisation’s insights (whether market research or competitive intelligence, it doesn’t really matter at this point…) function is built primarily on secondary data sources, it’s time for a serious conversation about what happens next. Not because those sources are bad, but because they’re shared.

The strategic question is straightforward: where does your unique informational advantage come from? If the answer is “the same platforms and feeds that our competitors subscribe to,” then your AI implementation, however sophisticated, is producing consensus outputs dressed up as proprietary insight.
