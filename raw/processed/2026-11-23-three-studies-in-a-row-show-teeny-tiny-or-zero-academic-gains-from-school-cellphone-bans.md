# Three studies in a row show teeny-tiny or zero academic gains from school cellphone bans

Since 2023, cellphone use during the school day has been banned or restricted in at least 40 states and the District of Columbia, in the hope that reducing digital distractions would help students focus on their lessons.

So how’s it working?

Last week researchers at Harvard University released the third major study of cellphone limits in U.S. schools and, like the two before it, it found remarkably little academic benefit. Media reports trumpeted the study’s findings of gains in student achievement as evidence of real progress. But read the fine print: Students’ combined math and reading scores, as measured by MAP tests, rose by only an extra 0.0087 of a standard deviation.

Readers, I try to avoid talking about abstract statistical units, but in this case, they’re important because they show just how teeny-tiny the academic gains were. The estimated improvement is less than one-hundredth of a standard deviation, a very small shift in student performance. In graduate school, I was taught that even an improvement of one-tenth of a standard deviation — more than 10 times the size of this effect — is quite small.

But the Harvard researchers translated that 0.0087 improvement into one to two weeks of additional learning a year. They characterized the gains as “modest” and said they could add up over time and help students recover half of the learning losses that accumulated between 2013 and 2020, before student performance took a steeper nosedive during the pandemic.

The tiny gain was found after comparing student achievement in schools where students had to put their phones in locked pouches with performance in similar schools that didn’t impose such strict bans.

In my interview with Tom Kane, a Harvard economist and lead author of the report, he made the case that even tiny improvements matter. He called the pouches, which cost only about $25, a better “bang for the buck” than tutoring programs that might raise math or reading skills by a lot more, but cost thousands of dollars per student.

An earlier national study, released in April 2026, reached a less optimistic conclusion. Researchers from Stanford, Duke, the University of Pennsylvania and the University of Michigan found little to no academic improvement in schools that adopted cellphone pouches. Like the Harvard researchers, they compared schools that required students to lock their phones away with similar schools that didn’t. But unlike the Harvard team, they didn’t have individual student test scores and instead relied on average scores for each school.

Three major U.S. studies on academic performance after cellphone restrictions

The pouches did make a difference. Cellphone use declined more in schools that adopted them, and teachers reported that students were on their phones less during class. Suspensions initially increased as the new cellphone rules went into effect, but disciplinary incidents subsided after the first year.

The first of the three studies, released in October 2025, also found a very small academic benefit, though not nearly as small as the Harvard study. It took place in one large Florida district, where schools with higher cellphone use before restrictions saw average test scores increase by 0.6 of a percentile point more than the scores of schools with lower prior cellphone use. That’s the equivalent of moving a student from the 50th to not quite the 51st percentile. To many, that boost would be hardly noticeable — a rounding error.

On the strength of this number, David Figlio, an economist at the University of Rochester and an author of the Florida study, declared that the bans were “helping kids” and that it was a rare case in public policy where the “data back up the hunches.”

## Critics argue about small numbers

Researchers disagree about that. And their Ivory Tower debate is now taking place in the thousandths of a standard deviation.

Tom Dee, a Stanford economist and an author of the earlier national study, pointed out that the Harvard team’s small positive findings vanished when they themselves crunched the numbers a different way. The Harvard paper reported an even smaller estimate, 0.0068 standard deviations, when researchers used their originally planned methodology. That result was not statistically significant, meaning the researchers could not rule out the possibility that the academic gains were actually zero.

Dee also noted that the Harvard researchers’ own test of whether their tiny gains were compounding did not pass statistical muster; they could not establish that the academic benefits were continuing to grow in the first couple years after a cellphone ban went into effect.

Brendan Bartanen, an associate professor of education and public policy at the University of Virginia, publicly criticized his peers. “In any reasonable world, the effect sizes here are basically zero. This intervention did not move student achievement in any meaningful way (it could still be a good policy!),” Bartanen wrote on Bluesky.

“We need to stop doing this as researchers,” he wrote, referring to interpretations that go “beyond (often well beyond) what our results can actually support.” It’s a “pernicious problem,” he added, that most researchers have engaged in.

Other researchers piled on, too. “The effect size is not modest,” wrote Candice Odgers, a professor of psychology at the University of California, Irvine, on LinkedIn. “It is negligible by conventional standards and the reporting of this as a massive victory for phone bans on children’s educational outcomes is …well, strange.”

“I am just worried we will declare victory without actually getting a win for kids,” added Odgers.

In response to the criticism, the Harvard research team justified the need to adjust their methodology because of missing data. But they agreed that it will take more time to see if the annual improvements will indeed add up to substantial learning gains. They stood by their assertion that small gains for students still count.

“We do not think our interpretation goes beyond what our results can support,” the authors of the Harvard study wrote me by email.

Even Stanford’s Dee, whose study found essentially no academic benefits from cellphone bans, wants the bans to continue. He told me he still finds the hypothesis that cellphones are distracting and harm children’s attention spans “compelling.” He hopes that as schools get better at implementing bans, students might see larger benefits. As long as students can still chat through Google’s educational apps or can access TikTok and Instagram on personal laptops and tablets they bring to school, the classroom distractions remain.

## Researchers plead for social media data

Another possibility is that the academic harm from cellphones isn’t primarily driven by their use during the school day. It may be all the other hours students spend scrolling through videos, messaging and playing games that reduce their attention spans, undermine their motivation to read and cut into their sleep.

Both Dee of Stanford and Kane of Harvard called upon social media companies to share their data with researchers so they can better investigate whether cellphone use is harming academic achievement, and which activities and hours matter most.

“The social media platforms, they know how much time kids are spending on Snapchat or watching YouTube or TikTok during the day and the night,” Kane said. “We could answer these questions about in-school and out-of-school use if we had the data.”

For now, many researchers are urging schools to stick with the bans anyway. But there’s a difference between believing a policy is worth continuing to try and claiming that the evidence shows it works.
