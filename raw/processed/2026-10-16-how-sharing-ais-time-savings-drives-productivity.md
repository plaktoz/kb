# How Sharing AI's Time Savings Drives Productivity

In 1926, Ford Motor Company publicly announced its five-day, 40-hour workweek without reducing weekly pay. Workers had fought for that formula long before Ford's announcement, but the anniversary offers a reason to revisit a century-old arrangement that has proven remarkably durable.

At Clark University, where I lead fundraising and alumni engagement, we tested whether giving employees a stake in productivity gains could help produce those gains in the first place. Employees would share in the gains through time off, while the university would see stronger results. The larger question for leaders is whether sharing productivity gains with employees can create a stronger incentive to find gains in the first place.

When an employee uses AI to finish a day's work in an afternoon, for example, a manager can usually give them more to do. That makes sense for the organization, but it creates an incentive problem for the employee: If greater efficiency simply produces more work, why invest the effort required to find the next efficiency?

Ron Shaich's book, Know What Matters, helped shape our thinking about incentives. Under his leadership, Panera developed a Joint Venture Partnership Program, which he described as "a kind of company-owned franchise model that made store managers and assistant managers part-owners of the cafes they managed." For leaders, the principle is broader than a four-day week: If employees help create productivity gains, consider whether they should share in some of the value created.

Microsoft Japan provided another precedent. In August 2019, it gave regular employees five paid Fridays off while encouraging shorter meetings and greater use of collaboration tools. Working days fell by 25.4% compared with August 2018, while reported labor productivity (sales per employee) was 39.9% higher. Microsoft later clarified that the productivity figure reflected multiple factors and could not be attributed to the challenge alone. Microsoft also reported that the challenge itself encouraged employees to find efficiencies so they could make more time for learning and rest.

After two years of productivity gains, our progress had plateaued despite process changes and new AI tools.

We put that proposition to the test within our division. From August 1 through September 19, we completed a 50-day experiment called "Project Rouge," named after Ford Motor Company's River Rouge complex. The formula was 100% of pay, 80% of traditional working hours and a target of at least 120% of baseline output.

Employees worked 32 hours across four days, with days off staggered to preserve Monday-through-Friday coverage. They were not expected to make up the eight hours elsewhere. Fewer than 10% worked additional hours, and we excluded activity attributable to those additional hours so it would not inflate the gains we reported.

Every measure improved by more than 20%, while employees worked approximately 20% fewer scheduled hours, with quality and service maintained or improved. Donor meetings increased by nearly 40%, while phone calls, handwritten notes and email outreach to donors nearly doubled. Equally important, average gift-processing time on the operations side decreased by 22%.

But we cannot attribute those gains to AI, the four-day schedule or any single intervention. We changed several variables at once, including processes, technology and incentives. Project Rouge was designed to test the combination, not to prove that any one element caused the result.

In an anonymous survey at the end of the experiment, 31 of 32 employees responded, a 96% response rate. Every respondent wanted Project Rouge to continue beyond its first phase. Eighty-one percent also wanted us to deploy more AI resources, while the remaining 19% were unsure.

For leaders considering a similar experiment, three conditions from our experiment may be especially relevant:

First, Clark President David Fithian often asks us to do "fewer things supremely well." An unused report generated in 30 seconds is still an unused report, so we began by eliminating unnecessary meetings, reports and emails. Before asking where AI can make work faster, leaders should first ask what work should disappear altogether.

Then we redesigned what remained while starting to apply technology where it helped. We also began introducing AI agents to streamline travel planning, receipt processing and email summarization.

Second, Clark had tested a university-wide four-day, 32-hour schedule for two summers, teaching us about coverage and planning. Project Rouge extended that schedule within our division and added an explicit performance requirement. Leaders need a coverage model, clear performance expectations and agreement about what cannot deteriorate when hours decline.

Third, our EverTrue-Clark Advancement AI Lab had spent more than three years experimenting with how AI could augment our work responsibly. We had learned where technology could help and where people needed to check its work and make decisions, allowing us to focus on what only humans can do best. Organizations earlier in their AI journey may need to build that literacy before tying reduced hours to expected productivity gains.

Before starting such an experiment, leaders should define both sides of the equation: what outcomes should improve and what safeguards cannot be compromised. Those might include customer service, quality, employee well-being, coverage or in our case, donor relationships.

Project Rouge is now approved through the end of December. Phase Two will test whether the gains endure over a longer period as we expand our use of AI and our AI literacy programs. It will also help us better understand what is producing the gains: simpler processes, clearer priorities, AI, the incentive created by the compact or a combination of these. That distinction matters because leaders should be wary of attributing a productivity increase to the most visible new technology when several aspects of work changed simultaneously.

AI is giving us the opportunity to rethink our processes and work faster. The more important test is what we choose to do with the time it gives back and whether sharing some of it can help us build better institutions and better lives. If employees have a stake in finding the next productivity gain, time itself may become a form of profit sharing.
