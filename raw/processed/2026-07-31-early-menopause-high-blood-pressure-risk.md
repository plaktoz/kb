# Early Menopause Linked to High Blood Pressure. How to Lower Your Risk

A large study published online ahead of print on July 29 in Menopause found that women who reach menopause before age 40 face a meaningfully higher risk of developing high blood pressure than those who go through menopause after age 45, with risk peaking among women who reached menopause between ages 25 and 35. Researchers analyzed 107,836 postmenopausal women in the UK Biobank (enrolled 2006–2010, no prior hypertension diagnosis) over a median follow-up of nearly 15 years, classifying them as normal (after 45), early (40–45), or premature (before 40) menopause, and as natural versus surgical menopause.

Over follow-up, 18,508 women (17.2%) developed hypertension: 16.6% among normal-onset menopause, 18.8% among early menopause, and 22.6% among premature menopause. After adjusting for more than 50 variables — weight, lifestyle, family history, lab values — premature menopause retained a 12.3% higher hypertension risk than menopause after 45. Surgical menopause's apparent association with hypertension disappeared once other factors were controlled for, suggesting early loss of ovarian function via surgery alone doesn't independently drive the risk.

Dr. Heather Hirsch, author of "The Perimenopause Survival Guide" (not involved in the study), explained the mechanism: "We have known for nearly a century that estrogen has important, direct effects on the cardiovascular system. Estrogen supports the production of nitric oxide, which helps blood vessels relax and dilate," and helps preserve arterial flexibility. Losing ovarian estrogen early means losing these protective effects years or decades sooner than expected, alongside indirect effects like poorer sleep, weight gain, and worsening insulin sensitivity.

Cardiologist Dr. Jayne Morgan (Hello Heart), also not involved in the study, recommends women discuss hormone replacement therapy with their doctor, take prescribed blood pressure medications, consider statins for cholesterol, prioritize sleep hygiene and exercise, check vitamin D levels, eat a diet higher in protein, fiber, and vegetables, and monitor blood pressure at home since it "will often increase silently."
