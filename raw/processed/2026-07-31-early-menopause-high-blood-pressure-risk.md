# Early Menopause Linked to High Blood Pressure. How to Lower Your Risk

A large new study using the UK Biobank found that people who experience menopause earlier than usual are more likely to develop high blood pressure later in life. The association was strongest among those who experienced menopause before age 40, with the highest risk observed between ages 25 and 35, published online ahead of print on July 29 in the journal Menopause.

Researchers followed postmenopausal females ages 40 and older with no prior hypertension diagnosis, enrolled between 2006 and 2010 and followed for more than a decade. Participants were classified as normal onset (after 45), early menopause (40–45), or premature menopause (before 40), and natural versus surgical menopause was distinguished. Younger age at menopause was linked to greater risk of hypertension; while surgical menopause initially appeared linked to higher blood pressure rates, this connection disappeared after accounting for other health and lifestyle factors, suggesting early loss of ovarian function from surgery alone may not independently raise the risk. The risk rose continuously down to the late 20s to mid-30s, challenging the conventional cutoff for defining premature menopause. The study also reaffirmed known risk factors: obesity, smoking, poor diet, diabetes, and existing heart disease.

Heather Hirsch, MD, author of "The Perimenopause Survival Guide" (not involved in the study), attributed the link to estrogen's role in the cardiovascular system: estrogen supports nitric oxide production, which helps blood vessels relax and dilate, and helps preserve arterial flexibility. "When a woman loses ovarian estrogen earlier than expected, she loses these cardiovascular effects years or even decades before she otherwise would have," Hirsch said, noting indirect effects including poorer sleep, weight gain, and worsening insulin sensitivity.

Cardiologist Jayne Morgan, MD (also not involved in the study), recommended discussing hormone replacement therapy with a doctor, taking prescribed blood pressure medications, considering statins for cholesterol, prioritizing sleep hygiene and exercise, checking vitamin D levels, eating a diet higher in protein, fruit, fiber, and vegetables, and monitoring blood pressure at home since it can rise silently between office visits. Per the American Heart Association, normal blood pressure is under 120/80 mm Hg; 120–129 systolic with diastolic under 80 warrants diet and exercise changes, and anything higher warrants a conversation about medication.
