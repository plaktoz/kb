# On AI Coding and Its Discontents

Back in January, Cal Newport received a note from a senior software engineer in Silicon Valley who described himself as an AI skeptic who became converted after trying Claude Code for the first time. "Overnight, it changed the way I do my job," he wrote. "It's really, really good." He explained he no longer used a standard development environment, instead exclusively using Claude Code in a terminal window and letting it program on his behalf. "If I had to guess, I'd say a task that would have taken me a week now takes me 2 days."

This past winter, when Newport surveyed more than 300 software developers about how AI was transforming their jobs, the majority told a similar tale of shifting from writing their own code to instructing AI agents.

Last week, that same engineer sent an alarming addendum. "I'm writing to give you an update on my current thinking about the state of AI in software engineering, because my attitude has shifted quite a bit." He said features he generated using Claude Code crashed their product on two separate occasions, and his boss warned that a third incident would get him fired. "I've never had quality issues like this before in my career."

The problem, he explained, is that AI-agent code *looks* reasonable but can contain hard-to-spot bugs. Reviewing it carefully is difficult because it's "famously hard" to understand code you didn't write yourself, so the review step becomes "easy to just blow it off (especially when we are all trying to '10x' our velocity)." Soon, systems start to break.

"The coding harnesses are useful and make life as a developer easier," he summarized, "but they also encourage laziness." He has returned to largely programming by hand: "Writing your own code, slowly but surely, and using LLMs for narrow or particularly annoying tasks... is the best way to produce the highest quality code, since it's the only way to properly understand it."

Newport says he increasingly hears similar accounts from others in the software industry. In addition to reliability issues, heavy AI-agent reliance can produce a mind-numbing workflow and an environment where junior developers never acquire the expertise to become senior developers capable of designing complex systems. Meanwhile, as frontier labs reduce subsidies on compute costs, burning through tokens in search of workable results is becoming prohibitively expensive.

Newport's takeaway: coders won't abandon AI, since its facility with programming languages is too valuable to ignore, but there's a lot of work left to figure out how to integrate AI into the industry in a way that actually works. He argues AI is not a magic "infinity machine" — it's a *normal* technology, and it may be time to start talking about it that way.
