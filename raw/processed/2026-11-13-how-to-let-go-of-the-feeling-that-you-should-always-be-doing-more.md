# How To Let Go of the Feeling That You Should Always Be Doing More

## Key Takeaways

Productivity guilt often stems from beliefs that our worth must be earned through achievement, but this mentality can lead to burnout and emotional distress.

Redefining productivity to include activities that restore your energy can help you feel and perform better.

Admitting your feelings of guilt and seeking support can ease negative self-talk and help you move forward.

In a world that celebrates capitalistic achievements, it can be hard not to feel guilty for simply doing nothing. But the truth is, rest can be productive because it helps prevent burnout and supports overall well-being.

As a chronic overachiever myself, it took me a long time to learn to overcome my productivity guilt and build healthier beliefs around work and rest. Ultimately, a few life lessons helped me overcome my productivity guilt and prioritize self-care. I hope they might help you do the same.

## 1. Reframe Rest as Productive Recovery

I used to equate acts of self-care—such as taking the day off to read, journal, go for a walk, or cook myself a nutritious meal—with being "lazy." Performing tasks that don't promise a tangible financial reward? Blasphemous.

But the truth is, rest is productive. It allows us to recover from a hard week's work. It also protects us from falling into the "productivity trap," a self-defeating cycle that tells us we need to be constantly producing, often at the expense of our well-being. It's a surefire recipe for burnout, which affects all areas of life, such as relationships, mental and physical health, and, ironically, work performance, says Sophie Elkins, ACSW.

"Just as we don't expect our phones to make a call on an empty battery, we can't expect to be our best selves when running on fumes," Elkins says. In fact, research shows that emotional burnout is associated with increased productivity loss. In short: Rest is not only productive, but a necessary investment in our well-being and work performance.

By reframing rest as productive recovery, we can let go of the idea that self-care is wasted time, says Emily Sotiriadis, LMFT.

## Tip

Whenever I felt burned out at my 9-to-5, I made it a priority to take a mental health day. I initially felt guilty for doing so. But after a full day of being cozy at home and doing activities that brought me joy, such as reading a novel or watching a Hugh Grant rom-com, I showed up to work the next day more rested and able to produce work I was really proud of.

## 2. Redefine Productivity Beyond Output

Often, we view our productivity as linked to our external outputs. But what if we redefined productivity to include activities where we're not simply producing things for work or school, but refilling our personal cup?

For me, productivity doesn't just mean writing articles as a freelancer or working toward my master's degree. It's also prioritizing daily exercise for my mental and physical health, scheduling time to catch up with friends, and finding creative inspiration by reading new fiction and visiting art museums.

Productivity can look like prioritizing relationships and things unrelated to work, slowing down long enough to be in our own heads, and practicing mindfulness, says Aliza Shapiro, LCSW. In other words, "embracing moments of 'being' over constant 'doing.'"

Prioritizing activities outside of work can also help us do our jobs better. Strong mental well-being and feeling socially connected to others can greatly benefit our work productivity. The alternative—i.e., social isolation and depressive symptoms—is associated with productivity loss.

## 3. Detach Your Self-Worth From Work

When much of our society has been programmed to believe that our worth is directly tied to how much we produce, how hard we work, and how much we accomplish, it's not uncommon to feel guilty about doing nothing, says Shapiro. Most people feel some level of shame or guilt when they're not producing. They may also secretly look down on those they view as not contributing to society, such as the unhoused and unemployed.

But when you tie your self-worth to your job title or work output, you play a dangerous game; you may feel high when you're outshining your peers and being praised for your hard work, but low when you're underperforming or being overlooked by your colleagues.

"Guilt over doing nothing stems from deeply held beliefs that we must earn our worth through output," says Sotiriadis. This is called "conditional self-worth," where you only feel valuable if [fill in the blank], she adds. For example, you may only feel valuable if you secure the promotion after taking on assignments beyond your job description, or are given the reins on a major work project. But self-worth isn't conditional. "We are worthy of existing just because we are human and we are here," Elkins affirms.

## Tip

When I feel guilty about not having the emotional energy or motivation to produce great work, I remind myself that I'm not "lazy" for prioritizing my mental well-being. I deserve compassion, regardless of how much I produce or accomplish.

## 4. Admit Your Guilt to Others

When I'm overcome with productivity guilt, I can fall into a spiral of negative self-talk where I bully myself for doing nothing. I might think, "I'm lazy and undeserving, so what's even the point?"

Constant negative self-talk often results in lowered self-esteem, increased anxiety and depression, difficulty in relationships, and even having a hard time making decisions due to internal mistrust, says Elkins. Consequently, it may further stifle our productivity.

When I find myself in these spirals, I find it helpful to admit my guilt to whoever is asking me for work. There is something liberating about admitting you're struggling, and I find that people appreciate the honesty. For one, it explains why you haven't submitted your assignment yet. And more often than not, people are willing to help—either by offering solutions or reminding you of all that you've already accomplished.

So the next time you feel worthless for being unproductive, try admitting to a trusted supervisor what's keeping you in a mental rut. They may be able to offer solutions for moving forward. If you don't feel comfortable doing so, reach out to a friend who can remind you of your self-worth and that it's okay to take a few hours or the day to do nothing.

## My Story of Overcoming Productivity Guilt

Our society tends to tie people's self-worth to what they produce. When I'm not actively producing work, I feel like I'm not only failing myself, but everyone around me.

As a Black woman who grew up in the United States, I was taught that I had to work 10 times harder to reach the same achievements and have the same opportunities as my white counterparts. As a teenager, I overcompensated by being super competitive with my peers, striving for perfection, and often taking on more responsibilities than I could handle.

It wasn't until my junior year of college, when I experienced a mental break so severe it pushed me to take a leave of absence from university, that I realized overworking myself and deprioritizing rest isn't sustainable long-term. I packed up all of my things and moved back home with my parents.

For the first time in a long time, I had the luxury of doing absolutely nothing. If it hadn't been for this impromptu gap year, I'd never have discovered that I was chasing a career that wasn't mine. I never would have rediscovered my love for writing or taken active steps to become the journalist I am today. However, it took a lot of reflection and ups and downs to get over my productivity guilt during that period.

## How To Determine When Guilt Is Warranted

There's a biological reason why we experience guilt. "Just like having a bit of anxiety before a test can drive us to study that extra 15 minutes, guilt allows us to recognize and reflect on questionable behavior, take accountability, and change our course of action," says Elkins.

When guilt is warranted, it keeps us tethered to our moral compass and ensures we're acting in accordance with our personal value systems, adds Shapiro. But when guilt is disconnected from our values and instead reflects a system we never signed up for (i.e., hustle culture), it can be counterproductive.

Whenever you're feeling guilty over doing nothing, ask yourself: Why? Are you feeling guilty because you're not meeting your personal expectations, or because it's been ingrained in you that if you're not producing, you're not doing your part in society?

Either way, remind yourself that it's okay to rest and do the things that refill your cup. Even if not outwardly recognized, it is productive in and of itself.
