# Teen Discovered With a Memory More Powerful Than 99.9% of The Population

For most of us, our memories are as fragile as they are precious. Even some of the most important events in our lives can quickly become a blur, missing key details and warping with time.

In some very rare cases, however, memories are as vivid as the day they were written.

Fewer than 100 people are believed to have highly superior autobiographical memories (HSAM), and a new study documents the experiences of one of them: a 16-year-old boy scientists have named KCL, to protect his identity.

He has the remarkable ability to recall events from his own life in surprisingly precise detail.

In February 2025, KCL reached out to cognitive researcher Krystian Barzykowski, who has published several papers on the phenomenon of involuntary autobiographical memory.

"He described having exceptionally early autobiographical memories, including recollections dating back to approximately three months of age (April 2009), as well as memories from early childhood (2010–2011) that were reportedly corroborated by his parents," Barzykowski and team describe in their case report.

"He further indicated that a sense of continuous, conscious autobiographical experience emerged around the age of three. Since that time, he reported that most of his memories are highly detailed and readily accessible."

KCL said his memories were often triggered involuntarily by small cues – a smell, a song, a certain sentence – claiming that he could recall detailed events from about half of the days that had passed since around August 2022.

The research team conducted a series of established tests with KCL remotely, as the boy lived far from the research team. They confirmed KCL did indeed have a HSAM, performing at a level statistically expected to exceed 99.9 percent of the general population.

On the other hand, his attention, working memory, and executive functioning all seemed relatively normal – which is surprisingly typical for people with HSAM.

There were no other signs that anything unusual was going on inside KCL's brain: as far as his parents reported, his development from birth had been otherwise normal, without any psychiatric or educational issues.

"Accumulating evidence suggests that [people with HSAM] are not cognitive 'superheroes'," Barzykowski and team write.

"On the contrary, they appear largely comparable to the general population in domains such as learning everyday information or memorizing numbers. What distinguishes them is the way in which they remember and access autobiographical events – information about what they have experienced, witnessed, and lived through."

Barzykowski and his colleagues were particularly interested in the involuntary aspect of KCL's memories, which is reminiscent of the first official case of HSAM published back in 2006, which described a woman with "nonstop, uncontrollable, and automatic" memories of her own past.

Further cases of HSAM have emerged since, but most of these studies have tested their subjects for voluntary recall abilities. Few studies have focused on involuntary autobiographical memories among people with HSAM.

Drawing on an established method they had tested in a general population, Barzykowski and team had KCL undertake a computer task in which he monitored a set of patterns on a computer screen. Periodically, they would ask him about the thoughts that had crossed his mind.

Compared to existing data from ordinary folks, KCL had a "markedly elevated" frequency of involuntary autobiographical memories arising during his tests.

But this only applied to thoughts about his own past. Spontaneous thoughts relating to the future were in a normal range, as was his overall frequency of task-unrelated thoughts.

The authors think this is particularly interesting: the growing evidence suggests HSAM is not so much a case of generally superior memory, but "a shift in the dynamics of retrieval, characterized by heightened cue sensitivity and a lowered threshold for accessing autobiographical information," they write.

"Its uniqueness may lie not only in what is remembered, but in how autobiographical memories are accessed, highlighting accessibility and retrieval dynamics as key mechanisms underlying exceptional autobiographical memory."

The research has been published in *Cortex*.
