# Critical Mass and Tipping Points

From Farnam Street (fs.blog). Category: Mental Models.

Critical mass (also called tipping point, boiling point, percolation threshold) describes the threshold at which something becomes prevalent enough to sustain its own growth. The concept emerged from a 1971 collaboration between game theorist Thomas Schelling and sociologist Mark Granovetter.

## Origins

**Physics:** The term originates in nuclear physics — the minimum fissile material needed to sustain a chain reaction. Below critical mass, the reaction fizzles. Above it, the reaction becomes self-sustaining. The concept was borrowed as a metaphor across disciplines.

**Sociology:** Morton Grodzins first applied it in the 1960s studying racial segregation and "white flight," observing how neighborhood demographic shifts accelerate once a threshold is crossed. A few families moving out creates conditions making more departures likely, in a feedback loop.

## Key Domains

**Business:** The point where a company becomes self-sustaining economically. PayPal illustrates deliberate manufacturing of critical mass: Peter Thiel described paying users $10 to join, generating exponential growth to reach the critical user threshold needed for the network to become valuable.

**Nassim Taleb's Concept:** "F*** you money" represents personal financial critical mass — assets sufficient to provide freedom from financial dependence without making one excessively wealthy.

**Psychology:** Gustav Le Bon identified three processes creating critical masses in crowds: anonymity, contagion, and suggestibility — together producing collective, less inhibited behavior.

## Gladwell's Three Factors (*The Tipping Point*)

1. **Law of the Few** — Three key personality types drive spread:
   - *Connectors*: Highly social people with vast networks
   - *Mavens*: Information specialists others rely on
   - *Salesmen*: Charismatic persuaders

2. **Stickiness Factor:** How memorable and compelling something is — determining both adoption speed and rate of decline

3. **Context:** Circumstances, timing, and environment must align

## The 10% Rule

Research from Rensselaer Polytechnic Institute found that once committed opinion holders exceed 10% of a population, ideas spread rapidly "like flame." Below that threshold, adoption is negligibly slow. The author suggests the true threshold may be even lower, citing Nassim Taleb's "Dictatorship of the Minority" concept.

## Applications

- **Virality:** Content reaches exponential spread once enough people share it
- **Technology adoption:** S-curve dynamics — slow start, rapid acceleration after threshold, plateau
- **Urban Legends:** "The Hook" story spread cautionary messaging once enough people knew and retold it
- **Revolutions:** China 1989 — individual student protests provided the critical mass that sparked demonstrations across 300+ cities
- **Disease spread:** Epidemics require a critical mass of susceptible individuals and transmission vectors

Critical mass dynamics explain why many phenomena follow S-curves: slow accumulation, then rapid acceleration, then saturation.
