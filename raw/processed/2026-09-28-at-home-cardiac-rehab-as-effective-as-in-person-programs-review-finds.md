# At-home cardiac rehab as effective as in-person programs, review finds

A recently updated Cochrane review examined whether cardiac rehabilitation could impact health-related quality of life, disease status, and mortality in people with cardiovascular disease (CVD). Cardiovascular diseases remain the leading cause of death globally, with the World Health Organization attributing about 85% of the 19.8 million CVD-related deaths in 2022 to heart attack or stroke.

The review found that exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation was linked to a "28% relative reduction in the risk of heart attacks" and reduced "all-cause hospital admissions by around 35%." It analyzed 107 randomized controlled trials involving 26,886 people with coronary heart disease, comparing structured exercise programs to no structured exercise. Researchers also found that within 6 to 12 months, one heart attack was prevented for every 71 participants in exercise-based rehab.

Rod Taylor, PhD, senior review author and professor at the University of Glasgow, called exercise "a great tool for improving the overall health of people with coronary heart disease." Cardiologist Rigved Tadwalkar, who was not involved in the study, described the results as showing "structured movement is a highly potent primary medical therapy," comparing rehab's benefit to that of daily medications.

Common exercise types in the reviewed studies included aerobic exercise, static cycling, walking, and circuit training. Tadwalkar noted that aerobic exercise is foundational to recovery, while also emphasizing an underused approach: strength training, which he said supports "long-term longevity" and metabolic health, particularly for older adults.

The review also assessed 27 studies on home-based cardiac rehab, finding that home- and digitally supported programs delivered similar clinical benefits to in-person, facility-based programs. Lead author Grace Dibben noted that cardiac rehab is "inexpensive and cost-effective" yet remains underfunded. Tadwalkar added that digital tools like smartwatches and remote coaching could reach patients who face barriers to attending in-person sessions.

On safety, Dibben addressed common fears about post-heart attack exercise, stating that properly prescribed activity is safe for most patients and supports recovery. Tadwalkar agreed, noting that exercise is safe once the acute cardiac event has been medically treated and stabilized, and recommended starting under professional guidance with gradual progression.

The review noted a limitation: only 17% of participants were women.
