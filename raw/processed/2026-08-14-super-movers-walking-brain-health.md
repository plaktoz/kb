# What 'super movers' can teach us about healthier brains

Researchers previously studied "SuperAgers" (older adults with exceptionally sharp memories); now they're studying "super movers" — people 80+ who walk significantly faster than peers. A study published July 14 in *Neurology* found faster-than-normal walkers were substantially less likely to develop cognitive impairment.

**Definition**: Super movers are 80+ with walking speed at least 1.5 standard deviations above the age/sex average. Across ~4,000 people followed 3-5 years, super movers had about half the risk of developing cognitive impairment. In a smaller group (~200) with MRI scans, super movers showed slower cognitive decline and better-preserved brain volume in memory/learning regions. In a third group of 690+, super movers had better cognition and lower prevalence of Alzheimer's and other dementias.

**Why walking relates to brain health**: Walking is a complex activity — the brain coordinates muscles and nerves while processing balance, vision, and environment simultaneously (e.g., navigating a busy street), requiring multiple brain regions to work together.

**Correlation, not proven causation**: Dr. Leana Wen (CNN wellness expert) cautions super movers may simply have been more physically active throughout life, have fewer chronic conditions, healthier diets, genetic advantages, higher incomes, or stronger social support — any of which could drive both mobility and cognitive health. It's also possible well-aging brains simply better maintain fast walking.

**Can you become a super mover later in life?** A March study in *Geriatrics* found 45% of people 65+ improved physical or cognitive function over a 12-year follow-up. Sedentary people can start with short walks and gradually increase duration/frequency; regular walkers can add faster intervals or hills. Consult a healthcare provider if you have chronic conditions or mobility limitations — the biggest benefit comes from going from none to a little exercise.

**When to see a doctor**: A gradual slowdown can be normal aging, but a relatively quick or symptom-accompanied change is worth flagging — causes include arthritis, muscle loss, medication side effects, neurological conditions, or vision/balance changes.

**Beyond walking**: Cycling, swimming, pickleball, tennis, golf, and strength training all matter. Controlling blood pressure, cholesterol, and diabetes; not smoking; a whole-food diet; adequate sleep; and staying socially/mentally engaged (hobbies, new skills, relationships) round out brain-healthy habits.
