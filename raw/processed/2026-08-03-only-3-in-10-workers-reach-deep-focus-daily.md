# Only 3 in 10 workers reach deep focus every day. Here's what's getting in the way.

Only about one-third of US workers said they reach a state of deep focus every day, according to a new survey (Resume Now's Fleeting Flow Report) suggesting meetings, busy work, and constant interruptions are making uninterrupted concentration increasingly difficult. The findings align with Microsoft's 2025 Work Trend Index, which found 80% of employees say they lack the time or energy to do their jobs, and that the average knowledge worker is interrupted by a meeting, email, or notification every two minutes — a pattern Microsoft calls the "infinite workday."

Based on a survey of 1,012 employed adults, the report found 31% of workers reach a state of "flow" every day, 46% achieve it several times a week, 15% only occasionally, and 8% rarely or never.

**Busy work is crowding out meaningful work.** 77% of respondents said repetitive or low-value work occupies a meaningful portion of their week, and 40% estimated at least a quarter of their working hours go to "busy work." Nearly two-thirds (64%) said only half or fewer of the meetings they attend are productive, and 38% spend at least five hours a week in meetings.

**Burnout and interruptions take a toll.** 41% identified burnout or fatigue as the biggest barrier to deep focus, followed by personal distractions (35%) and coworker interruptions (35%). Other distractions include social media (25%), excessive meetings (21%), constant notifications (19%), and technical issues (15%). Nearly 8 in 10 workers (79%) report some level of Zoom fatigue.

**The workday extends beyond office hours.** 58% feel pressure to remain available outside normal hours, with 32% feeling that pressure often or constantly. 44% work extra hours because they can't finish during the regular day, while 19% attribute overtime to manager or culture expectations.

Resume Now career expert Keith Spencer said busy work, unproductive meetings, and digital interruptions leave little room for sustained concentration, which over time affects productivity, morale, and contributes to burnout. The report concludes that employers may not have a productivity problem so much as an attention problem — and that reducing unnecessary meetings and protecting uninterrupted blocks of time may deliver bigger gains than new time-management tools.
