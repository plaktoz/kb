# Michael Polansky is training an AI model on skin that's still alive

Michael Polansky — better known publicly as Lady Gaga's partner and a former top deputy to Sean Parker — has quietly spent years building an AI-driven startup, Outer Biosciences, that keeps living human skin tissue alive for weeks outside the body to discover new skincare compounds.

Polansky grew up in Minnesota, studied applied mathematics and computer science at Harvard, and spent three years at Bridgewater Associates before joining Sean Parker's family office, where he helped stand up the Parker Institute for Cancer Immunotherapy. He met Germanotta in late 2019 through her mother.

Outer Biosciences, founded by Polansky in 2022, grew out of frustration that innovation in biology and chemistry has never matched software, largely because there's no ethical way to run experiments directly on people, and existing proxies (animal models, simplified cell cultures, lab-grown organoids) are poor stand-ins for real human organs.

Instead of building a synthetic organ, the company sources human skin that would otherwise be discarded after surgery, through vetted biobanks operating under institutional review board oversight and documented donor consent, principally the National Disease Research Interchange and the Cooperative Human Tissue Network. All samples arrive de-identified. A proprietary support system feeds the tissue nutrients and removes metabolic waste, extending viable life well past the industry norm of a few days to up to a month, preserving its "day-zero" molecular programs.

That extra time lets researchers track slower biological processes — collagen remodeling, pigmentation change, barrier repair — that take weeks to unfold. For example, researchers can induce UVB sunburn damage and track the stress, inflammatory, and recovery response over following weeks.

The system is a closed loop: an AI model predicts which untested chemicals are likely to have a beneficial effect on a specific skin function; those chemicals are run through the living-tissue system; the results feed back into the model, improving future predictions. Early on, the company relied on a "brute force" approach mining scientific literature, producing a couple of leads over 18 months. With AI layered in, the company now generates a new candidate roughly every six weeks, with six leads currently active and several dozen additional "hits" logged.

The universe of approved skin-active ingredients is small — about 120–130 FDA-recognized OTC active ingredients, closer to 200 if research-backed cosmetic ingredients are included. Outer Biosciences discovers cosmetic ingredients, not drugs, so there's no FDA approval needed; the path runs through standardized industry naming and OECD-guideline safety testing, then licensing to beauty or pharma partners for formulation and commercialization. Four of the company's six current leads look likely to reach commercialization.

The company has raised roughly $23 million to date from backers including Calm Capital, Brighter Capital, and Polansky's own firm Hawktail, and employs 19 people (mostly near Cambridge, Massachusetts). It also runs collaborative research partnerships, including with a pharmaceutical partner studying why certain cancer drugs cause skin rashes.

A competitor, Vivodyne (Philadelphia), builds lab-grown human organ tissue paired with predictive AI to replace animal drug testing, and recently raised close to $80 million led by Khosla Ventures. Polansky argues Outer Biosciences' advantage is that its living-tissue data doesn't exist anywhere else — unlike AI companies scraping the public internet, there is no "biology internet" to scrape — making the position more defensible, if slower to build. All of the company's AI work currently runs on-premise rather than in the cloud, because the company doesn't want the data in the cloud.
