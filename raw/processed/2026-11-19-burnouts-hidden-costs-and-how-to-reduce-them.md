# Burnout's Hidden Costs — And How to Reduce Them

## Key Insights

- Burnout carries significant business costs, making employee well-being an operational priority, not just a wellness concern.
- Realistic workloads, workable time off, and stronger manager support can help community financial institutions address burnout's underlying causes.
- Track staffing trends and employee feedback together to identify persistent pressure and evaluate improvements.

A phone running low on battery offers a straightforward warning: recharge soon or risk losing power. Most of us respond by reaching for a charger or switching to low-power mode, putting less important functions on hold. We don't expect the device to keep doing everything indefinitely on a dwindling charge. Employees rarely come with such clear indicators. They may keep answering messages, attending meetings, and helping customers even as their energy dwindles. By the time the problem becomes obvious, restoring their capacity may require more than a long weekend.

For community financial institutions (CFIs), that makes burnout more than an employee wellness concern. It deserves attention as a business issue, with implications for productivity, staffing, and the ability to sustain performance.

## What Does Burnout Cost?

Research published in the American Journal of Preventive Medicine in 2025 offers a useful estimate. Using a computational model, researchers calculated annual costs associated with employee disengagement and burnout of approximately $4K per nonmanagerial hourly employee, $4.3K per salaried nonmanager, $10.8K per manager, and $20.7K per executive. For a hypothetical US company with 1,000 employees and a typical mix of roles, the estimated annual burden reached $5.04MM. These are modeled estimates, not measured losses at every employer or figures specific to banking.

The distinction matters: CFIs should not simply multiply their headcount by one of those figures and call the result their burnout bill. The research instead provides a reason to examine where employee strain may be affecting attendance and performance.

Newer research suggests that workforce well-being remains under pressure. Mercer's 2026 Global Talent Trends report found that only 44% of employees globally described themselves as thriving at work, down from 66% in 2024. The report draws on a survey of nearly 12,000 executives, HR leaders, investors, and employees conducted in September and October 2025. Thriving is not the same measure as burnout, but the decline reinforces the need to pay attention to employees' capacity, not just their output.

## Look Beyond Wellness Benefits

Addressing burnout starts with adequate downtime, realistic workloads, and support for managers. For CFIs, the challenge is putting those priorities into practice every day, rather than leaving employees to manage the strain on their own. Here are several ways to turn those priorities into action:

- **Review what is creating the workload.** Ask teams which recurring tasks, approval steps, meetings, and reporting requests consume time without adding sufficient value. Before adding another priority, identify what can stop, wait, or be simplified. Use busy periods, vacancies, and major projects as prompts to reassess capacity.
- **Make time off workable.** Encouraging vacation means little if employees expect to keep monitoring email or return to an unmanageable backlog. Establish backup coverage, document essential procedures, and clarify which situations genuinely warrant contacting someone who is away. Assess whether employees can disconnect, not simply whether they have accrued leave.
- **Give managers room to manage.** A manager responsible for customer relationships, operational tasks, and staff development may struggle to give each responsibility enough attention. Review the balance between individual production expectations and supervisory duties. Regular leadership check-ins should address competing demands and resource gaps, not just results.
- **Build recovery into demanding projects.** A system conversion, examination, or other intensive initiative should have a recovery plan as well as a completion date. Consider staggered time off, temporary workload adjustments, and a review of deferred responsibilities before immediately launching the next major effort.
- **Ask specific questions and act on the answers.** "How is everyone doing?" may not reveal much. More useful questions include: Which deadlines are hardest to meet? What work follows you home? What prevents you from taking time off? Select a manageable improvement, assign an owner, and report back on what changed.

## Measure Capacity Alongside Performance

To evaluate these efforts, consider reviewing overtime, unused vacation, absenteeism, voluntary turnover, and employee feedback together. None establishes burnout on its own. Use the information to identify patterns and start conversations, rather than diagnose individuals.

For CFIs, the objective is not to eliminate every demanding day. It is to keep periods of extraordinary effort from becoming the normal operating model. Start with one team or process where pressure is persistent, remove a specific source of unnecessary work, and assess whether employees gain meaningful breathing room.
