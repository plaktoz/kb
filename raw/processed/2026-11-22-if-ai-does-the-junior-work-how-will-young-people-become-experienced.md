# If AI Does the Junior Work, How Will Young People Become Experienced?

I serve on a local school board and have spent my career in finance and strategy. Those are very different parts of my life, but lately they have led me to the same question: How do people develop judgment and experience if artificial intelligence starts doing more of the work through which beginners used to gain them?

Early in my career, I built financial models, checked numbers and prepared presentations. More experienced colleagues reviewed what I’d done, sometimes sending me back to reconsider an assumption that looked perfectly reasonable on paper. With enough practice and feedback, I learned that a correct calculation can still lead to a poor decision.

That work was producing two things at once: an output and a more experienced person. The company received something useful, and I gradually became capable of handling more complicated problems.

AI may change the economics of that arrangement.

Junior employees contribute useful work while they learn, helping offset the cost of supervising them. If AI can produce an initial analysis more cheaply, a company may decide it no longer needs as many beginners. The savings are immediate, while developing experienced staff takes years. And even after investing in someone, the employer cannot be certain that person will stay.

A company could automate junior assignments now and hire experienced professionals when it needs them. That may be a reasonable decision for one business. But if enough companies make the same choice, where will those experienced workers come from?

There are early signs that entry-level hiring is changing. A 2026 study of U.S. software job postings found an almost 15% decline in junior vacancies relative to senior ones following the public arrival of generative AI in late 2022. A U.S. Census Bureau working paper reported weaker initial employment and earnings among graduates of college majors most exposed to AI. These include such jobs as junior software engineers, financial analysts, graphic designers and marketing associates. Neither study proves that AI caused these changes or measures employers’ investments in training.

AI may also help beginners learn faster, and some companies may find better ways to train people with it. We cannot assume, however, that every employer will continue to find it worthwhile to hire inexperienced workers.

## The gap may begin in school

AI is also changing how students complete their work. A polished assignment may tell us little about how much a student understands or could reproduce independently. Teachers already work to assess that understanding. As schools decide how AI belongs in education, preserving opportunities for students to practice and demonstrate what they know independently should remain a priority.

In a 2025 randomized study of university students, those assigned to study with ChatGPT scored 57.5% on a surprise test 45 days later, compared with 68.5% among students studying without AI. The study was small and measured knowledge retention rather than critical thinking. It does not establish what is happening across K–12 classrooms, and AI used differently may improve learning.

Still, I worry about students who become accustomed to relying on AI to complete their assignments and then enter workplaces where the routine tasks that once helped beginners learn have been automated. Employers may expect them to exercise judgment sooner, while offering fewer opportunities to develop it through practice and feedback.

Districts already consult employers about changing skill requirements. Those conversations are valuable, although today’s vacancies and lists of required skills can only tell us so much about the work students will encounter several years from now.

Conversations with people working in education and workforce development have made me more aware of how difficult that planning can be. Labor-market information often lags behind changes in actual jobs, while educational pathways take years to complete.

An occupation may remain in demand even as employers change what they expect from someone applying for the first position.

I would like schools and colleges to look further ahead when reviewing career pathways with employers. Alongside the usual discussion of skills and credentials, we should ask how entry-level work is likely to change and where new employees will gain the experience needed to take on greater responsibility.

There are already internships, apprenticeships and other opportunities to learn through work. Are they giving students meaningful practice and supervision? Will enough opportunities be available as traditional junior roles change? We may also need to reconsider how the costs of developing beginners are shared. Schools on their own cannot replace every disappearing step in a professional career.

I worry especially about young people without professional connections. A student whose family knows people in an industry may find introductions, advice or informal opportunities to learn. Someone without those connections depends much more on accessible programs and employers willing to provide a first chance.

If those opportunities become scarcer, the differences could grow. Students who already face barriers to entering professional careers may find it even harder to gain the experience employers expect.

From my school-board perspective, I would rather see us examine these questions while we still have time to adjust how we prepare students. We may continue to see strong demand for experienced professionals while the opportunities that once helped beginners develop into them become harder to find.

The experienced people we need years from now will not simply appear when we need them. Someone has to give them the opportunity to become experienced first.
