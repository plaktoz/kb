# The 2026 Global Bond Sell-Off: Causes, Consequences, and Local Impacts

Long-term interest rates rose sharply in 2026, with the steepest climb in August and September. Yields on government bonds around the world rose to levels last seen nearly two decades ago. Because the bond market is the foundation of global finance, these rising yields have steadily increased borrowing costs for governments, businesses and households alike.

## The Global Rise in Borrowing Costs

The yield on the benchmark US 10-year Treasury note closed above 5% in September for the first time since 2007. It stood just under 4% at the end of February, before the war with Iran began, and gained more than half a percentage point in September alone. The 30-year Treasury yield rose above 5.6% by the end of September. Because bond prices and yields move in opposite directions, the rise in yields has cut the market value of bonds issued in the low-rate years, imposing losses on the banks, pension funds and individual investors who hold them, while forcing every new borrower to pay considerably more. The trend extends well beyond the United States. Long-term yields in Britain, Germany and France have climbed alongside Treasuries, and Japanese government bond yields are at their highest since 1996.

## Short-Term Rates, the Fed, and the Yield Curve

The Federal Reserve controls the overnight interest rate, but it does not set long-term rates; investors do, by buying and selling bonds. Plotting the yields on government bonds of every maturity, from three months to thirty years, gives what is called the yield curve. Its slope tells us how investors see the future: a steep curve means they demand much more to lend for long periods than for short ones.

Over 2026 the curve has moved in four phases. From February to mid-June, as the war with Iran pushed up energy prices and markets began to expect the Fed to raise rates, short-term yields rose faster than long-term ones, and the spread between the 10-year and 2-year yields narrowed from about 0.7 to under 0.3 percentage points. Over the summer the pattern reversed. Long-term yields led the climb, and the spread widened to about 0.5 points by mid-August. From then on short-term yields took the lead again as a rate increase came into view, and the spread narrowed to about 0.2 points around the Fed’s decision. On September 17, the Fed raised its target range by a quarter point, to 3.75–4.00%. In the days that followed, long-term yields again rose faster than short-term ones, and the spread widened to about 0.4 points by the end of the month.

Figure 2: The Treasury yield curve at the end of 2025, at its flattest in June, at its steepest in August, and at the end of September

What happens next depends on why the Fed is raising rates and how much investors trust it. If central banks tighten to contain an energy-driven rise in inflation, short-term yields typically rise more than long-term ones, and the curve flattens further. A Fed that convinces investors it will keep inflation in check can even bring long-term yields down, because lenders then need less compensation for the risk of inflation eroding their returns. If investors instead doubt the Fed’s resolve or independence, they demand more for lending long, and long-term yields rise regardless of what happens to short-term rates.

## How Yields Impact the Economy and Hawai’i

The US Treasury market sets the baseline cost of money for the global economy. Lenders price loans off Treasury yields of a similar maturity and add a risk premium. Auto loan rates follow 3-to-5-year yields and mortgage rates follow the 10-year yield. The average 30-year fixed mortgage rate rose above 7% in September, its highest since early 2025. (Credit card rates are tied instead to the banks’ prime rate, which moves with the Fed’s policy rate.)

Figure 4: The 30-year mortgage rate and the 10-year Treasury yield

In Hawai’i, where home prices are among the highest in the country, 30-year fixed mortgage rates above 7% place tight constraints on affordability and housing market activity. On a median-priced O’ahu single-family home, about $1.18 million over the past year, a buyer putting 20% down pays roughly $6,270 a month in principal and interest at 7%, against $3,970 at the 3% rates many owners locked in during 2020 and 2021. That gap of about $2,300 a month also discourages those owners from selling, which holds down the number of homes on the market. Nationally, about half of outstanding mortgages still carry rates of 4% or less, down from about two-thirds in early 2022.

Figure 5: Monthly payment on a median-priced O’ahu home at mortgage rates of 3% and 7%

Higher yields reach beyond housing. Local businesses face higher costs to finance expansion or operations. State and county governments pay more to borrow for infrastructure projects, and higher debt service costs leave less tax revenue for public services.

Higher US interest rates also reach Hawai’i through the exchange rate. Although the Bank of Japan has been raising its domestic interest rates, US rates remain well above them, and that gap has kept the Japanese yen weak against the dollar. In late July the yen fell to its weakest level in about four decades. For Hawai’i, a weak yen makes travel to the islands considerably more expensive for Japanese visitors, one of the state’s most important tourism markets.

## Breaking Down the Rise in Long-Term Yields

A long-term yield can be thought of as having three parts: the inflation-adjusted short-term interest rates investors expect over the life of the bond, the inflation they expect over that period, and a term premium, the extra return they demand for locking up their money for ten years rather than lending it a few months at a time. Two approaches show which parts moved.

The first approach compares ordinary Treasuries with inflation-protected ones (TIPS). The spread between their yields, known as breakeven inflation, measures the inflation investors expect. Since the end of 2025 the 10-year Treasury yield has risen by about 1.1 percentage points, while breakeven inflation has risen by only about 0.1 point. It climbed in the spring, after the energy shock, and then fell back. Almost all of the increase is in the real, inflation-adjusted yield.

Figure 7: Change in the 10-year yield, the real yield and breakeven inflation since the end of 2025

The second approach splits the yield into the short-term rates investors expect over the next ten years and the term premium. Neither can be observed directly, so the split relies on statistical models. The models attribute most of this year’s rise to expected short-term rates. Investors have come to believe that rates will settle at a higher level than they thought in January. The term premium rose earlier. It was below zero for most of the decade before 2025, when investors accepted less on long bonds than on rolling over short ones, and climbed to about 0.75 percentage points by the end of 2025. It rises when investors grow less sure about future inflation, interest rates or the government’s finances, or when more long-term debt has to find reluctant buyers, and falls when those pressures ease.

## Supply, Demand, and the Drivers of Higher Yields

Over the past few years, several forces have pushed long-term yields up, some by adding to the supply of bonds and others by thinning the demand for them.

The primary driver is the volume of government debt. The US budget deficit is running at about 5.5% of gross domestic product with unemployment near 4%. Deficits that large usually occur during recessions or wars. In an economy close to full employment the government would normally borrow far less, leaving room to borrow when the next downturn arrives. Before 2020, in years when unemployment was below 5%, the deficit averaged about 1% of GDP. Debt held by the public has now reached about 100% of GDP. Because the government must sell a large and growing amount of bonds to fund its deficits, it must offer higher yields to entice investors to absorb them.

Figure 9: The federal deficit and the unemployment rate since 1981, with recessions shaded

Investors have known about large deficits for years. What has changed is their confidence that governments will deal with them. Budgets keep adding to the debt even when the economy is strong, and political leaders show little appetite for raising taxes or trimming the largest programs.

Other large borrowers face similar pressure, including Britain, France, Italy, and Japan. Japan’s government debt exceeds twice its GDP and its 10-year yield has climbed from near zero in 2021 to almost 3%. These countries’ own debts are only part of the story, because national bond markets are linked across borders. Investors can move money between countries, so when US yields rise, other governments must pay more to keep buyers, and part of the rise abroad is the US increase spreading outward.

Demographics compound the supply problem. For decades, the baby boom generation was in its peak earning years, accumulating savings and buying bonds, which helped hold yields down. As that generation retires, it draws down those savings, and aging populations draw heavily on programs like Social Security and Medicare, forcing the government to borrow even more.

Private borrowing is now competing with the government for capital. Technology companies are issuing large amounts of debt to finance the data centers required for the artificial intelligence boom; their bond sales have more than doubled this year to over $200 billion. That is still small next to Treasury issuance, and economists disagree on how much it moves government bond yields, but it adds to the demand on a finite pool of global savings.

Inflation remains a persistent risk. The conflict with Iran has disrupted energy supplies and pushed diesel prices to record highs. Expected inflation has risen only modestly so far, but because inflation erodes the value of a bond’s fixed payments, investors demand higher yields when the risk of it grows, and central banks in the US, Europe and Japan have turned more willing to raise rates to contain it.

Finally, the buyer base for US debt has changed. For decades, foreign central banks were reliable buyers of Treasuries regardless of price. They ran large trade surpluses and needed a safe, liquid place to park their reserves, creating a “global savings glut” that kept yields low. Foreign investors now hold about 30% of the Treasuries in public hands, down from close to half in the early 2010s.

Figure 10: The share of Treasuries held by foreign investors since 1970

Japan illustrates the shift. For years the Bank of Japan held Japanese interest rates near zero, which made it attractive to borrow cheaply in yen, convert the money into dollars and buy higher-yielding US Treasuries, a strategy known as the carry trade. With Japanese yields now higher, that trade pays far less, and Japanese investors have more reason to keep their money at home. Japan remains the largest foreign holder of Treasuries, at roughly $1 trillion, so even a modest shift in its investors’ preferences matters for US yields.

As foreign buyers step back, the marginal buyers of US debt are increasingly hedge funds. These funds do not typically use their own cash; instead, they borrow in the short-term “repo” market to finance large Treasury purchases. Because they rely on high leverage to profit from small price differences, they are sensitive to market swings and prone to selling quickly when volatility rises, which can turn an orderly rise in yields into a disorderly one.

## Assessing the Risks: A New Normal or a Brewing Crisis?

Financial experts disagree on how worried we should be.

One perspective frames the 5% yield as a return to historical norms. Ten-year yields were above 5% through most of the 1980s and 1990s, periods remembered for strong growth. In this view the near-zero rates of the 2010s were the anomaly, a result of sluggish post-crisis growth, and a higher cost of capital enforces financial discipline, compensates investors for inflation risk and steers money toward the most productive investments. The pace of this year’s rise, though rapid, has also been orderly. In Britain’s gilt crisis of September 2022, the 30-year government bond yield jumped by more than a percentage point in a few days, and the Bank of England had to step in. This year the US 10-year yield has taken about a month to rise half a point.

The opposing perspective accepts that 5% is not unusual but warns about the context. Government debt is far higher today than the last time yields stood at these levels, and a large deficit in good times leaves little room to respond when a recession comes. The US federal government now spends more on interest than on national defense, close to 4% of GDP. That bill will grow as older, cheaper debt is refinanced at today’s rates, and much of it is paid with new borrowing. That cycle raises the risk of a disruptive financial event, especially with leveraged investors holding a growing share of the market.

## What to Watch in the Months Ahead

The near-term direction of the bond market depends heavily on energy prices and on the Federal Reserve’s ability to bring inflation down without stalling the economy. If energy supplies improve, the case for higher short-term rates should weaken; if not, further rate increases are possible. Investors will also watch how much advance guidance the Fed’s new chair, Kevin Warsh, chooses to give, and whether rising Japanese yields keep drawing Japanese money home.

The US Treasury has tried to steady the market. It has doubled the size of its operations to buy back older long-term bonds, from $2 billion to $4 billion at a time, paid for by issuing short-term bills. The buybacks are a small, mechanical adjustment in a market where more than $1 trillion of Treasuries trade every day, not a solution to the underlying fiscal imbalance. Ultimately, bringing long-term yields down will require smaller deficits, a step that demands difficult political decisions on taxes and spending. Until then, the era of very cheap long-term credit appears to be over.

For Hawai’i, a long stretch of high rates means lasting pressure on home sales, on the cost of borrowing for businesses and local governments, and on travel from Japan. How long it lasts depends on the national and global forces described above, which lie outside the islands’ control.
