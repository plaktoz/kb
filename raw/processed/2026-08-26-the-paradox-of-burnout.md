# The Paradox of Burnout

Burnout most commonly afflicts people in *desirable* jobs — doctors, teachers, academics — rather than those in mundane ones.

Drawing on Jonathan Malesic's book *The End of Burnout*, the core mechanism is: burnout emerges from the **gap between expectations and reality**. Prestigious careers attract idealistic expectations, and when reality falls short, the psychological crash is severe.

Malesic's own story illustrates this — a tenured theology professor who found students disengaged and his work largely ignored. Ironically, his earlier stint as a parking attendant produced no burnout at all; coworkers simply did their jobs and found meaning elsewhere in life.

## Key Insight

Passionate engagement can *create* conditions for burnout, not prevent it. The antidote isn't a dead-end job, but **balance** — matching expectations to reality while maintaining outside sources of meaning: relationships, hobbies, health, and social support.

As Young notes, "balance is often more productive than an obsession with work." The person treating work as one part of life builds resilience, enabling sustained performance over the long run — ultimately outpacing the all-in approach.
