# Women with Type 2 Diabetes Face Higher Mental Health Risks Than Men

A UK-based study published in *PLOS Medicine* examined health outcomes in 28,720 people diagnosed with type 2 diabetes between 2010 and 2020. Researchers used data from the UK Clinical Practice Research Datalink to track comorbidities developing after diagnosis, grouped into mental health conditions, cardiovascular/kidney disease, and hypertension.

**Key findings:**
- Hypertension was most common overall, affecting roughly 20–21% of both sexes
- **8.1% of women** developed mental health conditions (anxiety, depression, or somatoform disorders) vs. **5.3% of men**
- Women ages 16–39 showed the highest probability of developing these conditions
- **8.4% of men** developed cardiovascular or end-stage renal disease vs. **5.3% of women**
- Mental health trajectories were linked to earlier mortality

**Important caveats:** Women averaged 92 primary care visits vs. 32 for men, meaning conditions may simply be "recorded sooner in women rather than occurring at genuinely different rates," according to study author Fabiola Eto.

Cardiologist Cheng-Han Chen cautioned that findings may reflect "differences in how commonly certain conditions are diagnosed," rather than direct diabetes effects. Experts recommend routine psychological screening as standard in diabetes follow-up care for all patients.
