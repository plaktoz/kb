# Game Theory 101: Anticipate Your Competitor's Next Move

Featuring Henrique Castro-Pires. By Avery Forman on November 23, 2026

What if you could anticipate your competitor’s next move before making your own? Game theory can help business leaders think several steps ahead and make smarter strategic decisions, says Henrique Castro-Pires.

Launching a new product raises a host of strategic questions, from when to enter the market to how to price and promote the product. If a competitor is developing something similar, does it make sense to rush your item’s debut or take time to refine your offering?

Game theory, a classic mathematical framework that models strategic interactions, can help executives think through these decisions and predict likely and desired outcomes to make informed decisions. Popularized by the 2001 Oscar-winning film A Beautiful Mind, which chronicled the life and work of pioneering mathematician John Nash, many businesses deploy game theory principles to help leaders make strategic decisions in fields ranging from biology and artificial intelligence to economics and the boardroom.

A C-suite might not even know it’s using game theory when parsing options, says Harvard Business School Assistant Professor Henrique Castro-Pires, but chances are some of the principles’ maxims are at work. His teaching module, “Game On: Strategic Thinking, Competition, and Cooperation,” offers advice for executives to help them sharpen their choices and reach business goals faster by using game strategies to their advantage.

“Managers are thinking about how others are going to respond all the time, and the toolkit of game theory just helps them be a bit more systematic about it,” says Castro-Pires. “Game theory helps avoid making unsubstantiated assumptions about behavior. It requires you to systematically go through incentives and think about how others are going to respond.”

As AI puts pressure on businesses to make quicker decisions—and increasingly puts companies head-to-head with bots that are making strategic calls—modeling potential outcomes is becoming more valuable than ever, he says.

“The timeframe you have to analyze and anticipate the situation becomes shorter,” says Castro-Pires. “If you’re playing against an agent, you have to think, ‘What is the goal of that agent? What is the instruction that was given to that agent?’”

## Applying game theory to strategic decisions

While the theory is commonly used to gain an advantage against a competitor, Castro-Pires says it’s also useful to for managing relationships with suppliers, buyers, and other parts of a company’s food chain.

Game theory might reveal that a seemingly attractive move, such as cutting prices to win customers, could trigger a competitive response that leaves everyone worse off. Or it might help leaders recognize when cooperation creates more value than competition. For instance, pushing a supplier for lower prices may deliver short-term savings but undermine the supplier’s ability to invest in quality, whereas a cooperative agreement that accounts for both parties’ priorities may create greater long-term value.

Consider entertainment rivals Marvel and DC Comics, Castro-Pires suggests. If both are working on a superhero movie, what happens to ticket sales if they both launch in the same week versus a month apart? Or at the beginning versus the end of the summer season, compared to the holidays?

“When they're thinking about those things, there is going to be some interaction, and they will have to try to anticipate what the other is doing,” he says.

Game theory frames strategic decisions around the players, their available actions, such as adjusting prices or increasing advertising, and the payoffs at stake—from profit to reputation. Leaders can create a payoff matrix to map these interdependencies, helping them identify their best response to another player’s anticipated moves.

An outcome will sometimes result in a Nash equilibrium, where both players are getting the best payoff possible given the other’s choice, and neither player would want to unilaterally change their action if given the option. Sometimes players need to make choices simultaneously, and other times one business makes a first move, and then the competitor responds. Game trees can illustrate different sequential actions and potential outcomes to help leaders determine the strategic decisions that make the most sense.

## How to analyze players and consider next moves

Before starting a game, Castro-Pires advises leaders to analyze each player, assessing their objectives, available actions, desired outcomes, and how much each player values those outcomes to understand the incentives shaping their decisions. With players analyzed, Castro-Pires suggests that leaders ask themselves the following questions:

What would I do in my competitor’s shoes? Also consider: What would the competitor do in response to each of your possible actions?

What are your best responses to your competitor’s actions? Consider all the possibilities and how you would respond to each, instead of going directly to the competitor action you think is most likely to occur, he advises.

What’s most likely to happen? The prediction is the outcome in which each competitor best responds to the actions of the other competitors, i.e., a Nash equilibrium.

What would make the game more favorable for you? The answer might be moving faster or signaling a commitment that would change the payoff.

How can you induce or incentivize certain behaviors in other players? “If my competitor does A, what is my best path forward? My competitor does B, what is my best path forward? You do it for all the possibilities, and you also do it in the reverse, putting yourself in the shoes of the competitor and thinking what is best for them to do,” explains Castro-Pires.

One potential variable: Long-term business relationships can change some of the dynamics. For instance, a partner who values sustainability might opt for a more expensive green choice to maximize a positive relationship outcome even if it lowers profits, he says.

While companies may not be able to predict every player’s move precisely, the exercise can help them challenge their assumptions before committing resources. And although playing a game may not eliminate uncertainty altogether, it may help business leaders avoid being surprised by consequences they could have anticipated.

## How leaders can play the game

Ready to start playing? Castro-Pires offers these steps for incorporating game theory into your corporate strategy.

## Take the time to carefully analyze each player

This assessment should include what each player values most and the payoffs and consequences that matter to them.

## First look forward, then reason backward

Think through the choices the competition might make in the future, then adjust your responses from there.

## Understand that each interaction can change the game

Whether a company cooperates or retaliates against an opponent could alter the nature of the competition.
