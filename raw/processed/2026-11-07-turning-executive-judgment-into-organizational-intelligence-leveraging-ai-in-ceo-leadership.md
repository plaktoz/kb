# Turning executive judgment into organizational intelligence: Leveraging AI in CEO leadership

The judgment of the CEO and top leaders is one of the most valuable sources of knowledge in the organization. AI can now codify that tacit wisdom and make it available to everyone, for the first time.

**The best CEOs we know** cannot fully explain how they make decisions. Their reasoning is buried so deep that articulating it feels rather like trying to describe how you ride a bicycle. They can talk compellingly in general terms about what they value and what they look for, but they struggle to delineate what happens at the actual moment of decision, the instant when a board decision becomes "ready," or a strategy stops feeling distinctive enough. That opacity has been a challenge for as long as the CEO role has existed. Understanding what lies beneath an excellent leader's judgment should be a strategic advantage for organizations. Instead, it's almost always been a lost opportunity.

AI can now turn this structural opacity into instructive clarity. Consider what happens when a great CEO leaves a company. The documents of their tenure remain: the strategy decks, the board minutes, the decision memos, and an evidence trail of every call. So do the people who have worked on and through it all with them, many of them apprenticed under the CEO. What does not survive the transition, however, is the reasoning behind thousands of decisions that people were not always party to. Most successors spend several months reconstructing a standard of judgment that was taken for granted. The people they inherit continue attempting to calibrate to standards they could sense but never fully define. This is not a failure of talent but a limitation of transmission inside a system. And it restricts everyone's ability to contribute to the continued growth and renewal of the organization.

What AI makes possible, for the first time, is a means of extending the reach of a leader's reasoning further than any calendar or organizational hierarchy could enable. It codifies standards and methods of decision-making and judgment, such as the criteria a leader applies, their patterns of inference, and the burden of proof they demand, in a form that the whole organization can learn from and build upon. This can lower the cost of iterating toward a decision, opening more pathways and scenarios, and building higher confidence behind each. This is the project we have explored during the past year with sitting CEOs across industries.

Importantly, leaders who are making the most of AI in this context are not seeking to replace human judgment with AI analysis—anything but. McKinsey's research on what separates excellent CEOs from average ones—drawn from hundreds of interviews and years of direct engagement, partnership, and observation and articulated in our books *CEO Excellence* and *A CEO for All Seasons*—lands on an important differentiator: executive judgment, applied at speed, exercised consistently, and sustained under ambiguity. The CEO and other key leaders—of business units, functions, and teams—are the ones who must decide under ambiguity and chart the company's course. The novel approach we will describe in this story augments and enables human judgment and agency, not just at the top but throughout the organization.

## The five parts of an executive AI

Most leaders today are in the early stages of wiring AI into what might be called their "personal productivity stack." Those who are further along are enjoying early, real returns. Synthesis, preparation, and pattern recognition across large volumes of information are all becoming faster. The more consequential opportunity AI presents for a CEO and the organization is not just acceleration but amplification: making the leader's best thinking and standards available to the entire organization, with scale and consistency.

An "executive AI" is what makes that amplification possible: an ambient layer with context, memory, and a persistent system of record, built around personal and organizational knowledge graphs. The AI accumulates input from leaders across the company, creating a record of, for example, how they empower people, manage stakeholders, and make decisions—all the various aspects of executive judgment. As this knowledge base grows and is synthesized, leaders across the company can tap into it whenever needed to ensure they are armed with the organization's best collective wisdom.

In practice, an executive AI has five interlocking parts:

* *Steering the direction of attention*. The backbone here is a living "knowledge graph" of the leader's world, an evolving ontology of the decisions, people, commitments, and signals that comprise the role. It powers the whole system and stays current by capturing the leader's own stream of work—documents, voice notes, emails, meeting notes, and more.

  The result is a dynamic system of leading indicators that surface the most complex matters a CEO and other leaders will face. It triages what deserves their attention and focus, without burdening them with complexity. A leader thoughtfully repurposes those freed-up hours for the work that only they can do, the work that demands their unique judgment. The return is not simply more time, but better attention across their true priorities: people, talent, organization, strategy, and stakeholders.
* *Codifying tacit knowledge*. An expert executive AI translates the mental models, inference patterns, and standards of excellence that have accumulated in and around leaders into a form that the rest of the organization can read, recognize, and apply when a leader is not in the room. This includes how they evaluate options, stress-test decisions, and calibrate the burden of proof. Layered on top is a thinking trail: a time-stamped stream of the leader's insights, questions, and ideas that are captured by the system, integrated into the knowledge graph, and automatically threaded to the relevant decisions.

  With an executive AI, the forms of tacit knowledge that most often go unrecorded, such as a reframe that changes how a problem is posed, a question that redirects an analysis, or a pattern recognized only in retrospect, are captured rather than lost. The act of codification is an intervention in its own right: putting plain language to what good looks like unearths new conversations about standards that many organizations have operated by for years. This repository of named leadership patterns becomes a shared, democratized intellectual resource that informs decision-makers and colleagues throughout the organization. The AI keeps learning from what executives do as they address hundreds of leadership moments: what they look at first, what closes a question, what raises the bar, and what ensures that a decision is ready to be taken.
* *Augmenting decisions in real time*. When a judgment call comes up, the executive AI draws on codified knowledge, the reasoning the leader has been developing around that decision, and a live read of the status of relevant conversations. Together, these inputs sharpen the question the CEO needs answered. These inputs are organized and preserved in a kind of virtual dossier for each decision, a persistent thread with memory and context. By the time the recommendation reaches a leader, the options have been laid out, the trade-offs articulated, and the first questions the leader would likely ask anticipated and addressed. This can clear the predictable questions, so the more meaningful debate over questions with less precedent can be richer and deeper.
* *Sparring*. Leaders can now pressure-test the strategy and the decisions that matter most before action is taken. The executive AI can widen and deepen the range of useful perspectives by allowing executives to draw from the documented thinking patterns of other named leaders. For example, it might show how the organization's deepest expert on a given question would dissect the same decision. It can offer the view of someone with a different vantage point on the business, or a command of how similar choices have failed before, or a sharper eye for risks and blind spots the top team is less likely to see. These are people who hold the company's most specialized knowledge but cannot attend every executive meeting.

  Sparring with these models of alternative thinking creates an always-on peer that can help leaders expand the perspective they bring to a decision. This is only of value, of course, if they construct an operating model in which their personal sparring with the top team enables rich discussion (for example, by creating a "red team–blue team" construct to build a strong case for and against major decisions). Much of a leader's role is not just getting to the right answer, but building consensus around it, and an executive AI can help with both. For example, to better explain to stakeholders why options were assessed, discarded, or chosen, a CEO could spar with synthetic personas of a board or an investor base, or test communications for different audiences in advance.
* *Closing the learning loop*. After every important decision and meeting, the executive AI constructs a short, structured debrief that captures what mattered, what was missing, and what should change. Those signals refine the system and build a behavioral portrait of the leader that is honest in ways that are harder for executives constrained by familiarity or perceived hierarchy to accomplish on their own. The model tracks such things as whether relational decisions are being delegated under pressure, and whether the narrative the CEO carries to the board has been sufficiently stress-tested. Leaders can reflect on and learn from what's been captured to improve their responses to the most persistent challenges.

The method that makes this possible is not self-reporting. In our experience, asking leaders to describe their own thinking is less revealing than seeing them in action. An executive AI relies on elicitation techniques such as shadowing and reaction interviews, which capture how a leader actually operates: what they probe first and what they ignore, whom they consult before a major call, how they deliver hard feedback, when they step in and when they let a team find its own way, and how they read a room before weighing in.

Over time, that behavioral record becomes more accurate than any interview could be, and more useful. It can bolster apprenticeship, which may have been deprioritized as calendars became challenging. And it helps the CEO cement their legacy by handing the organization to a new leader who is even better than they are and is fully informed about the past decision-making process.

## Efficiency and effectiveness go hand in hand

A 12-year Harvard Business School study by Michael Porter and Nitin Nohria, which remains the most detailed investigation of CEO time use, found that the average CEO works 62.5 hours a week. However, less than half of that time advances strategic priorities.

A strong AI for executives can increase a leader's number of meaningful hours. It helps build an efficient workflow around parallel time-based metrics: rework eliminated, meeting time compressed, and critical hours reclaimed each week and redeployed toward the work only the leader can do. In our experience, decision support and prioritization begin returning usable time within the first month, and the operating model itself is ready to be recalibrated after two to three months of tuning. This drives quick, meaningful wins.

The best leaders will use this as a springboard to improve their effectiveness. Most CEOs describe to us a peculiar kind of exhaustion that has very little to do with the number of hours worked. It is the exhaustion of performing leadership in short bursts between escalations and constant context-switching, when what they seek is the actual work of leading. When the operating model works, executives regain the ability to lead in a more sustainable way: in the strategic choices only they can make, in the time with the board and the frontline, in the time to develop the people around them, and in the space to keep growing as leaders themselves. The leader improves as the same system that unlocks their reasoning also reflects it back, showing where their thinking holds, where it falls short, and where it needs to evolve.

A strong AI is also a springboard for encouraging progress throughout the organization. When a leader's standards become more legible across the company, a measurable increase in effectiveness develops. Decisions arrive ready to be debated on the first pass, with options already framed, trade-offs drawn out, and the asks made more clearly. The sparring that pressure-tests decisions becomes sharper and less encumbered by distractions. Over time, the collective calibration of what good looks like accelerates across every level of the organization. These gains also improve the AI capability itself as it learns from widespread use and review. Each reinforces the other: as the organization learns, the system sharpens, and as the system sharpens, the organization learns faster, creating a flywheel effect.

## Designing against the downside

A system like this can fail in various ways. As leaders use and deploy an executive AI, they must be alert to potential pitfalls and agile enough to respond swiftly. A lack of attention may result in nothing more than a faster, more efficient version of the existing operating model. This can make things worse. Output volumes may rise, but the standards behind the work stay implicit and unshared.

Leaders must watch for at least four kinds of pitfalls, in our experience:

* *Refill:* An executive AI can open more hours on a CEO's calendar. Inertia and reliance on a standard-issue operating model can refill those reclaimed hours quickly with equally marginal work. The operating model must shift to protect those hours for rest and reflection. Without that, decision-making may not improve.
* *Convergence:* If both the CEO and the top team calibrate against the executive AI, curveballs and anomalous ideas that change outcomes can get filtered out before anyone sees them. The corrective is to instrument the system for dissent: record what was rejected and why, require an outside input on major decisions, and watch for variance rather than only for agreement. A related concern is insularity. Executives who lean hardest into AI might think they need their teams less—a sparring partner that replaces debate among teams, rather than sharpening it, needs to be thought about differently.
* *Inheritance:* A system tuned to one leader's standard can hand a successor dogma. The record must hold the conditions and reasoning behind a standard, not only its conclusions. And the new leader must treat this as revisable from the first day of their tenure.
* *Governance:* A system like this raises important governance questions that must be worked through as part of building sustainable organizational learning in a risk-aware way. These include what gets captured and what is deliberately excluded, how long it is retained, who inside the company can read which layer, and how the whole of it is treated under privilege and in discovery.

## How to start

In our experience, what works is proof before scale. The first sprints build a baseline from real decisions, using the same three-to-six-month record of materials, meetings, memos, and board packs described above. From that foundation, escalation thresholds (the bar at which decisions reach a leader or are resolved beforehand) get defined, recurring meetings are observed across multiple cycles, and the system is calibrated to the rhythms of the actual executive office rather than a theoretical one (see case study, "From 1,800 meetings to a full-blown learning organization").

## Case study: From 1,800 meetings to a full-blown learning organization

**The starting point** was a calendar that had run out of control. The CEO of a complex organization had logged 1,800 meetings in the previous year. Nearly 500 of them generated no decisions and required follow-ups, with the resulting volume of coordination crowding out meaningful work.

Our partnership with their team began with observations and shadowing, drawing on three to six months of decision materials, meetings, memos, and board packs. The goal was to see how leaders made decisions in practice: what they probed first, what closed a question, and what opened a new one. Multiple elicitation techniques were used to reverse-engineer the tacit knowledge and standards that guided the decisions. From that foundation, three capabilities were stood up in sequence.

The first was a meeting preparation system oriented around decision readiness and discovery of content rather than scheduling. Each day surfaced not just what was on the agenda, but how ready each item was for a decision. The second was a codification layer, translating the CEO's inference patterns and standards into a form that the AI and the broader organization could access, apply, and maintain as a living and evolving repository. The third was a feedback mechanism that allowed the broader organization to draw on the codified standard ahead of high-stakes conversations, rather than discovering it after the fact. These are three of the five parts described above: attention, codification, and the learning loop. They were built in sequence as part of the advancing capability.

Within the first few months, critical indicators of quality and effectiveness had begun to move. Thirty percent more decisions arrived ready on the first pass. Escalations to the CEO dropped sharply. And the organization accumulated roughly 100 reusable knowledge assets, codifying standards that had previously remained opaque. Just as telling was where the CEO's reclaimed hours went—to developing the internal candidates who could one day succeed them, to time in the field with frontline teams, and to other critical work that cannot be delegated.

There is no standard scorecard for an executive AI. Each organization needs one built around its own decisions and leaders, and in our experience, the most useful measures fall into two groups. Flow measures show whether work moves differently through the company. Examples include critical-path latency (the time decisions spend waiting on a senior calendar), first-time decision rate (the share of items decided at first presentation rather than deferred), delegation depth (the share of decisions closed below committee because the standard is explicit), and commitment closure (the share of commitments made in the room that are actually closed). Personal measures show whether the leader's own work has changed. Examples include judgment ratio (the share of hours spent on work only the leader can do), compounding ratio (how often questions are answered from what is already known rather than researched again), and fragmentation index (the share of thinking time that comes in unbroken blocks).

When a measure stalls, it usually points to its own remedy. Decisions still deferred at the first presentation suggest the codified standard is incomplete, and each deferral becomes a rule to add. Decisions still escalating suggest thresholds were set too cautiously or are not yet trusted. Reclaimed hours that refill with marginal work signal that the operating model, not the system, needs to change. And the two groups need to move together: Flow gains alone mean the organization has changed, but the leader has not, while personal gains alone mean the leader has more time, but the company works as before.

## Calibrated to every season

A developing leader benefits from a developing modern executive AI system. McKinsey's *A CEO for All Seasons* synthesizes the learning arc of a CEO into four phases: stepping up, starting strong, staying ahead, and sending it forward. Each phase carries its own characteristic set of imperatives, and a skilled leader will make the most of a powerful AI to manage the challenges specific to each stage of their journey.

A leader stepping up will use the system to pressure-test their narrative and surface what the board will most be looking for from top talent: the unstated priorities and standards that sit beneath the formal mandate. A leader who starts strong will engage in deep listening, synthesize views across the organization, and make their standards explicit early enough for them to be examined, debated, and refined. A leader staying ahead will tune the executive AI to inject outside perspective into a system that naturally gravitates toward confirmation bias by their middle years. And a leader in the sending-it-forward phase needs to orient the system toward executive development and transfer by getting the nuances of their own learning and reasoning into the organization before the transition arrives. At every step of the way, leaders can use AI to prepare for what comes next and to surface the capabilities and questions that the following season will demand.

The executive AI's architecture stays constant across all four seasons, but it is never limited by where a leader is on their journey. At every stage, it is a powerful tool for any leader looking to drive bigger and bolder progress.

AI transformation strategies often overlook the fact that role modeling is one of the most powerful levers for change. A CEO can mandate adoption through some combination of tools, training, and productivity targets. But what they cannot mandate is the gradient of human development that occurs when people witness, repeatedly and at close range, what outstanding judgment in action looks like. A leader using AI to make their thinking visible does not just improve their own performance. They create a new kind of organizational learning, one where the standard travels faster than any training program could carry it. The organization develops not because it was told to, but because it was shown how.

CEOs are unlikely to be remembered for becoming the most efficient users of AI. What they will be remembered for is something more consequential: that they built effective organizations and a cadre of leaders who learned faster, calibrated better, and developed in ways that classic mandates could not have produced alone. The most valuable knowledge in any organization lives below the level of language. Leading CEOs are now using AI to bring this to the surface. In so doing, they are not just building better organizations but are changing what it means to lead one.
