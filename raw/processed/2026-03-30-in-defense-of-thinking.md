# In Defense of Thinking

Ten years after publishing Deep Work, Cal Newport revisits his central argument in a New York Times essay: "The problems I focused on in Deep Work, and in my writing since, have been getting steadily worse. In 2016 my main concern was helping people find enough free time for deep work. Today I think we're rapidly losing the ability to think deeply at all, regardless of how much space we can find in our schedules for these efforts."

Distractions intensified over the past decade with instant messaging tools like Slack and low-friction video meetings like Zoom. Social media, generally still admired when Deep Work came out, has morphed into an "addictive TikTok-ified slurry of optimized brain rot." New AI tools now offer quick-fix shortcuts to whatever intellectually engaging work remains.

Newport calls for "a revolution in defense of thinking," suggesting concrete actions:

- Stop consuming social media, treated as digital junk food to eliminate from a healthy content diet.
- Keep phones plugged in and charging at home instead of on your person.
- Push for policy (citing Australia's example) to ban social media for kids.
- Build work cultures where phones and laptops stay out of meetings, with collaboration strategies that don't require constant messaging.
- Stop vague demands to "use AI" and instead carefully integrate tools only where they make people smarter, not just busier.

"I'm done ceding my brain — the core of all that makes me who I am — to the financial interests of a small number of technology billionaires or the shortsighted conveniences of hyperactive communication styles," Newport writes. "It's time to move past fretting about our slide into the cognitive shallows and decide to actually do something about it."
