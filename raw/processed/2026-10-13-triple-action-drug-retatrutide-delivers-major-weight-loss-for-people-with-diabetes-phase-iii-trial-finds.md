# Triple-action drug retatrutide delivers major weight loss for people with diabetes, Phase III trial finds

A recent Phase III clinical trial, [TRIUMPH-2](https://linkinghub.elsevier.com/retrieve/pii/S0140673626018611), evaluated the efficacy and safety of retatrutide, a triple receptor agonist and experimental once-weekly injection being developed to treat obesity and type 2 diabetes. What sets it apart from drugs such as semaglutide (Wegovy), which targets one hormone, and tirzepatide (Zepbound), which targets two, is its ability to activate three hormone receptors at once.

Developed by Eli Lilly, retatrutide mimics GLP-1, which lowers blood sugar and promotes fullness, and GIP, which enhances the body's insulin response and helps reduce body weight alongside GLP-1. It also activates glucagon receptors, adding a third mechanism that increases energy expenditure and metabolic activity, helping the body burn more energy.

After 80 weeks of once-weekly injections, 1,152 adults living with both obesity and type 2 diabetes saw major changes on the scale. Those on the highest dose lost an average of 18.8% of their body weight, roughly 20.7 kg (about 45.5 pounds), compared with 5.1% in the placebo group. The loss was even bigger for those who started with a BMI of 35 or higher, who lost an average of 21.9% of their body weight on the 12 mg dose.

The findings are published in *The Lancet*.

## Overcoming the body's resistance

Weight loss isn't always about meeting existing beauty standards. It can be medically necessary, since weight gain is linked to many metabolic and cardiovascular conditions. Studies show that even a modest weight loss of 5–10% can lower both systolic and diastolic blood pressure and raise good cholesterol.

For people with type 2 diabetes, losing weight has historically been a tougher challenge than for those without the condition. The advent of glucagon-like peptide-1 receptor agonists, better known as GLP-1 agonists, has helped change that, making weight loss more achievable for people with obesity and type 2 diabetes.

Medicines such as semaglutide and tirzepatide improve weight and blood sugar, but they rarely achieve an average weight loss greater than 15% in people with type 2 diabetes. In previous trials of retatrutide, researchers observed up to 25% weight loss among participants.

The drug's third target—glucagon receptor agonism—alongside GLP-1 and GIP may explain its ability to overcome the body's resistance and unlock greater weight loss for people with diabetes.

The 80-week TRIUMPH-2 trial was designed to put once-weekly retatrutide to the test in adults living with both obesity and type 2 diabetes. Researchers wanted to find out whether it could push weight loss beyond previous limits while safely improving blood sugar control, overall cardiometabolic health and physical quality of life.

They designed a randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial in which neither the participants nor medical staff knew who received retatrutide and who received a placebo. Participants were randomly assigned to receive retatrutide at 4 mg, 9 mg or 12 mg weekly, or a weekly placebo. To help the body adjust, retatrutide dosing started at 2 mg and increased every four weeks until participants reached their target dose.

## Outcomes of triple-action treatment

After 80 weeks, participants taking retatrutide lost an average of 11.9%, 16.8% and 18.8% of their body weight as the dose increased, compared with 5.1% in the placebo group. At the highest dose, 12 mg, 75% of participants shed at least 10% of their body weight, while nearly one-third of participants lost 25% or more, a level of weight loss comparable with bariatric surgery.

The benefits extended beyond weight loss. Retatrutide reduced HbA1c, a marker of long-term blood sugar control, by 1.4–1.5 percentage points, compared with 0.45 percentage points with placebo. Participants saw improvements in several cardiovascular risk markers, as well as reductions in waist circumference, systolic blood pressure and triglyceride levels.

The side effects seen in participants were mostly digestive, with diarrhea in up to 34% of participants, nausea in up to 28% and vomiting in up to 16%. Adverse events led 8–12% of participants in the two higher-dose groups to discontinue treatment, compared with 5% in the placebo group.

Based on the trial results, retatrutide looks promising for treating obesity in people with type 2 diabetes, but more research is needed to find out whether those benefits extend to other obesity-related complications.
