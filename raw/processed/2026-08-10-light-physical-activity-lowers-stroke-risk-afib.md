# Even Light Physical Activity May Lower Stroke Risk in People With AFib

A study of 87,340 adults from two large Norwegian population studies (Tromsø Study and HUNT) found physical activity was associated with lower rates of stroke and death, regardless of atrial fibrillation (AFib) status. 6,539 participants had AFib at baseline or developed it during follow-up (median 13.5 years; 3,415 strokes, 7,833 deaths recorded). Published in the Journal of the American Heart Association (Aug 5, 2026).

Participants were grouped as inactive (18%), low activity (38%), moderate activity (24%), or high activity (22%). Across the full cohort, low/moderate/high activity were associated with 9%/19%/18% lower stroke risk versus inactivity, and 11%/18%/22% lower all-cause mortality risk — with similar benefits for people with and without AFib. Higher activity was also linked to longer life expectancy in AFib patients: +0.50 years (low activity), +0.66 years (moderate), +1.15 years (high), versus inactivity.

Notably, benefits weren't limited to highly active people — moving from inactivity to even relatively low activity levels was associated with better outcomes. As an observational study, it cannot prove causation; limitations include self-reported exercise levels and no data on anticoagulation treatment.

Experts emphasized exercise should complement, not replace, standard AFib treatment (rate/rhythm control medications, anticoagulants). AFib-related exercise fears (triggering episodes) are largely unfounded for non-elite-endurance-athletes. Practical starting recommendations: begin with a 5-10 minute walk once or twice daily if sedentary; choose an enjoyable activity; prioritize consistency over intensity; use the "talk test" to gauge effort; increase duration before intensity. People with significant AFib symptoms or other cardiovascular conditions should consult a doctor before starting a new exercise program.
