# The Real Reason Most Enterprise AI Never Pays Off

For the past two years, enterprises have raced to adopt AI. Now they are facing a more important, expensive question: why isn't it paying off the way it was supposed to?

The gap between AI adoption and its value is widening. According to Gartner, only 39% of technology leaders are confident their AI investments will improve financial performance. That points to a deeper issue: Companies have adopted AI faster than they've built the foundation to support it.

The real differentiator isn't the AI. It's the investment in data, governance and change management that supports it. Gartner found that organizations with successful AI initiatives invest up to four times more in those areas.

The market is already responding to this shift. Recent moves such as the acquisition of Domo's AI and data platform business by Progress Software highlight how enterprises are prioritizing unified data, analytics, and application platforms as the foundation for trusted, scalable AI.

The pattern behind these findings is consistent: usage has scaled faster than the infrastructure needed to make that usage valuable. Generating an AI output has become nearly free, but trusting the output and proving what it costs to produce is a different story.

## Activity Is Not the Same as Value

Ben Schein, Vice President, AI, and Business Transformation at Progress Software, spent years studying why some enterprise AI deployments compound in value while others generate overly expensive noise. His conclusion is direct: most organizations cannot currently answer a basic question about their own AI usage.

"The honest answer is upstream of almost every explanation companies give themselves," Schein said. "They lose visibility the moment AI spend stops mapping to a workload. Model selection, prompt volume, agent loops, embedded tools, these are all just places where that one failure shows up. Without a shared unit that ties an AI action to a workload, an owner, and an outcome, a company doesn't have an AI strategy. It has a subscription."

According to a recent survey from my company, Prosper Insights & Analytics, more than half of executives and business owners (54%) say they already use generative AI, compared with 36% of employees.

Yet adoption has not translated into trust. Nearly four in 10 Americans say AI still requires human oversight to produce reliable results.

There are nearly six in 10 who are concerned about how AI uses their personal data.

"AI doesn't become valuable when a model gets smarter," Schein said. "It becomes valuable when it's connected to a business and becomes a system of action. We see this across the platform. Companies aren't succeeding because they adopted a better model than their competitors. They're succeeding because they can trust the data the model is working from."

## Why Data Silos Quietly Inflate the Cost of Every AI Initiative

As organizations expand AI across the business, many are finding the biggest obstacle isn't the model but the quality, accessibility, and governance of the data behind it. Without a trusted data foundation, AI becomes harder to scale, more expensive to manage and less reliable.

Jason Longhurst, Senior Director, Product Marketing at Progress Software, said organizations often underestimate the impact of fragmented data. Without a connected, governed data foundation, AI initiatives are more likely to produce inconsistent results, increase operational complexity and fall short of expectations.

"When everyone in an organization uses their own AI assistant to connect to different systems and piece together how their data relates, you don't just get inconsistent answers," he said. "You lose the ability to share that work across a team, and governance and data provenance become almost impossible to maintain."

For Schein, that challenge ultimately comes down to accountability.

"Every AI interaction should carry who triggered it, what workload it belongs to, which datasets it touched, which model ran it, and what it cost," he said. "If a company cannot produce that row for a given output, it doesn't have a cost problem yet. It has a visibility problem, and that's worse, because it compounds silently until someone finally asks for the number and doesn't like the answer."

## What Enterprises Get Wrong About Model Selection

Many organizations still view AI through the lens of model selection. But as competition has intensified, leading AI models have become increasingly interchangeable, with published pricing varying by as much as 30-fold depending on the provider and capability.

Schein argues that competitive advantage comes from matching the right model to the right workload, not simply choosing the most advanced one.

"Portfolio management means capturing that price curve instead of being captured by it," Schein said. "High-volume, repetitive work should run on small models in batch mode. Frontier models should be reserved for the decisions that actually require them. The organizations seeing real returns are disproportionately the ones matching machinery to the job, not the ones chasing the newest release."

The mistake isn't choosing the wrong model; it's treating model selection as the biggest driver of AI value. In reality, that mindset often leads organizations to over-invest in capabilities they rarely use.

## Governance as Infrastructure, Not a Constraint

Governance is often viewed as a barrier to innovation, but research suggests that well-designed governance can help accelerate it. BCG found that companies that centralize AI requests and streamline or automate reviews are 30% more likely to approve the average AI use case within four weeks. As AI becomes more embedded across the enterprise, governance is becoming a prerequisite for turning AI investments into business value.

That shift is changing how organizations approach AI. Increasingly, the focus is moving beyond model selection and toward building a trusted data foundation that supports consistent, reliable outcomes.

For many organizations, that means centralizing structured and unstructured data in a governed environment where AI can operate on trusted information rather than fragmented datasets. The objective isn't simply better AI outputs. It's greater confidence in the decisions those outputs support.

Early results suggest the approach can pay off. Organizations that have centralized performance data report measurable improvements in visibility, efficiency, and business outcomes.

"Organizations often view governance as a constraint, but in practice it's what gives AI the context and control required to operate at enterprise scale," said Ed Keisling, Chief AI Officer, Progress Software. "A model is only as effective as the information, tools, skills and guardrails surrounding it. When organizations can provide trusted context and maintain control over how data is accessed, used, and governed, AI outputs become more reliable, more explainable, and ultimately more valuable."

## The Real Competitive Advantage Left on the Table

The AI conversation has focused on who has the best model, but as models become more accessible, the real difference is the quality of a company's data.

"Enterprises retain value by investing in what vendors cannot ship: their own context," Schein said. "Customer history, taxonomies, operational data, institutional judgment, organized and governed, that's the moat. The same model produces dramatically different values depending on the context it's grounded in, and that context is an asset a company owns. It isn't something it rents."

For business leaders, AI success is no longer defined by adoption alone. The organizations seeing the strongest returns are the ones that can measure AI's impact, understand its cost, and trust its results
