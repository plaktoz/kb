# Penn State study flags AI tutor reasoning gaps

Suman Saha, associate teaching professor of computer science and engineering at Penn State, described early results from an AI tutor engineering education project in a Nov. 13, 2026 question-and-answer article published by Penn State News. An early analysis of the tool's first classroom use, in an upper-level engineering course in spring 2026, found that in 21 of 119 recorded student-AI interactions, the chat showed evidence of reasoning that was absent from the answer the student ultimately submitted.

The tool, called ALAASKA ("Adaptive Learning for All through AI-Powered Student Knowledge Assessment"), reads a student's response, estimates their current understanding, and returns a targeted explanation, question, hint or brief knowledge check rather than a solved answer. Saha said it "does not require an extensive history of student data before providing support," so it can adapt within a single activity. Development began in 2025, building on earlier classroom work with short AI-generated learning aids such as quizzes, flashcards, summaries and videos.

## What the early numbers show

In the spring 2026 pilot, students worked through nine homework assignments and four projects with ALAASKA in the course. Penn State News did not report the number of students, the course's name or number, or how the 119 analyzed interactions were selected from the full pilot. Saha described the 21-of-119 finding as an "early analysis," not a peer-reviewed result, and said instructors "may learn more" by examining the problem-solving process in a chat than by grading only the final submitted answer.

Saha said the biggest challenge generative AI poses for engineering education is that it can make it "easier for students to bypass the reasoning" the field is meant to build, but that AI can instead work as a "learning scaffold rather than an answer generator," particularly in large courses.

## A two-year NSF grant for a controlled comparison

The early pilot is now backed by a two-year, $199,009 National Science Foundation Research Initiation in Engineering Formation grant (award number 2605386). Co-principal investigators are Stephanie Cutler, associate research professor, and Ibukun Osunbunmi, assistant research professor, both at Penn State's Leonhard Center for the Enhancement of Engineering Education.

The funded phase will compare two sections of the same large computing course: one using ALAASKA, the other completing "equivalent activities and reflections" through the course learning management system, with aligned syllabi, assignments and rubrics. Saha said the design "tests whether that conversational support, not AI use in general, improves learning," while acknowledging it "does not assume students in the comparison section never use external AI tools." Planned data sources include pre- and post-surveys, course performance records, interaction logs, and qualitative coding of student reflections and selected conversations.

Saha said the broader goal is to produce design guidance, assessment tools and analysis methods that other instructors can adapt, shared initially through faculty-development work at the Leonhard Center, with testing planned across more courses, Penn State campuses and partner institutions.

Penn State's comparison design echoes findings elsewhere in AI tutoring research. Microsoft Research's StudentSim project trains AI tutors against simulated students specifically to probe how tutoring strategies affect learning before a live classroom trial, while a University of Maryland trial found a different AI tutoring tool was linked to lower college grades when used without structured oversight, and a two-year trial of Khanmigo found only small math gains. Together, the studies point toward the same open question ALAASKA's funded comparison is built to test: whether structured, mistake-aware AI support changes learning outcomes, not just whether AI tutoring exists.

## Background

Penn State's Leonhard Center for the Enhancement of Engineering Education, based in the university's College of Engineering, supports instructional research and faculty development across the college's academic units; its research-active staff include the two co-principal investigators on this grant. The National Science Foundation's Research Initiation in Engineering Formation program funds early-career and research-initiation projects that study how engineers are educated and trained, rather than funding engineering research itself. Saha's team began developing AI-assisted classroom activities in 2025 before building ALAASKA and deploying it for the first time in the spring 2026 course now under study, making this NSF award the first dedicated funding to formally evaluate the tool's effect on learning.
