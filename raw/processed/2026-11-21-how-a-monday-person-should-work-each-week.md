# How a Monday Person Should Work Each Week

I had a hunch I was a Monday person. Paying attention to how I like to work agreed.

I already liked to work this way: protect early-week deep work, and concentrate my meetings on Wednesday.

Would I tell you to copy my week? No. Use your own recurring pattern. This is my account of how I like to plan. I’m no researcher. I would not call it a controlled study, or proof that a personal preference measures productivity.

If you already use a wearable, export your data and explore that file yourself. Keep unrestricted AI access away from the sensitive health account. If you don’t wear anything, compare one week of calendar activity with a simple energy log. Note the hours that repeat.

## Start with energy, then the immovable stuff

So which layer do you usually plan first?

I plan in three layers, in this order. In a Q&A I clarified it. Identifying the high-energy window comes first. Assigning work comes later.

Energy. Name when you naturally have the most energy and concentration. Mine is early in the week. Mondays are strong. I keep deeper work off Wednesdays.

Fixed commitments. Then account for commitments that cannot move. Energy preferences do not override them.

Focus blocks. Place focused work as close to those strongest available hours as you can. Then fill what’s left with flexible meetings and errands.

Map the three layers onto a weekly calendar.

## Reserve the best hours before the week fills in

Monday and Tuesday mornings on my calendar are recurring blocks from 7 a.m. to noon, unavailable for booking.

During weekly planning I replace the placeholders with specific work. I am not doing uninterrupted deep work for every minute of those mornings. The block reserves the hours. Planning decides what deserves them.

I aim to group meetings on Wednesday. Thursday and Friday stay more flexible. My schedule is not a formula for you.

Reserve your strongest hours by default. Then decide what deserves them.

Create one recurring reservation for your best window. Assign it a specific purpose during weekly planning.

## Give the focus time a real address

Ever finish the week and realize the real work got the leftovers?

Plan around the focus time. If you’re beginning, start with three hours of uninterrupted work per week, in blocks you can keep. If you already have an established practice, my target is one hour per workday. Those are coaching targets I use. I didn’t get them from a study. Your role and your existing commitments decide what you can actually hold.

Reserve next week’s focus blocks before you accept more flexible meetings.

## Make the week resilient

I used to plan every hour. A calendar that only works when nothing changes is fragile.

I design a resilient week now. It protects important work and leaves room for a moved meeting, an interruption, or a low-energy day. Where does your week break if one thing shifts?

I deliberately leave 15- or 30-minute gaps between meetings. I use them to recover, handle follow-up, or absorb overruns. Without those gaps, a small change can force important work into next week. An empty slot is useful capacity.

Review next week’s calendar. Find where one unexpected change would cause a domino effect, and add margin there. Start with one event called Buffer. Notice how it gets used. I’m describing my week, with no speed measurement attached.

## Four questions, out loud

What drains your energy every week? What deserves more space? What consistently gets skipped? What could be automated or batched with technology or AI?

Which one stings?

I use these four in my weekly review, looking for what repeats. The answers show what to protect and what to remove, reduce, or delegate. Watch for the repeat.

I give ChatGPT the questions and ask it to interview me one at a time. I answer aloud, with no worry about polished wording. Then I ask for a detailed summary or coaching suggestions. The tool organizes my reflection. I still have to know what happened during the week. Confidential details stay out. Naming ChatGPT is not an endorsement.

Try this prompt:

Ask me these questions one at a time: What drains my energy each week? What deserves more space? What consistently gets skipped? What could I automate or batch? After I answer all four, summarize the patterns and suggest one change to test.

Use these four in your next weekly review, then change one thing on the calendar.

## Shorten the block you keep skipping

If an important habit keeps disappearing, shorten it before you abandon it.

Thinking time is my example. When a 30-minute session keeps getting skipped, try a recurring 15-minute session. A smaller block you actually keep can preserve the practice better than an ambitious block you routinely ignore. I’m suggesting the adjustment. Some work still needs the full time.

Find one repeatedly skipped block and reduce its duration for the coming week.

## One follow-up loop, with you still editing

I use AI to move meeting follow-up beyond transcription.

Lindy captures meeting action items and drafts follow-up emails. I review and edit them between calls, before anything goes out. Meeting tasks go into Airtable. Another agent checks them twice a day and messages me on Slack about upcoming work. I reply with direction so the agent can handle preparation such as research, templates, or draft materials, while I still make time for final touches.

Start with one bounded workflow. Meeting capture, then a follow-up email draft you review. Add task storage and agent check-ins after that. Keep confidential work details out.
