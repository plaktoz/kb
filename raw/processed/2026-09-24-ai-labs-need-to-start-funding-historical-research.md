# AI Labs Need to Start Funding Historical Research

Current frontier AI models (GPT-6 Sol and Opus 5.5) have reached a threshold where pairing them with professional historians could yield genuine advances in historical knowledge — something that wasn't true even a year prior.

The most "tractable" historical problems for AI involve cryptography, tracing texts across translations, and connecting findings siloed in niche subfields. Key criteria include fully digitized sources, multilingual reasoning demands, and problems that can be clearly verified or falsified.

## Case Studies

**Enigma decryption:** GPT-6 Astra cracked a previously unsolved 1941 German message, apparently by discovering archival notes about Bundesarchiv radio collections that even expert researchers couldn't fully trace.

**John Dee's *Liber Loagaeth*:** Astra concluded the supposedly coded manuscript is largely nonsense syllables, while detecting increased repetition after a specific date — suggesting the scryer Kelley grew lazier over time.

**Alchemical knowledge transmission:** Opus 5.5 analyzed Samuel Hartlib's papers and identified a likely source Newton drew upon, noting both Newton and Hartlib used *different* anagram-codes for the same ingredient, Hungarian vitriol.

**Charles V cipher letters:** Opus 5.5 partially deciphered two 16th-century Spanish letters — only to discover both had already been decoded (one in the 1530s, one in 1916), which nonetheless validated the model's capabilities.

## Three Proposed Interventions

1. Digitize and freely open archival manuscripts
2. Provide historians with free API/compute access
3. Historians should collaboratively identify "millennium problems" analogous to mathematics

AI risks cognitive offloading when used as a writing substitute, but when expanding research beyond any single person's horizon, it becomes genuinely "mind-expanding."
