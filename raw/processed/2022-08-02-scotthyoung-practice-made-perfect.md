# Practice Made Perfect: The 10 Keys to Optimize Improvement

Getting good at anything requires practice, but not all practice is equal — "practice doesn't make perfect, but permanent." Ten research-backed strategies improve the rate of skill improvement:

1. **Start with examples.** Problem-solving without guidance risks practicing a poor strategy or missing the underlying pattern because attention is on completing the goal. Study worked examples first.
2. **Retrieve, don't reread.** Testing yourself with a closed book (free recall, flashcards, practice questions) is more effective than re-reading. Alternate between attempting a problem and reviewing the solution, shifting toward more independent problem-solving with experience.
3. **Spread out your sessions.** The spacing effect means the same total study time produces far more durable memories when spread across days rather than crammed.
4. **Mix up your practice.** Blocked practice (all Unit 1, then Unit 2) lets the brain predict problem types too easily. Interleaving practice better approximates how problems appear in real life.
5. **Put confusing examples side-by-side.** When two situations get mixed up, compare them directly to see what distinguishes them rather than hoping repetition resolves the confusion.
6. **Use self-explanations.** Walk through and explain each step of a new method to yourself; understanding, not just memorization, is what lets you adapt when a question's format changes.
7. **Analyze your past performance.** For skills at the edge of your ability, you can't simultaneously perform and monitor — record yourself and review afterward.
8. **Use the 85% Rule for sliding difficulty.** For tasks with a clear correct/incorrect distinction, aim for roughly 85% accuracy — hard enough to challenge, not so hard it's just frustrating.
9. **Improve your feedback.** Direct feedback (the actual result) is more accurate; indirect feedback (others' commentary) is more constructive. Seek both.
10. **Match your practice to real life.** Skill transfer is narrower than people assume. Get clear on the actual real-world application, and for ambiguous skills, move back and forth between deliberate practice and doing the real thing.
