# NT-proBNP Blood Marker May Help Identify Older Adults at Higher Risk of Heart Disease

A study published in August found that elevated levels of N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP) — a heart stress marker measurable via blood test — may signal increased cardiovascular risk in older adults with no prior heart disease history.

## Study Details

The research examined 8,454 participants (mean age 78) who lacked prior cardiovascular disease. NT-proBNP levels were measured at baseline and again at three years.

- **10.6%** developed *incident* heart stress (none at baseline, elevated at year three)
- **15.1%** had *sustained* heart stress (elevated at both time points)
- Both groups showed higher rates of cardiovascular events and mortality
- After an 8-year follow-up, adjusted absolute cardiovascular disease risk was **16.5%** (incident group) and **15.8%** (sustained group)

## Expert Perspectives

Cardiologist Dr. Karishma Patwa noted the findings suggest cardiovascular risk "may be dynamic," but cautioned the study doesn't demonstrate that lowering NT-proBNP itself reduces risk — calling it "a promising prognostic finding rather than a treatment target."

Dr. Kaustubh Dabhadkar emphasized that heart failure is a clinical diagnosis and that an elevated NT-proBNP reading without accompanying symptoms or signs "may not be meaningful" from a cardiac standpoint. He also raised concerns about unnecessary health anxiety when patients encounter elevated results without clinical context.

## Limitations

- **98% of participants were white**, limiting demographic generalizability
- NT-proBNP can be elevated due to aging, kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, and other non-cardiac causes
- Levels can read falsely low in obesity or with certain medications
- No current evidence that screening leads to improved outcomes

## Current Role in Screening

Experts agree the test is not yet ready for routine screening. Patwa noted it could eventually play a role in cardiovascular risk assessment, but said "there is not yet enough evidence to recommend screening" given uncertainty around whether interventions improve outcomes.

Dabhadkar highlighted one established use case: monitoring patients on cardiotoxic chemotherapy, where early NT-proBNP changes can prompt treatment adjustments before irreversible damage occurs.

If elevated levels are detected, recommended follow-up includes detailed patient history, physical examination, and an echocardiogram to assess heart function and rule out valve issues.
