# FDA Clears First Single-Biomarker Blood Test for Alzheimer's

On August 24, 2026, the FDA cleared a new single-biomarker blood test — the **Elecsys® Phospho-Tau (217P) Plasma (pTau217)** — developed collaboratively by **Roche and Eli Lilly**, to help detect signs of Alzheimer's disease in adults aged 55+ showing symptoms of cognitive decline.

## How It Works

The test detects phosphorylated tau 217 in blood, one of the earliest specific indicators of Alzheimer's. According to neurologist Peter Gliebus, MD, it "rises early in the disease and remains mostly normal in other neurodegenerative conditions." Though a tau marker, it closely correlates with brain amyloid accumulation.

Results are categorized as **positive, intermediate, or negative** for amyloid pathology, using validated cutoffs applicable across both primary and specialty care settings.

## What Sets It Apart

Previous FDA-cleared tests include:
- The **Lumipulse G pTau217/β-amyloid 1-42 Plasma Ratio** (cleared May 2025) — measures two biomarkers
- The **Elecsys pTau181** (cleared 2025) — primarily rules *out* Alzheimer's pathology in primary care

The new pTau217 test is a single-biomarker assay supporting both **rule-in and rule-out** assessment from one blood draw, using the same cutoffs across care settings.

Laura Nisenbaum, PhD (ADDF), noted: "Just over a year ago, there were no FDA-cleared blood tests for Alzheimer's — now there are four."

## Access and Availability

The test is a standard blood draw, intended to run on Roche's existing laboratory infrastructure. It must be ordered by a physician and is **not available for self-ordering**. It is cleared only for symptomatic adults 55+, not as a general screening tool for healthy individuals.

## Impact on Diagnosis

Roughly 75% of dementia cases remain undiagnosed, largely because confirmatory methods like PET scans and spinal taps are expensive, invasive, and limited to specialized centers. Gliebus called this clearance "a significant inflection point" — the first test able to both confirm and exclude amyloid pathology from a single sample on widely available lab equipment.

Clinical neuropsychologist Megan Glenn, PsyD, emphasized the test "supports a careful clinical evaluation and does not replace a comprehensive assessment by a qualified professional."

## Limitations

Internist Dung Trinh, MD, cautioned that the test:
- Is **not a stand-alone diagnostic**
- Has not been validated for predicting future dementia in asymptomatic individuals
- Has not been established for monitoring treatment response
- Leaves an unclear path for patients with **intermediate results**

He stressed that positive biomarker findings must still be interpreted alongside clinical context, disease stage, contributing conditions, and appropriate care planning.
