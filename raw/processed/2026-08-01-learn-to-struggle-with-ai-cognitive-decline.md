# Learn to 'struggle' with AI to fight cognitive decline

Singapore's education sector is entering a second wave of the AI debate: not academic-integrity bans, but concern over AI's long-term cognitive impact. SUTD engineering student Puah Joon Leng deliberately wrestles with practice problems before asking AI to identify his mistakes, saying "when the AI schools me, it will be more impactful." Researchers and educators increasingly view this willingness to struggle — rather than take AI shortcuts — as a precious and endangered trait.

Venky Shankararaman, vice-provost of education at Singapore Management University, warns: "We will not graduate thinkers." He describes students losing confidence not from failing, but from believing AI does everything better than they can. SMU now designates foundational courses where students must demonstrate they can work independently of AI before applying it within their discipline. "Learning happens in that friction," he said. "If you do not go through that struggle and AI produces a nice output straight away, there is danger."

This phenomenon — cognitive offloading — has empirical backing. A 2025 MIT Media Lab EEG study of 54 individuals aged 18–39 found that people writing essays with AI assistance showed measurably lower brain engagement and worse recall of what they had "written" than those writing independently or using a search engine. Separately, a study of 1,000 Turkish high school students found that while AI-tool users submitted better homework, they scored 17% lower on supervised exams with the tools removed, having bypassed the "effortful learning" phase.

An Bo, head of AI at Nanyang Technological University, said: "My biggest concern is that we may be raising a generation that never learns to struggle productively, and struggle is where learning actually happens." Educators like SUTD's Sumbul Khan now have students log every decision to use, reject, or bypass AI output on shared platforms to build accountability. Experts also warn Singapore's real digital divide is shifting from device access to who has been taught to use AI tools critically — a "supercharging" effect happening more outside the classroom than inside it.
