# A protein older than blood circulation could transform cancer immunotherapy

Scientists at Nagoya University discovered that complement protein C3 — evolutionarily ancient, present even in sponges and jellyfish — can prevent immune-suppressing myeloid cells from building up inside tumors, but only when C3 is produced locally within the tumor. C3 circulating in the bloodstream did not influence how well immunotherapy worked.

In mice, when liver-produced (circulating) C3 was reduced by 90%, an anti-PD-1 immunotherapy drug remained just as effective. But when researchers stopped tumor fibroblasts from producing C3 locally, the same treatment became less effective — even though circulating C3 fell by only 9%. Locally produced C3 breaks down into a fragment called iC3b that blocks harmful myeloid cells from entering the tumor, giving immunotherapy a better chance to work.

Researchers recreated this local effect with a drug that mimics C3's blocking function, restoring immunotherapy efficacy in tumors that had previously resisted treatment and significantly extending survival in mice. In human lung cancer samples, patients with higher local (tumor-tissue) C3 levels had better treatment outcomes and survival — about half responded to treatment versus none of the patients with lower local C3 — while bloodstream C3 levels showed no such link. The findings, published in Nature Communications, suggest new therapeutic approaches for cancers that don't naturally produce sufficient local C3, and could help identify which patients are most likely to benefit from immunotherapy.
