# Workers Who Are Most Sure AI Makes Them More Productive Have 4.5 Times the Burnout Risk. A New UCLA Model Shows How Ranking Coworkers Against Each Other Can Turn AI Gains Into Longer Hours.

A 200-person tech company where AI made people busier, a survey of nearly 3,900 workers, and a game-theory paper from UCLA all point the same way: AI doesn't automatically give anyone time back. The survey measured belief, not output, so it can't prove AI causes burnout. The paper explains how it could, and what a manager can change.

4.5x

burnout risk of US workers who strongly agree AI makes them more productive vs those neutral or disagreeing (WebMD Health Services 2026 Workplace and Employee Survey, n = 3,872; self-reported, correlational)

For eight months, Xingqi Maggie Ye, a doctoral researcher at UC Berkeley’s Haas School of Business, spent regular days inside a US technology company of about 200 people. Everyone there had broad access to generative AI tools. She sat in meetings, watched how people moved between tasks and did more than 40 interviews.

She didn’t start out looking for overwork. “We did not start with a fixed hypothesis about whether AI reduces work or increases it,” she told Haas News in February. What she and her co-author, associate professor Aruna Ranganathan, found was that “[e]mployees worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked to do so.”

People sent prompts at lunch, before meetings and in the evening. Some ran several AI agents at once while they reviewed code or sat in meetings. “This created a rhythm where both the human and the machine were constantly in motion,” Ye said.

That was one company. This week, a research brief from UCLA’s Anderson School of Management put that finding next to a large worker survey and a new economic model. Together they make a case I think every manager rolling out AI should hear.

The promise of AI at work, in plain terms, is that it gives people time back. The same report can be done in less time, so people go home earlier or do more interesting work.

So why would the workers who feel most helped by AI also be the most burned out? And is that the tool’s fault, or the way the job is set up around it?

## What the survey actually measured

The survey is the 2026 Workplace and Employee Survey from WebMD Health Services, an employee well-being company. It went into the field in February and reached 3,872 full-time US employees online. About four in five said they already use AI at work.

Among daily AI users, 94% said the tools make them more productive. Then comes the line that’s been quoted ever since: “the most productive AI users carry a burnout risk 4.5 times higher than their peers.”

The report’s own chart shows where that number comes from. Among AI users, 45% of those who strongly agree the tools make them more productive fall in WebMD’s High Risk burnout group, compared with 9% to 10% of those who are neutral, disagree or strongly disagree. Among those who simply agree, it’s 28%. These are self-reported answers from the 80% of respondents who use AI at work, and they show a correlation, not a cause.

Read the body of the report and the definition is narrower than that sentence. The comparison is between “employees who strongly agree that AI makes them more productive” and “those who are neutral or disagree.” Nobody’s output was measured. The group called “most productive” is the group most sure the tool helps them.

That matters. It’s a survey taken at one point in time, so it can show the two things travel together. It can’t show which one causes the other. People in the most demanding jobs may lean on AI the hardest and feel its help the most, and be burning out because the job is demanding. Heavy workloads could be driving both.

WebMD calls it a paradox: “the very employees delivering the greatest productivity gains through AI are simultaneously the most vulnerable to burnout.” I’d put it more carefully. The employees who believe AI helps them most are also the ones reporting the most strain. That’s still worth taking seriously. It’s just a correlation with a self-reported label on it.

## Why it might happen anyway

This is where Auyon Siddiq comes in. He’s an associate professor at UCLA Anderson, and his working paper, “The Rat Race at Work: Technology and Burnout,” dated October 2026, doesn’t use survey data at all. It’s a model, built on game theory, of how people decide how hard to work.

The setup is simple. Better technology makes each hour of work produce more. Working harder raises the risk of burning out, and burnout ends a person’s production. And part of each worker’s pay depends on how they stack up against coworkers.

Under those conditions, Siddiq shows, a better tool can make workers worse off. If everyone gets more productive at once, the bar for staying ahead of your colleagues rises too. So everyone works harder just to hold their place, and nobody actually gets ahead. “Better technology can create a rat race,” the paper says. “Employees work more intensely in anticipation of being compared against more productive coworkers.”

The UCLA Anderson Review’s summary, written by Carla Fried and published Nov. 11, puts the same idea in one line: “As a group, they’d all be better off working less. But with the scoreboard in place, the rat race is on.”

That’s a first-principles answer to why Ye saw people working through lunch without being told to. When the tool makes your time worth more and your rewards depend on beating the person at the next desk, the rational move is to use the extra capacity, not bank it.

Siddiq’s paper points to how common relative ranking is in tech. It notes that in 2025, Meta set a target for teams of 150 or more to place 15% to 20% of employees in its lowest performance category, and that Microsoft and Amazon have used similar systems.

You don’t need a formal forced ranking for the effect to kick in, he argues. “Bonuses, raises, and promotions are often limited,” the paper says, so employees may see that getting ahead depends on outperforming colleagues even when the review system never says so.

The model also has a twist I didn’t expect. If technology keeps getting better, the rat race can cool off. In workplaces where burnout is common, some workers drop out, and the people who simply pace themselves end up ahead by staying in the game. The paper says welfare can “initially decrease in technology quality as competition intensifies, but eventually recover.” That’s a prediction, not a promise, and nobody knows where today’s AI sits on that curve.

## The part about wellness programs

The finding most likely to annoy HR departments is about wellness spending. In the model, a company focused only on profit pushes people harder and then spends more on burnout prevention to keep them working. It still ends up with more burnout than a company that eases the competition in the first place.

There’s a condition on that result, and it’s worth being precise about it. The paper finds this pattern when the productivity gain from technology is modest. When the gain is large, the math flips: easing off becomes expensive, because each hour produces so much, and even the company that cares about its people may end up spending more on burnout prevention than the profit-focused one. As the brief puts it, “even the well-intentioned firm can’t resist the lure of much greater productivity.”

So the model’s warning gets sharper as AI gets better. The bigger the gain per hour, the stronger the pull to push people, whatever the company’s values.

When conditions improve, Siddiq warns, a firm may respond by raising the stakes. In the model, an improvement in working conditions can lead to higher actual burnout once management adjusts its incentives. The UCLA brief’s verdict: “wellness benefits, however generous, don’t necessarily slow the rat race. Changing the scoreboard can.”

Thank goodness, then, for the meditation app.

## Where you’d see it first

If the model is right, the rat race should show up most where three things meet: AI that clearly speeds up the work, pay or promotion that depends on rank, and work that can spill into any hour. That describes a lot of software teams, consulting, sales and law.

It also suggests where it should show up least. Jobs paid by the piece or by fixed targets that don’t move, and jobs where the shift ends when the shift ends, give people less reason to race. A call center worker measured against a fixed handle-time target is in a different game from an engineer ranked against peers each half year, even if both use the same AI tool.

That’s a testable prediction, and it’s one a company could check with its own data in an afternoon: compare hours and burnout among AI users on teams with forced ranking against teams without it.

Each piece of evidence has a clear limit. Ye and Ranganathan studied one company, and an ethnography is designed to explain how something happens, not how often. The WebMD survey is large but cross-sectional and self-reported, run by a company that sells well-being programs. Siddiq’s paper is theory, not measurement.

But they aren’t the only signals. Siddiq cites a study using US time-diary data, by Jiang and co-authors, that found workers in more AI-exposed occupations work longer hours, with stronger effects where AI complements their work. He also cites activity data from about 10,000 AI users at ActivTrak showing email and messaging rose sharply after people adopted AI. I haven’t independently checked those two studies, so I’m reporting them as he describes them.

Put together, the honest summary is this: there’s growing evidence that AI makes many people busier, not freer. The strongest explanation on offer is about pay and ranking, not the software.

## My read

In my opinion, the simplest explanation is the right one here. Work expands to fill a faster tool when people are rewarded for beating each other rather than for finishing the job. AI didn’t invent that. It just made every hour count for more, which raised the stakes of the contest.

That changes who’s responsible. A worker can’t opt out of a rat race alone without falling behind. A manager can change the rules of the race. That’s why I think Siddiq’s recommendations matter more than any burnout statistic.

He suggests easing quotas that force managers to label a fixed share of people as low performers, giving more weight to things like collaboration, and, when a company improves working conditions, committing not to raise performance targets at the same time. None of that costs money. All of it costs a manager the comfort of a simple scoreboard.

The honest limit: nobody has yet tested Siddiq’s model against data from a company that changed its ranking system after rolling out AI. That’s the study I’d most like to read.

## Back at the 200-person company

Ye and Ranganathan didn’t tell the company to take the AI away. They proposed what they call an “AI practice”: deliberate pauses before big decisions, batching non-urgent AI output instead of reacting to every result as it lands, and protected time for people to talk to each other. “The intention is not to slow innovation,” Ye said, “but to ensure that productivity gains remain aligned, thoughtful, and sustainable over time.”

What struck her most, she said, was a gap between moments and months. In the moment, people described momentum and expanded capability. Looking back, “[t]hey described feeling busier, more stretched, or less able to fully disconnect.”

My recommendation, if you manage people who use AI: before you set next year’s targets, write down what you’ll do with the time AI saves your team. If the answer is “raise the bar,” you’ve chosen the rat race. If you’re going to raise it anyway, change how you rank people first, so the extra capacity shows up as better work and not just longer days.
