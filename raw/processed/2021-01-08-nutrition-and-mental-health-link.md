# Nutrition and mental health: Is there a link?

Nutritional psychiatry is an emerging area of research looking at the role of nutrition in the development and treatment of mental health problems, asking whether diet helps prevent mental health conditions and whether nutrition interventions help treat them.

## Preventing mental health conditions

A review of 21 studies from 10 countries found a healthful dietary pattern — high in fruit, vegetables, whole grains, olive oil, fish, low-fat dairy, and antioxidants, low in animal foods — was associated with reduced depression risk, while a Western-style diet (red/processed meat, refined grains, sweets, high-fat dairy) was linked to significantly increased risk. An older review found high compliance with a Mediterranean diet associated with a 32% reduced risk of depression. A study of adults over 50 found a link between higher anxiety and diets high in saturated fat and added sugars; similar associations appear in children and teens. Observational studies can show association but not prove cause and effect.

## Treating mental health conditions

The SMILES trial, one of the first randomized controlled trials on diet and depression treatment, gave 67 people with moderate-to-severe depression either dietary counseling (a Mediterranean-style diet) or social support. The diet group had significantly greater improvement in depression symptoms — 32% achieved remission versus 8% in the control group — though it was a small, short-term study. Not all research agrees: a study of 1,025 adults found no significant difference in depressive episodes from a multinutrient supplement and behavioral activation after 12 months, while a meta-analysis of 16 RCTs found dietary interventions significantly reduced depression symptoms but not anxiety.

## Supplements

Low levels of folate, magnesium, iron, zinc, and vitamins B6, B12, and D have been linked to worsening mood and depression risk, but evidence is inconclusive on whether supplementing beyond adequate levels helps. Omega-3 supplements have shown effectiveness for anxiety and depression in some reviews, though it's unclear whether that holds for people who already get adequate omega-3 intake.

## Why nutrition may matter: three theories

Inflammation — diets high in anti-inflammatory foods (fruits, vegetables, whole grains, healthful fats) are associated with reduced depression risk. Gut microbiome — healthy gut bacteria produce about 90% of the neurotransmitter serotonin, and a healthy microbiome is linked to lower depression rates. Indirect effects — people with healthful diets may also engage in more exercise, better sleep, and less smoking, all independently linked to better mental health.

Overall, the take-home message is that while more research is needed, current studies suggest food choices may have some influence over mental health — but diet is just one piece of a much more complex picture, and anyone with depression or anxiety symptoms should work with a healthcare provider.
