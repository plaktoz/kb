# Meditation techniques: How to meditate for stress, sleep, and focus

Meditation is a family of mind-body practices that focus attention to calm the nervous system and support well-being. Consistent daily practice can produce modest improvements in stress, anxiety, sleep, mood, and attention, though it isn't a substitute for medical or psychological care.

Meditation and mindfulness are related but distinct: meditation is structured practice time, while mindfulness — the state of being present and aware without judgment — is the quality of attention meditation cultivates, which can also be practiced throughout the day outside formal sessions.

Evidence-based benefits, per Harvard Health: mindfulness-based programs are associated with improvements in anxiety, stress, and mood; a meta-analysis of 30 studies found a small but noticeable reduction in chronic pain from mindfulness meditation; mindfulness-based stress reduction (MBSR) has improved symptoms in irritable bowel syndrome; and a review of 18 studies (1,654 participants) found mindfulness meditation improved sleep quality more than educational approaches, though not more than established treatments like CBT or exercise. Regular meditation is also associated with changes in brain structure and function in regions tied to attention, learning, and emotional regulation.

Dr. Darshan Mehta of Harvard's Benson-Henry Institute for Mind Body Medicine recommends starting with just five minutes a day, most days of the week, building gradually toward 10–20 minutes — consistency matters more than session length. Early benefits tend to be subtle: more space between a stressful event and your reaction, or an easier time falling back asleep. On handling distracting thoughts, Mehta notes the goal isn't to stop thinking but to change your relationship to thoughts — noticing when the mind wanders and gently returning attention to an anchor (like the breath) is itself the successful "rep," not a failure.

Simple techniques covered: diaphragmatic (belly) breathing, box breathing (inhale/hold/exhale/hold for a count of 4 each), a body scan for releasing physical tension (10–15 minutes, good before sleep), and moving meditation through yoga or tai chi — the latter linked in research to improved balance and reduced stress, particularly in older adults.

Mehta's cautions: don't use meditation in place of medical or mental health care or to delay diagnosis/treatment; check an instructor's training if joining a structured program; tell your care team about any complementary practices you use; and pause and consult a provider if meditation seems to intensify distress.
