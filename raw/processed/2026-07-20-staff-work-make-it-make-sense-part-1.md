# Staff Work, Part 1: Make It Make Sense Before It Goes Up

source_url: https://zixianchen.com/blog/staff-work-make-it-make-sense-before-it-goes-up

---

This is the first of three posts collecting accumulated advice on staff work — the thinking, analysis, and communication that help someone understand an issue and make a decision. This post covers thinking through the issue, communicating it upwards, and making a recommendation.

Great officers are good at the small, large, short, and long stuff — they can frame a difficult issue, answer a two-line query, prepare a useful deck, and still notice the wrong annex was attached. They make decisions easier to reach, and where there's genuine controversy, they make the disagreement, trade-offs, and consequences apparent enough for someone to decide.

## A. Before you begin

**Start with the actual problem.** Many papers begin with a solution because someone already wants to build, buy, change, or reorganize something, and the problem statement gets written backwards to justify the proposed answer. A useful problem statement should help distinguish between options — if every possible solution seems to fit, the problem is probably still too broad or vague.

**Work out where the levers are.** A paper can describe a lot of pain and still say little about what anyone can do. Who owns the policy, controls the budget, process, or system? "We need to work closely together to sort out X" is often what remains after a paper has described a problem without identifying who needs to decide or change something.

**Add something of your own.** Don't be a postbox — compiling input from subject matter experts, arranging it nicely, and calling that "your work" isn't enough. Contribution should appear in judgement: which input matters, where inputs conflict, what perspective is missing, what you recommend after considering everything.

**Keep real disagreements visible.** It used to be common practice to force agreement before sending something up, and disagreeing in a high-level meeting was bad form. That's no longer the norm — nowadays disagreement should be explained: where it sits, why it matters to the decision, whether it concerns facts or acceptable risk or who bears the cost. A recommendation can still be made, but the decision-maker should understand the strongest case against it.

**Context, context, context.** Policies and earlier decisions were usually made by people in circumstances that made sense at the time, but circumstances change while the policies and requirements survive. Ask what problem a rule was meant to solve and whether the assumptions still hold — some decisions are current and deliberate, others have simply been copied forward because nobody went back to ask why the original requirement existed.

**Think one question ahead.** If something is urgent, be ready to explain what happens if it's delayed. If a position is defensible, know the precedent. If recommending an exception, consider what it does to future cases. Read the recommendation as the approver would, then as the person who has to carry it out — they may care about different things. Going too far in this direction earns the "kiasu, jumpy, over-staffing" label, so judgement matters.

## B. Communicating upwards

**Point first, history later.** Officers often explain issues in the order they discovered them, leaving the boss unsure why they're being spoken to until the end. Start with the point — "we need to delay the submission by one week because Finance and Legal disagree on X" — then give the history that matters. This is essentially "Bottom Line Up Front": tell the answer or recommendation first, then the reasons in the order needed.

**Give just enough information.** Unloading everything creates noise that makes it hard to assess the situation; too little creates inefficient back-and-forth and blind spots. Include information that could change the view — a relevant precedent, uncertainty in evidence, a non-obvious trade-off, or why the obvious alternative doesn't work. "Enough" varies by issue and by the specific boss.

**Know more than you say.** Senior people can often skip the prepared story and go straight to the number or assumption that worries them. Keep supporting material in annexes or hidden slides. If you don't know something, say so rather than making something up.

**Recommend something.** After explaining an issue, say what you think should happen. Ending a briefing with "so what should we do?" after days of fact-gathering is unhelpful — even an uncertain assessment ("I think we should allow the exception because...") gives the boss something to agree with, disagree with, or correct.

**Tell your boss what you need.** State clearly whether you need approval, a direction before more work, intervention with someone else, or if it's just a heads-up. This matters especially when presenting.

**Say when the work is already moving.** Use continuous tense ("I am checking the figures with Finance") to signal work already underway, rather than future tense which suggests it hasn't started — provided that reflects reality.
