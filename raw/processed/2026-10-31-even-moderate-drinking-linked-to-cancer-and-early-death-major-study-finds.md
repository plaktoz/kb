# Even moderate drinking linked to cancer and early death, major study finds

A major new study suggests that even modest amounts of alcohol may carry serious health consequences. Researchers found that drinking levels many Americans consider moderate are associated with higher risks of premature death, disability, cancer, heart disease, and other long-term health problems.

Published in the Journal of Studies on Alcohol and Drugs, the research challenges the longstanding belief that moderate drinking is harmless or may even provide health benefits. The findings suggest that alcohol's risks begin at relatively low levels of consumption and increase as people drink more.

"This study provides the most comprehensive U.S. estimates to date of lifetime risks of alcohol-attributable mortality and morbidity, showing that even moderate levels of consumption increase the risk of premature death and disability," said study co-author Katherine M. Keyes, PhD, professor of Epidemiology at Columbia University Mailman School of Public Health.

"No protective effect of drinking was observed even at low levels," added Keyes, whose research examines alcohol and other substance use across different stages of life.

"Even low levels of alcohol use come with health risks," said lead study author Kevin Shield, PhD, an associate professor at the University of Toronto and a senior scientist who leads the World Health Organization (WHO)/Pan American Health Organization (PAHO) Collaborating Centre in Addiction and Mental Health. "And that risk continues to increase the more someone drinks."

Major Study Examines the Lifetime Health Risks of Alcohol

To better understand how drinking affects health over a lifetime, researchers from the United States and Canada conducted an extensive review of existing scientific evidence.

Medical experts evaluated more than 7,200 scientific articles examining diseases and injuries associated with alcohol consumption. They used this evidence to determine how drinking influences the risk of individual health conditions, then combined those findings with large national health data sets.

Using statistical models, the team estimated how different levels of alcohol consumption affect the likelihood of developing serious illnesses or dying prematurely over a lifetime.

The analysis covered a wide range of health problems linked to alcohol, including cardiovascular disease, liver disease, injuries, and several forms of cancer. Among the cancers examined were those affecting the esophagus, mouth, and breast.

By considering these conditions together, the researchers were able to estimate alcohol's overall health burden rather than focusing on individual diseases.

Why Two Drinks a Day May Be Riskier Than You Think

One of the study's most important findings concerns drinking habits that many people would not consider excessive.

Consuming two alcoholic drinks per day, a level often viewed as socially moderate, was associated with a substantially higher risk of premature death attributable to alcohol.

The findings also provide more specific information than the latest U.S. Dietary Guidelines, which recommend that Americans "limit alcoholic beverages" but do not identify a particular amount that can be consumed safely.

Earlier dietary recommendations set daily limits of two alcoholic drinks for men and one for women. However, what counts as a single drink depends on the beverage and its alcohol content. A standard drink generally corresponds to 12 ounces of beer, 5 ounces of wine, or 1.5 ounces of spirits, although differences in alcohol concentration can change those amounts.

According to the researchers, the newer guidelines communicate a useful 'less-is-best' principle but lack specific numerical estimates that would help people understand how health risks change with consumption.

Their analysis was intended to fill that gap by measuring risks across a broad range of drinking levels.

Does Moderate Drinking Have Any Health Benefits?

For years, moderate alcohol consumption has sometimes been promoted as beneficial for cardiovascular health. The new findings cast doubt on that idea when the full range of alcohol-related health effects is considered.

The researchers found no statistically significant protective effect on overall health at any level of alcohol consumption.

They acknowledged that relatively low alcohol intake may be associated with a reduced risk of ischemic heart disease and stroke. Ischemic heart disease occurs when reduced blood flow deprives the heart muscle of the oxygen it needs, often because arteries have narrowed or become blocked.

However, any potential cardiovascular benefits must be considered alongside alcohol's association with cancer, liver disease, injuries, and other serious conditions.

When the researchers combined these outcomes, the potential benefits were outweighed by the harmful effects, even among people consuming seven alcoholic drinks per week.

This broader assessment helps explain why a drinking habit that appears beneficial when examining one disease may still increase overall health risks.

Alcohol Risks Vary From Person to Person

Although the findings offer a clearer picture of the long-term dangers of alcohol, the researchers emphasize that the estimates describe risks across populations, not the precise likelihood that any individual will develop a particular disease.

The statistical models relied on "the best possible data," according to the research team. Nevertheless, personal health outcomes can differ considerably depending on genetics, lifestyle, drinking patterns, and other individual factors.

To calculate the overall burden of alcohol consumption, the investigators estimated risks for health conditions already known to have a causal relationship with drinking. They then combined those estimates to assess the total impact.

The researchers also acknowledged that scientific understanding of alcohol's health effects continues to evolve. Emerging evidence suggests that drinking may be associated with additional conditions, including pancreatic cancer, that require further investigation.

"Understanding those relationships, and how much alcohol contributes to those risks, is an area that still needs further work," said Keyes and Shield.

Despite these uncertainties, the results offer an important reference point: alcohol consumption above one drink per day was associated with increased health risks for both men and women.

The researchers believe that giving people more specific information about these risks can help them make better decisions about how much they drink.

"Having a clearer threshold helps people better understand what level of drinking is associated with increased risk and make more informed decisions when drinking."

Why the Findings Matter for U.S. Alcohol Guidelines

The research also raises questions about how scientific evidence is considered when federal drinking recommendations are developed.

In an accompanying editorial, Robert M. Vincent, a former associate administrator for the U.S. Substance Abuse and Mental Health Services Administration, discussed the circumstances surrounding the research and its role in the federal dietary guidance process.

"The Alcohol Intake and Health report was explicitly invited to inform alcohol guidance during development of the Dietary Guidelines for Americans, 2025-2030," he writes. "Despite the study's adherence to its mandate, its findings were sidelined."

Vincent's comments highlight concerns about whether the research received appropriate consideration during the development of national recommendations.

The study's findings reinforce the importance of understanding alcohol's cumulative health effects rather than assuming that moderate consumption is beneficial or risk-free. Although individual risks vary, the overall pattern was clear: greater alcohol consumption was associated with greater harm, and the researchers found no overall health advantage to drinking.
