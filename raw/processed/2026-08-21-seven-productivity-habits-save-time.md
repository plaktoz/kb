# 7 Productivity Habits That Save More Time Than They Cost

Most productivity advice asks you to add something — a new app, a new routine. The habits that actually make a difference work the opposite way: they remove friction instead of adding structure, and keep working even when the week gets busy.

**Habit 1. Batch your decisions.** Decide once, use all week. Settle recurring low-stakes choices (meals, what to wear, when meetings happen) in one sitting instead of relitigating each daily. Ten small decisions a day, each thirty seconds, is five minutes lost to nothing — batch them into a ten-minute Sunday decision instead.

**Habit 2. Build in unscheduled slack.** A calendar with no gaps looks efficient but behaves like a house of cards. Leaving real, unbooked time — even thirty minutes between blocks — gives the day somewhere to absorb the inevitable instead of falling apart from it.

**Habit 3. Cut the task list to what matters today.** Most task lists are really two lists pretending to be one: a short list of what genuinely needs to happen today, and a longer list of what would be nice to get to eventually. Separate them; look only at the short list most mornings.

**Habit 4. Automate or drop recurring low-value tasks.** Before optimizing a repeated chore, ask whether it needs to happen at all. A task removed entirely costs nothing, forever — a bigger win than simply doing it faster.

**Habit 5. Do a five-minute weekly reset.** A short look at the week ahead (Sunday evening or Monday morning) prevents dozens of small decisions later. Keep it brief — a rough shape for the week, not a detailed plan.

**Habit 6. Protect one buffer block a day.** A fixed, defended stretch of time (even thirty minutes) gives urgent things somewhere to land without displacing everything else already planned.

**Habit 7. Close the day with a two-minute shutdown.** Note what's carrying over to tomorrow before finishing up, so your mind doesn't have to hold onto open loops overnight. Most lingering work stress comes from unfinished things circling in the background, not from the work itself.

None of these habits ask you to do more — each removes a small piece of friction. Individually each saves a few minutes; together, across a normal busy week, they add up to roughly an hour or more of energy and attention otherwise spent just keeping up. These are also the habits that survive a bad week, which is the real point.
