# The Risks of Cognitive Delegation and the Challenge of Accountability in the Age of AI

Warren Buffett said "the most important quality for an investor is temperament, not intellect" — success depends on sound judgment under uncertainty. AI adoption is transforming the investment profession in ways that may violate this principle.

## Cognitive Delegation

Investment professionals increasingly rely on AI-generated outputs before developing sufficiently robust internal understanding of the underlying analytical processes. Recent research shows this behavior introduces fragility: investment theses may look technically coherent while investors lose the ability to question, defend, and adapt them when necessary. Entry-level analysts can now produce sophisticated financial models and theses without internalizing the underlying frameworks, which becomes evident in live discussions where defending assumptions and revising conclusions in real time is critical. This isn't limited to juniors — as reliance grows, even experienced professionals' internal mental models (the simplified frameworks used to interpret complex realities) may erode, a serious risk in high-stakes, time-pressured situations where independent reasoning matters most.

## Accountability and Governance

As AI integrates deeper into investment processes, the line between human judgment and machine inference blurs, yet legal, regulatory and ethical frameworks hold individuals and organizations — not machines — responsible for outcomes. Firms must treat AI as a tool operating within a well-defined architecture of human oversight, requiring clear decision rights, transparent documentation, and robust audit mechanisms. Without these safeguards, firms risk black-box dependency. Disentangling human versus AI contributions also complicates compensation, promotion, and talent evaluation, eroding trust in performance attribution over time.

## AI as Amplifier

As AI expands available information and analysis speed, competitive advantage will depend not on data or computational access but on judgment and accountability. The firms best positioned to thrive will design investment processes that preserve constructive friction, foster independent thinking, and challenge both human- and AI-generated conclusions — treating AI as a tool whose effectiveness depends on the human systems in which it operates.
