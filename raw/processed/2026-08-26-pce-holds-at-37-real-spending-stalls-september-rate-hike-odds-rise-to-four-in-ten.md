# PCE Holds at 3.7%, Real Spending Stalls: September Rate Hike Odds Rise to Four-in-Ten

#### July data hands Warsh stagflation signal as inflation and demand crack simultaneously

The Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge refused to budge in July, and real consumer spending nearly stopped growing — a combination that handed incoming Fed Chair Kevin Warsh one of the least convenient data points of his young tenure, arriving the morning before he is scheduled to open the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium in Wyoming.

The Bureau of Economic Analysis released its monthly Personal Income and Outlays report Wednesday morning showing the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index — the measure the Fed formally targets — rose 0.2% from June and held steady at 3.7% year over year, one-tenth of a percentage point above the Dow Jones consensus forecast of 3.6%. The reading matched June's annual pace exactly. Core PCE, which strips out volatile food and energy prices and is the figure policymakers weight most heavily when calibrating policy, rose 0.2% month over month and 3.3% year over year — exactly in line with consensus but still more than a full percentage point above the Fed's 2% long-run target.

The headline number was the smaller problem. The spending figure was worse. Real consumer spending — adjusted for inflation — rose just $1.3 billion in July, a gain of less than 0.1% for the month, a sharp deceleration from June's inflation-adjusted gain of 0.4%. In other words: households are buying almost exactly the same amount of real goods and services in July as they did in June, even as prices remain nearly twice the Fed's target.

### What the Numbers Actually Show

The headline miss was on the annual rate. The month-over-month 0.2% gain was broadly in line with some forecasts, but the unchanged annual figure at 3.7% confounded the broader expectation of modest progress.

Within the breakdown, services prices rose 0.3% month over month and 2.5% year over year — driven primarily by wages and shelter costs, which tend to be stickier than goods prices. Goods prices registered only 1.3% above a year ago. The fact that services inflation is running nearly double the goods rate matters for the speed of any future disinflationary progress: services prices, tied to wages and rent, typically take 12 to 18 months to respond to monetary tightening, whereas goods prices move within quarters. The Fed has been tightening the economy for years; services inflation's persistence means the last mile of disinflation is the slowest one.

That pattern is now four months old, not four weeks. Core PCE held at 3.3% year over year in April, May, June, and July — four consecutive months with no net improvement. The BEA has also announced a PCE methodology overhaul scheduled for September's release — meaning July's figures could face retrospective revisions that shift the picture. That overhaul makes the current reading simultaneously important and provisional.

### Why Real Spending Near-Flat Is the Story Beneath the Inflation Story

Beyond the price data, the report painted a sobering picture of where American consumers actually are right now. Personal income rose $115.1 billion, or 0.4% for the month, with disposable personal income increasing 0.5%. Households are still earning more. They are not spending more in real terms.

Nominal spending rose $36.3 billion, driven by a $86.2 billion increase in services outlays, partly offset by a $49.9 billion decline in goods spending. But once those numbers are adjusted for the inflation that consumed most of the apparent gain, real purchasing activity was essentially flat.

The personal saving rate ticked up to 3.0%, historically low but a shift suggesting some households are now opting to accumulate savings rather than spend.

That caution is visible at the ground level. Walmart — America's largest retailer and one of the most reliable barometers of mainstream consumer behavior — posted its slowest US same-store growth in six years last week, with same-store sales rising just 2.6% in the second quarter, missing the 3.7% analyst estimate. CFO John David Rainey told analysts "consumers have been more pressured" than earlier in 2026, attributing much of the pullback to gasoline prices climbing past $4 a gallon in July. Retail sales fell 0.6% in July, the steepest monthly decline in more than a year. Goldman Sachs warned that real consumer spending growth could slow to as low as 1% in the second half of 2026 — the equivalent of Americans cutting their spending pace by more than half.

### Energy's Durable Shadow

A significant driver of inflation's persistence is energy costs, which surged earlier in 2026 following the escalation of hostilities involving the United States and Iran and ongoing supply disruptions at the Strait of Hormuz. Those shocks pushed West Texas Intermediate crude oil to a peak near $113 per barrel in April before a partial pullback; WTI was trading in the mid-$80s per barrel as of this week.

The ripple effects have proven durable. The national average for gasoline has hovered near $4 per gallon, with diesel averaging above $5.60 — costs that flow through transportation, logistics, and the prices consumers pay across nearly every product category. The diesel crack spread — the margin between crude and the diesel refined from it — touched a record near $102 per barrel in August, reflecting a structural supply tightness in distillates that energy traders and economists expect to persist well into the fall.

### How September Hike Odds Moved After the Release

Markets recalibrated quickly after the 8:30 a.m. ET release. According to CME Group's FedWatch tool rate probabilities, the probability of a September rate hike climbed to approximately 40.1% following the report, up from around 33.1% a week earlier. A hold remained the base case at roughly 59.9%, but the direction of travel is clear: each data point that fails to show meaningful cooling adds ammunition to the case that the committee must act.

The practical stakes for anyone carrying variable-rate debt are immediate. A 25-basis-point hike at September's meeting would raise the prime rate within days of the vote, directly increasing the interest cost on credit card balances, adjustable-rate mortgages past their initial fixed period, and home equity lines of credit. Tens of millions of American households carry at least one of those products; the delay between the vote and the rate increase is measured in days, not months.

"Inflation remains well above the Fed's 2% target," said Bret Kenwell, US investment analyst at eToro. "A hotter-than-expected reading could renew pressure on policymakers to keep interest rates higher for longer."

Three FOMC members — Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack, Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari, and Dallas Fed President Lorie Logan — had already voted for an immediate quarter-point hike at July's meeting, the widest hawkish dissent the committee has produced since September 2016. Wednesday's data gives those three members another piece of evidence for the argument they have already made publicly. Four more votes would flip the committee to a hiking majority.

Post-release analysis from Yahoo Finance and other outlets noted that the burden of proof has shifted. The market still assigns a better-than-even probability to another hold. But the pathway to that hold now requires either Warsh's August 28 Jackson Hole speech to carry a credibly dovish signal, or the August jobs report and August CPI — due in early September — to show meaningful cooling.

### Can Warsh Hold at Jackson Hole With This Data in Hand?

The timing of Wednesday's release is particularly significant. Warsh — who succeeded Jerome Powell as Fed Chair in May 2026 — will deliver his first keynote address at the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium in Grand Teton National Park, Wyoming, on Friday, August 28, beginning at approximately 10:00 a.m. ET. He will arrive at that podium having just seen a data print that, by his own stated standard, should strengthen rather than weaken the case for action.

Warsh has built his public case for patience around an argument that artificial intelligence investment will prove disinflationary over time — that AI-driven productivity gains will eventually allow economic output to rise faster than wages and prices, doing some of the Fed's inflation-fighting work without requiring additional rate increases. That thesis appeared in a November 2025 Wall Street Journal op-ed and has shaped his posture through two FOMC meetings. To put that thesis into operational form at the Fed, he launched five task forces in July 2026 reviewing AI, communications, inflation frameworks, economic data, and the balance sheet — with the AI and Productivity task force co-led by Marc Andreessen, economist Charles I. Jones, and Microsoft executive Asha Sharma.

That framework now faces a stress test. Core PCE has refused to move for four months. Real consumer spending has nearly stalled. Services inflation — the component furthest from the reach of the supply-chain improvements that AI's productivity gains would most directly address — is running at nearly twice the goods rate. A Bank of America survey of fund managers found that 69% expected Warsh's Jackson Hole speech to strike a neutral tone. Neutral is now priced in. The asymmetry is clear: a speech that leans hawkish would move markets significantly; a speech that reads as neutral barely moves them at all.

Warsh has also deliberately scaled back the forward guidance that markets grew accustomed to under Powell, declining to submit his own rate projection in the June Summary of Economic Projections and describing his Jackson Hole remarks in advance as a "blank piece of paper." That deliberate opacity — combined with FOMC minutes from the July meeting indicating that "many" participants believed rate hikes would "likely be necessary if inflation did not decline" — means every phrase he delivers Friday will be parsed for clues.

### Does Warsh's AI Thesis Explain Why Inflation Is Still Above Three Percent?

Warsh's disinflation argument has a particular vulnerability that Wednesday's data underscores. His task force is studying AI's impact on productivity, and his thesis relies on that impact materializing as a disinflationary force within the relevant policy horizon. But the Fed's own Dallas branch has documented that data-center electricity demand from the AI buildout could raise annual PCE inflation by 0.04 to 0.13 percentage points per year through 2030 — meaning the sector Warsh expects to bring inflation down is, in the near term, modestly pushing it up.

Governor Lisa Cook cited that same dynamic in her May 2026 AI inflation speech, noting that more than $1.5 trillion in data-center construction plans have been announced, only a fraction of which have been realized, meaning the construction-cost pressure will continue for years. Vice Chair Philip Jefferson and Governor Michael Barr have argued that heavy capital investment required to deploy AI at scale could raise neutral interest rates — the rate at which monetary policy is neither stimulative nor restrictive — making the case for holding not stronger but weaker.

The external assessment is similarly skeptical on the near-term timeline. A joint poll by the University of Chicago and the Financial Times found roughly 60% of top economists believed AI's inflation impact over the next two years would be close to zero.

### What Comes Next, and What It Means for Your Wallet

The September FOMC meeting is scheduled for September 15-16, and two major data points will arrive before policymakers vote: the August jobs report and August CPI, both due in early September. ClearBridge senior investment strategy analyst Josh Jamner expects the Fed to treat those releases as decisive inputs.

"The data still gives the Federal Reserve time to wait and see," said Heather Long, chief economist at Navy Federal Credit Union. "It's not getting worse, but it didn't get any better in July either."

LPL Financial chief economist Jeffrey Roach offered a note of cautious optimism, suggesting that a potential inflection point may be approaching if retailers pass along tariff rebates in the form of lower consumer prices — an outcome that could push core inflation below 3% as soon as October. Walmart has said it will direct some of its $2.9 billion in tariff refunds toward keeping prices low. But that scenario remains speculative, and four consecutive months of core PCE at 3.3% suggest the structural barrier is real, not arithmetical.

The next PCE release, covering August 2026 data, is scheduled for September 30 at 8:30 a.m. ET — two weeks after the FOMC will already have voted.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**What does PCE holding at 3.7% mean for the chance of a September rate hike?**

It pushed the odds meaningfully higher. Before the July PCE release, CME FedWatch tool data showed September rate-hike odds at around 33%. After the release, they rose to roughly 40%. The reason is specific: the July reading confirmed that inflation has made no annual progress since June, and it arrived alongside evidence that real consumer spending is nearly stalling. Three FOMC members already voted for an immediate hike in July; four more votes would flip the committee to a majority for a rate increase. Whether those four additional votes materialize depends primarily on the August jobs report and August CPI, both due in early September.

**If the Fed raises rates in September, how quickly would that affect my credit card bill or adjustable-rate mortgage?**

Variable-rate consumer debt — credit card balances, adjustable-rate mortgages past their initial fixed period, home equity lines of credit — is tied to the prime rate, which moves in lockstep with the federal funds rate. A 25-basis-point September hike would raise the prime rate by the same amount within days of the vote. For credit cards, that typically translates into a higher annual percentage rate on the next billing statement. For an ARM, the rate adjustment depends on when your loan's reset date falls, but the benchmark rate feeding into that calculation rises immediately.

**Why has core PCE been stuck at 3.3% for four straight months, and is that a sign the Fed is losing the fight?**

Four consecutive months at 3.3% reflects a specific structural feature of where inflation now sits: it has largely migrated out of goods — where supply-chain normalization and energy pullbacks have helped — and into services, where wage growth and shelter costs drive prices. Services prices respond to monetary tightening much more slowly than goods prices, typically with a 12-to-18-month lag. The Fed's rate hikes from earlier in the cycle are still working their way through that system. Economists are divided on whether this represents entrenchment or simply a slow-moving normalization; the distinction matters enormously for how many more months of above-target readings the Fed can absorb before its credibility with inflation expectations becomes a separate problem.

**What is Warsh expected to say at Jackson Hole on Friday, and why does it matter?**

Warsh delivers his first major keynote as Fed Chair at the Jackson Hole Economic Policy Symposium on Friday, August 28, at approximately 10:00 a.m. ET. The symposium's theme — "Financial Innovation: Implications for Payments and Policy" — creates an opening for both rate-expectation signals and, given Warsh's stated positions on digital assets, potential commentary on stablecoins and central bank digital currencies. Markets have priced in a neutral tone from roughly 69% of fund managers surveyed, meaning neutral has little market impact and the risk is asymmetric: a hawkish lean moves markets meaningfully, a neutral speech barely registers. The real question Wednesday's PCE print creates is whether Warsh can credibly argue for patience in his first keynote when the data he just received shows inflation stuck and consumer spending slowing — a combination his AI disinflation thesis does not explain and does not resolve in the near term.
