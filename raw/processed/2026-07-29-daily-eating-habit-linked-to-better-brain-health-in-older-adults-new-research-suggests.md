# Daily eating habit linked to better brain health in older adults, new research suggests

source_url: https://www.foxnews.com/health/daily-eating-habit-linked-better-brain-health-older-adults-new-research-suggests

---

By Angelica Stabile, Fox News Digital.

Eating within a shorter daily window could help support brain health as people age, according to new research. Preliminary findings presented at NUTRITION 2026 — the American Society for Nutrition's annual meeting — suggest older women who ate within eight to nine hours per day saw slight cognitive improvement compared to those who spread meals over about 12 hours.

## Study findings

The small six-month trial included 47 postmenopausal obese or overweight women aged 50 to 79, each advised to reduce daily food intake by about 500 calories. Participants were divided into two groups: one ate within an average 8.2-hour window (usually 10 a.m. to 6 p.m.), the other across a 12.3-hour window.

Both groups lost about 15 pounds over six months, but the shorter-window group scored higher on cognitive tests measuring spatial planning and problem-solving, and showed fewer errors on memory and learning tests (though no meaningful difference in reaction time or multitasking).

"These outcomes suggest a better ability to remember information during everyday tasks and reduce mistakes related to memory, attention and problem-solving," said lead researcher Sue Shapses, Ph.D., a professor at Rutgers University and Rutgers-RWJ Medical Center. "Reductions in the eating window predicted greater improvements." Researchers believe time-restricted eating may support brain health by regulating circadian rhythm, reducing inflammation, and improving metabolic health — and that losing weight alone can help "ward off some of the aging-related cognitive decline."

Women, and adults who are overweight or obese, tend to face a higher likelihood of developing Alzheimer's disease and other forms of dementia as they age, according to the researchers.

## How might meal timing help the brain?

Lauri Wright, Ph.D., director of nutrition programs at the USF College of Public Health (not involved in the study), noted: "This is an interesting study because it suggests that when we eat may matter in addition to what and how much we eat... The results are promising, but we need larger, longer studies before recommending time-restricted eating specifically to improve brain health." She noted possible mechanisms: a longer overnight break from eating may improve insulin sensitivity, reduce chronic inflammation, support circadian rhythms, and give cells more time for repair.

New York-based nutritionist Robin DeCicco added that eating within a restricted window often leads to fewer calories consumed and lower markers of inflammation and oxidative stress, and that fasting periods give the digestive system a chance to rest.

## Caveats

Wright cautioned that other factors — regular physical activity, adequate sleep, blood pressure and blood sugar control, avoiding smoking, social connection, and an overall healthy dietary pattern such as the Mediterranean diet — have much stronger evidence for protecting cognitive function over time. "Think of meal timing as one potential piece of the puzzle rather than a magic bullet." Intermittent fasting isn't appropriate for everyone: Wright advised against it for children and teens, pregnant or breastfeeding women, anyone with a history of an eating disorder, and people with diabetes on insulin, and both experts recommended medical supervision.
