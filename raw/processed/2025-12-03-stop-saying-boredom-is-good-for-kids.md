# Stop Saying Boredom is Good for Kids

Chronic boredom is harmful to adults, causing stress, disengagement, and poor well-being. Academic researchers have shown that boredom in the workplace can be just as damaging as burnout. But search for information about childhood boredom and you'll find the opposite message: articles describing boredom for kids as "fantastic," "important," and full of benefits.

News articles, social media graphics, parenting discussions, and even policy proposals are full of praise for how great boredom is for children. The author questions why so many adults disregard children's autonomy, capabilities, and intellectual needs.

## Unstructured play is different than boredom

Play is described as a keystone of childhood, essential for unstructured free time where children discover their own interests. The author shares that her daughter invented activities like building homes for stuffed animals, drawing mazes, and creating games—describing these as not boredom at all.

By contrast, the author notes her daughter experienced real boredom while attending school, where she began zoning out, lost curiosity, became passive, and grew upset when making mistakes in activities she once loved. Returning to homeschooling reportedly restored her daughter's curiosity, creativity, and resilience.

## Boredom and Busy-ness are not opposite

Boredom is defined as weariness from lack of interest, dullness, or repetition—not simply about workload but about how engaging the work is. The piece references "boreout," a term for chronic workplace boredom linked to poor well-being, disengagement, stress, and higher quit rates.

The author illustrates boredom with examples: a math class too easy or too hard, repetitive tasks, or being forced to read overly simplistic books. She quotes Lotta Harju, an assistant professor at EM Lyon Business School: "Don't assume that keeping people busy will cure boredom. It does not."

## An endless appetite for new information

The author critiques an LA Times article arguing children shouldn't learn phonics before age 5-6, quoting researcher Wolf: "I even think that it's really wrong for parents to ever try to push reading before 5." She argues this framing ignores the harms of understimulation and quotes neuroscientist Erik Hoel's counterargument that "young children have an endless appetite for new information."

## Conflating challenge, amount of free time, and foci of motivation

The author identifies three variables often wrongly conflated: level of challenge, time spent, and whether motivation is internal or externally imposed. She argues intellectual challenge doesn't require significant time or external pressure.

## Stop relying on false binaries

The piece challenges false dichotomies around screen time, arguing children use computers for creative pursuits like digital art, music, and coding—sometimes collaboratively. She critiques a social media graphic presenting "wired" versus "wild" childhood as opposites, offering a revised version to break the binary. She poses a comparison: a child inspired to write after reading a space book versus after watching an educational YouTube video—arguing neither scenario involves boredom.

## Skills that open up the world

The author highlights skills like reading, coding, and touch-typing as gateways to expression and exploration, noting children's ideas often exceed their handwriting ability. She recalls her own childhood fascination with a National Geographic mammal encyclopedia, emphasizing that children need guides to discover fields of interest, and that customization to each child's needs and reactions is essential.

## Children's Needs

The author distinguishes overscheduling and standardized-testing pressure (which she calls harmful) from the need for intellectual engagement (which she considers compatible with free time and autonomy). She concludes by paralleling adult workplace boreout with childhood boredom, calling for children to receive "appropriately calibrated challenges" that are interesting and engaging.
