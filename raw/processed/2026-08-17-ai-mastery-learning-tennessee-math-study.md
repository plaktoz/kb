# Slow math: Kids may learn more when AI makes them review mistakes

Artificial intelligence makes many tasks faster and easier. But a new study suggests students may learn more when AI makes them slow down. In an experiment involving more than 6,000 middle schoolers in Tennessee, students learned slightly more math when an AI tutor walked them through their mistakes and required them to demonstrate the same skill correctly three times in a row before moving on.

Researchers tested four approaches to practicing fractions: students were randomly assigned to conventional computer-based instruction or the same software with an AI tutor, and within each group, half had to correctly answer practice questions three times in a row after a mistake. Students used software similar to Khan Academy for 50 minutes during math class, then took a 15-minute retention test a week later.

The winning combination was AI tutoring plus repetition — students needed to demonstrate mastery, not just receive help. Students using this AI-enhanced "mastery learning" approach scored about 3 percentage points higher than students receiving conventional computerized instruction. "I don't want to jump out and say we've demonstrated that AI is going to be the game changer that we hope it is," said Philip Oreopoulos, lead author and an economist at the University of Toronto, "but it might be the first kind of evidence that shows there's at least some hints that it has some positive value against no AI at all."

The study, "Making AI Tutoring Productive: Evidence from a Mastery-Based Math Practice Experiment," was conducted by researchers from the University of Toronto and the University of Pennsylvania's Wharton School, and is scheduled to circulate as an NBER working paper.

The researchers think AI helped because it walked students through their mistakes instead of simply showing them a solution — without AI, students could skim a step-by-step example without figuring out what went wrong. Students using AI plus mastery learning spent more time per question, a sign of deeper engagement, and were more likely to get the next question right after a mistake.

There was a limit to the benefit: students in the AI-plus-mastery group performed better mainly on the easiest fraction questions, most similar to what they'd practiced; the advantage did not extend to harder problems, and the intervention did not produce deeper or more transferable understanding of fractions. Oreopoulos cautions against concluding mastery learning is the best way to use AI in math instruction — his team tested only four combinations and plans to keep testing features against each other.
