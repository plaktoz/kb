# Should Students Learn Science Through Inquiry?

Many educators assume inquiry-based science teaching is best because professional scientists work through inquiry — but this confuses a subject's epistemology (how experts generate new knowledge at the frontier) with its pedagogy (how novices should be taught the fundamentals).

Research distinguishes novices from experts not just quantitatively but qualitatively: in one study, expert physicists solved problems by perceiving underlying mathematical structure, while less-expert physicists fixated on surface details (airplanes, headwinds) even when they possessed the relevant knowledge. Experts' intellectual labor happens at the edges of a domain (discovering new knowledge); novices' task is the slow, steady acquisition of the domain's core nodes and connections.

The most effective pedagogy for novices is explicit instruction, typically in four phases: (1) curricular delineation — mapping out exactly what knowledge nodes/connections students need; (2) checking prerequisite knowledge before introducing new content; (3) teacher-led explanation using "blank canvas modeling" (live-built diagrams) to exploit the multimedia effect while avoiding the redundancy and split-attention effects from cognitive load theory; (4) checking/consolidating understanding via whiteboards, followed by practice that starts with similar/blocked examples before mixing them with prior knowledge ("decompose and recompose").

Explicit instruction alone is insufficient — students also need systematic retrieval practice over time to secure knowledge against forgetting. The paper's core claim: assuming science pedagogy should mimic science epistemology (i.e., "teach through inquiry because that's how scientists work") is a categorical error, since novices lack the knowledge base to generate insight through open-ended inquiry.
