# Microbiome Moonshot Proposed to Address Chronic Diseases

Researchers at Rutgers University are calling for a comprehensive initiative to explore the connections between the human microbiome and chronic diseases. This extensive effort aims to enhance understanding and address the significant impact of microbial communities on health.

## Understanding the Microbiome's Impact on Health

The human microbiome, a complex community of trillions of microorganisms residing in and on our bodies, plays a crucial role in shaping both immunity and metabolism. Factors such as antibiotic use, cesarean births, and diets high in processed foods can disrupt these microbial communities.

Researchers have posited that such disturbances could be linked to the increasing prevalence of chronic health issues, including obesity, asthma, and food allergies.

Martin Blaser and Maria Gloria Dominguez-Bello, prominent microbiologists from Rutgers, have highlighted the need for more rigorous research to establish definitive cause-and-effect relationships between microbiome disruptions and the aforementioned chronic conditions. Current evidence suggests correlations, but substantial funding is required to conduct extensive controlled studies to validate these associations.

## Proposed Research and Public Education Initiatives

In response to the growing body of evidence, the U.S. Department of Health and Human Services convened a roundtable discussion with Blaser, Dominguez-Bello, and other leading researchers to strategize a robust research agenda. This initiative is designed to address priority areas in microbiome research, particularly during critical periods such as pregnancy and early childhood.

As outlined in an article in Nature Medicine, the proposal identifies 10 key priorities for investigating the microbiome's influence on chronic diseases. These priorities not only emphasize research but also stress the importance of public education to foster awareness about the microbiome's role in health.

## Long-term Implications and Educational Efforts

The initial stages of microbiome development occur during the first 1,000 days of life, from pregnancy to a child's second birthday. This timeframe presents a unique opportunity to study how protecting microbial growth in early life could lead to improved health outcomes in the future. Blaser and Dominguez-Bello advocate for long-term studies that follow individuals from pregnancy through adolescence to examine potential links between early alterations in the microbiome and later health issues.

The researchers have categorized their recommendations into four main areas:

- Building scientific foundations,
- Advancing clinical applications,
- Pursuing high-impact opportunities, and
- Providing education.

They call for standardized research practices to ensure results are comparable across diverse populations, especially beyond affluent Western nations where most current research is concentrated.

## THE CNP TAKEAWAY

This call for a microbiome moonshot underscores the potential for understanding how microbial health influences chronic diseases. Supporting research during critical developmental periods could pave the way for preventive measures and better health outcomes.
