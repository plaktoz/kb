# From Diagnosis to Wellness, AI's Healthcare Revolution

Artificial intelligence is changing far more than hospital paperwork, according to Georgia Tech researchers — it's beginning to help doctors make clinical decisions, identify mental health risks earlier, expand care through smartphones and wearables, and extend healthcare to underserved populations. "Medicine has clearly embraced the human-AI fusion approach," said Jon Duke, director of Georgia Tech's Center for Health Analytics & Informatics. "The use of AI is encouraged, but reliance on AI is not."

Clinical AI tools like OpenEvidence now give healthcare professionals AI-generated, source-backed answers to medical questions, and AI is used within electronic health records to generate clinical notes from patient conversations and summarize complex charts. Duke's open question: "whether physicians trained alongside AI will develop the same level of independent clinical judgment as previous generations."

Munmun De Choudhury, professor in Interactive Computing, said AI is helping public health systems move from reactive to proactive by identifying emerging mental health concerns from digital and clinical data, potentially enabling earlier intervention before someone reaches a crisis. Alexander Adams, who studies wearable sensing and remote monitoring, noted that "no stand-alone medical devices generate nearly as much data as smartphones and smartwatches," making them a natural focus for AI, with particular potential to improve access in rural and underserved communities.

Rosa Arriaga cautioned that AI's impact on chronic disease management has remained limited despite its promise, and warned that increased reliance on data tools risks turning clinicians into "data checkers" with less patient interaction. She noted randomized controlled trials showing some people benefit from AI-delivered "therapy," but cautioned that greater AI interaction could also lead to isolation and problematic parasocial relationships with AI, eroding the social fabric.
