# Expertise expert offers 8 tips for learning a new skill

If you want to master a musical instrument, sport, or software program, the key is a diligent, deliberate approach — not the 10,000 hours you may have heard about. Practice time is important, said Jeffrey A. Greene, McMichael Family Professor at UNC's School of Education, but it's only one of several factors. Basing his advice on the deliberate practice model developed by cognitive psychologist K. Anders Ericsson, Greene recommends:

1. **Find a guide** — look for a source that breaks down the steps, whether a book, online guide, or instructor.
2. **Set up a distraction-free environment** — it's easier to set up a context where distractions won't happen than to resist them once they do.
3. **Build endurance** — practice multiple days a week, starting with 15-30 minutes and building up; even experts generally can't practice more than four or five hours a day.
4. **Practice deliberately** — be intentional and mindful, starting with a basic part of the skill and consciously paying attention to it. "De-automatize" skills you've plateaued on by concentrating and slowing down.
5. **Find motivation** — both intrinsic and extrinsic motivation are fine, as long as you feel in control and it's your choice; extrinsic motivation only becomes harmful when it's coercive or manipulative.
6. **Get feedback** — a coach or partner's outside assessment, even if non-expert, gives insight on progress. Feedback should "feed forward" toward the next goal, not just assess past performance.
7. **Get the right kind of feedback** — assessments should make you feel in control of your choices, competent, and cared for; these three things strengthen intrinsic motivation.
8. **Be your own coach** — notice improvements, write them down, and internalize the skills and confidence gained. Most people benefit from coaching no matter how advanced they are, but can do much of that work themselves.

Greene's final tip: have fun.
