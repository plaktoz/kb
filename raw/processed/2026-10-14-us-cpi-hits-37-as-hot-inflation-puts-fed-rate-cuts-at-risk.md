# US CPI Hits 3.7% as Hot Inflation Puts Fed Rate Cuts at Risk

U.S. inflation accelerated in September, putting renewed pressure on the Federal Reserve as it considers its next move on interest rates. The latest Consumer Price Index report showed that price increases remained stronger than economists expected, reinforcing concerns that inflation will take longer to return to the central bank’s 2% target.

The headline CPI rose 3.7% year over year, up from 3.4% in August and above economists’ forecast of 3.6%. Monthly inflation increased 0.6%, matching expectations. Core CPI, which excludes food and energy, climbed 2.6% annually and 0.3% monthly, with the annual reading exceeding the 2.5% forecast.

With headline and core inflation both exceeding expectations, the Federal Reserve has less room to ease borrowing costs without risking another setback in its fight against rising prices.

## Rising Gasoline Prices Drive the Inflation Surge

Energy costs played a major role in September’s inflation increase. Gasoline prices jumped 9.1% from August and were 34.2% higher than a year earlier. The broader energy index rose 5.2% during the month, while food prices remained unchanged.

Higher fuel costs place an immediate burden on consumers by increasing the cost of commuting and transportation. Businesses also face higher expenses for shipping, logistics, and energy-intensive production, creating another source of pressure on operating margins.

The surge in gasoline prices also raises the risk that higher energy costs will feed into other parts of the economy. As transportation and production expenses increase, businesses may pass some of those costs on to consumers, keeping inflation elevated beyond the energy sector.

## Core Inflation Raises the Bar for Fed Rate Cuts

The latest figures strengthen the case for keeping interest rates elevated as policymakers seek clearer evidence that price pressures are easing.

Cutting rates too soon could stimulate consumer spending and business investment, making it harder to bring inflation under control. The Fed is therefore likely to maintain its restrictive monetary policy until inflation shows more sustained progress toward its target.

This puts pressure on expectations for near-term rate cuts. Although the central bank also considers employment conditions and its preferred inflation measure, the Personal Consumption Expenditures price index, September’s CPI report makes an early cut harder to justify. Another hotter-than-expected inflation reading would reinforce the case for holding rates steady.

## Treasury Yields Rise as Stocks Face Pressure

Financial markets responded to the inflation report by reassessing the outlook for monetary easing. U.S. stock futures moved lower, while Treasury yields climbed as investors adjusted their expectations for Federal Reserve policy.

The 2-year Treasury yield rose to around 4.91%, while the 10-year yield reached approximately 5.29%. The two-year yield is particularly sensitive to expectations for near-term Fed policy, making its increase a sign that investors are demanding higher returns amid uncertainty over the interest-rate outlook.

Rising yields create a headwind for equities because they increase the return available from government bonds and raise the discount rate applied to future corporate earnings. This is particularly challenging for technology and other growth stocks, whose valuations often depend on profits expected years into the future.

The Nasdaq 100 faces particular pressure under these conditions. Higher borrowing costs can reduce the appeal of expensive growth stocks, while persistent inflation can also raise operating expenses for companies across the economy. Businesses with strong balance sheets, resilient demand, and consistent cash generation are better positioned to withstand this environment.

Energy producers may benefit from elevated fuel prices, but the broader market faces a more difficult backdrop as investors reassess valuations and the cost of capital. Continued increases in Treasury yields would reinforce that pressure, especially for stocks trading at high earnings multiples.

For investors, the prospect of higher interest rates for longer creates a challenging environment for equities. Rising borrowing costs can weigh on corporate investment and consumer spending, while higher Treasury yields make stocks less attractive relative to bonds. Companies with strong cash flow and resilient earnings are likely to be better positioned to withstand this pressure.
