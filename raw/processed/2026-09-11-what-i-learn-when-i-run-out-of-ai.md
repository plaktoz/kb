# What I Learn When I Run Out of AI

Jack Cheng reflects on how hitting weekly AI usage limits has unexpectedly become valuable. When powerful models like Fable are exhausted, he falls back on capable but less intensive tools — and the forced wait lets ideas mature before he acts on them.

He compares unlimited AI access to a problem Susan Graham identified with early time-sharing: "there's also a certain amount of sloppiness you get into." Without constraints, he catches himself "snacking" — a term from Will Larson describing the tendency to choose easy, low-impact tasks over harder, meaningful ones.

Drawing on computing history (NASA's Annie Easley hand-carrying punch cards, Alan Kay's critique of institutional time-sharing), Cheng notes that immediacy in computing has always carried a cost to deliberation. Sherry Turkle identified this in 1984: the computer's promise of instant, correct results makes it hard to step away.

His central metaphor is the Oracle at Delphi — Greek pilgrims traveled 11km uphill to seek answers, and Cheng suggests the journey itself may have clarified the questions. He now wants to build similar pauses into daily work: letting feature ideas sit overnight, writing down why a question matters before bringing it to AI.

His conclusion: some ideas will still merit immediate action, but others will fade once the pull of instant gratification is removed.
