# My PCOS Diagnosis Took 5 Years. Why the Name Change to PMOS Gives Me Hope

**Author:** Mandy French | **Fact-checked by:** Jill Seladi-Schulman, Ph.D. | **Date:** August 31, 2026

---

## A Long Road to Diagnosis

The author first encountered PCOS at age 18, when her aunt was diagnosed. Recognizing shared symptoms — weight gain, acne, and irregular periods — she sought testing from her primary physician. Despite believing she had the condition, she was dismissed and told to diet and exercise instead.

Five years passed before she received an accurate diagnosis at 23, when a gynecologist reviewed her records and responded: *"Of course you have PCOS."*

## Treatment After Diagnosis

Her gynecologist prescribed metformin to address insulin resistance and recommended staying on birth control for hormonal symptom management. Within months, her weight began to drop and her overall wellbeing improved.

Nearly two decades later, she still manages fatigue, mood changes, muscle aches, and headaches — but now with greater self-understanding and intentional lifestyle management.

## The Name Change to PMOS

In May 2026, experts began renaming PCOS to **polyendocrine metabolic ovarian syndrome (PMOS)**, better reflecting its whole-body nature rather than emphasizing ovarian and fertility aspects not present in all patients.

The author describes this as feeling like *"a game-changer,"* expressing hope it will lead to more comprehensive, personalized care. She notes that many patients receive only birth control or metformin, despite PMOS being a multisystem condition. Recent research has also linked PMOS to elevated heart disease risk.

## Looking Ahead at 40

Turning 40 prompted a renewed health focus. She began the GLP-1 medication Mounjaro, refined her diet, and increased physical activity. She hopes the renaming marks a first step toward improved diagnosis and individualized treatment — particularly for those newly diagnosed.
