# Why Burnout Should Be a Workforce Risk Management Concern

Federal workforce changes in 2025 were accompanied by measurable shifts in how employees experienced their work. Gallup research found federal employees reported higher levels of burnout following workforce reforms, though some engagement and satisfaction measures later showed signs of recovery as employees adapted.

Findings from the Partnership for Public Service's 2025 Public Service Viewpoint Survey — developed after the government discontinued its long-running Federal Employee Viewpoint Survey — showed significant declines in employee confidence and engagement. Overall engagement scores fell to 32 out of 100, with more than half of employees reporting their engagement worsened over the past year. Employees remained committed to public service, but there was a clear disconnect between their dedication and their confidence in the workplace systems supporting them.

**Why burnout matters for workers' compensation.** When employees experience higher stress or burnout, it can contribute to more complex workers' compensation claims and longer recovery timelines, even when injuries aren't directly caused by workplace stress. Injured employees in high-stress environments often need more behavioral health support and face added challenges coordinating a return to work, while agencies that cultivate a positive work environment can reduce these obstacles.

Author Steve Keith (Sedgwick Government Solutions) recommends agencies focus on clearly defined job roles with manageable workloads, positive organizational culture, and reducing stigma around accessing health care. He argues federal agencies should treat employee wellbeing and workforce risk management as linked: early intervention and case management connect injured employees with the right specialists at the right time, and evaluating injury trends across an agency can identify workplace safety issues, strengthen prevention, and reduce workers' compensation costs.
