# Is there a 'best' diet to help lower Alzheimer's risk?

**Author:** Corrie Pelc | **Fact-checked by:** Jill Seladi-Schulman, Ph.D. | **Published:** August 31, 2026

## Key Takeaways

- A healthy diet may help reduce Alzheimer's disease risk.
- Diets like Mediterranean, DASH, and MIND have been linked to lower Alzheimer's risk.
- A new review finds that diet's effectiveness depends on more than just food choices.
- Biological mediators and personal moderators both shape how diet affects risk.

---

## Overview

Researchers have been investigating lifestyle-based strategies to reduce Alzheimer's risk, including sleep, physical activity, not smoking, and nutritious eating. A new review published in *Frontiers in Nutrition* examines why no single diet works universally.

Kuldeep Kumar, PhD (Bond University, Australia), the review's corresponding author, noted that while diet is "the most common modifiable risk factor," it interacts with biology in complex ways.

---

## What the Review Examined

Researchers analyzed how dietary patterns affect key **biological mediators**:

- Blood-brain barrier dysfunction
- Gut-brain axis dysregulation
- Hyperhomocysteinaemia (elevated homocysteine)
- Insulin resistance
- Neuroinflammation
- Oxidative stress

They also assessed **moderating factors**:

- Age
- *APOE4* genotype
- Biological sex
- Disease stage
- Physical activity
- Socioeconomic status

---

## No Universal Diet Solution

The review proposes a moderator-mediator framework to personalize dietary recommendations. Carriers of the *APOE4* gene, for instance, may respond differently to certain diets than non-carriers.

Kumar summarized: "Diet has the potential to affect Alzheimer's disease and its progression. It should be critically understood and more personalized approaches should be developed."

---

## Expert Commentary

**Dr. Dung Trinh** (MemorialCare Medical Group) called the findings useful for moving beyond the idea of a single "best food," but cautioned that this is a review of existing evidence, not a new clinical trial. He emphasized diet as one part of a broader strategy alongside exercise, blood pressure management, diabetes control, and sleep.

He noted that Mediterranean, MIND, and DASH diets all share common ground, generally emphasizing "vegetables, fruits, whole grains, legumes, nuts, and healthier fats while limiting highly processed foods."

**Meridan Zerner, MS, RDN** observed that even patients following an apparently ideal diet can still experience cognitive decline, highlighting the role of individual variation. She noted that dietary benefits operate through "various, interrelated pathways" rather than a single switch.

---

## Practical Tips

- **Match diet to personal health conditions:** DASH may suit those with hypertension; Mediterranean may better address insulin resistance.
- **Prioritize sustainability:** The most effective diet is one a person can maintain for years, as benefits accumulate over time.
- **Focus on shared principles:** Increase plants and healthy fats, minimize ultra-processed foods — then adapt to culturally familiar foods.
- **Consult a registered dietitian** to tailor general principles to individual needs rather than following a rigid template.
