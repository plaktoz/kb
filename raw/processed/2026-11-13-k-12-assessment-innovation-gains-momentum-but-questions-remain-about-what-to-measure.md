# K-12 Assessment Innovation Gains Momentum, but Questions Remain About What to Measure

Nashville, Tenn.

The traditional model of K-12 assessment is beginning to give way to approaches that emphasize more frequent feedback, real-world skills, and closer connections to classroom instruction. But as districts experiment with new methods, they face the challenge of finding ways to make assessments more meaningful without sacrificing consistency and reliability.

At the annual *EdWeek Market Brief* Summit in Nashville, Tenn., this week, panelists discussed how schools are rethinking assessment as states experiment with new testing approaches and educators look for better ways to understand student learning.

They also raised questions about how to measure skills that don't lend themselves easily to traditional tests and whether Artitifical Intellingence can make assessment more useful without taking time away from instruction.

The national conversation has shifted from whether standardized assessment should exist to how it can better serve students and educators, said Lillian Pace, vice president of policy and strategic advancement at KnowledgeWorks, an organization that focuses on learning and assessment that better prepares students for the future.

Six years ago, debates over assessment were largely polarized between those seeking to abandon existing testing structures and those defending them, she said. Now, she sees growing interest in developing more meaningful alternatives.

"There's a tremendous amount of hope around artificial intelligence," Pace said, particularly its potential to address longstanding challenges in measuring student learning.

That interest is also reflected in state policy. Twenty-four states applied for a recent federal competitive grant program for state assessment innovation, Pace said, despite funding being available for only three or four states. She also noted growing interest in measuring durable skills, such as communication, collaboration, and problem-solving, alongside traditional academic subjects.

Some of the most promising innovations are emerging through grant-funded partnerships that allow vendors and districts to co-develop solutions, Pace said. Traditional requests for proposals can be too restrictive for an environment where technology and assessment needs are changing quickly, she said.

Pace encouraged vendors to advocate for more flexible purchasing processes that allow for ongoing collaboration and iteration.

James Worthington, director of secondary curriculum support and MTSS in North Carolina's Iredell-Statesville Schools, discussed a pilot program that helps teachers assess durable skills through regular classroom assignments.

Working with the state and educational testing and assessment organization ETS, teachers in the district of more than 20,000 students have redesigned projects to evaluate skills like collaboration. In one example, Worthington said a group of high school students studying Shakespeare produced a puppet show, requiring them to divide responsibilities, manage conflicts, and work together.

AI is helping address one of the biggest obstacles to expanding that approach – the time required to evaluate student work, he said. An AI tool conducts an initial check using teacher-developed rubrics, allowing educators to review and override its recommendations.

"It takes the bulk of the work off that teacher's plate and allows them to focus on the actual work that's being done," Worthington said.

Teachers can also submit student work as they go and receive almost immediate feedback on what students should do differently, he said.

In Miami-Dade County Public Schools, with more than 300,000 students, the challenge is balancing the growing number of assessments with the need to protect instructional time, said Omar Riaz, assistant superintendent of assessment, research, and data analysis.

Florida's shift to administering state assessments three times a year has prompted the district to reconsider how it uses interim and diagnostic testing, he said. State assessments can identify gaps in grade-level proficiency, but often don't explain the underlying skills a student needs to develop.

The district is also exploring AI-powered tools that could provide immediate feedback on reading and other skills without requiring students to enter a formal testing environment, Riaz said.

"Can we test them dynamically? Do we need to really go into that testing environment to be able to provide a valid test result?" Riaz said.

However, not every durable skill should necessarily be measured, Pace said, noting that a concept like empathy can be difficult to assess consistently across different cultures and communities.

As assessment systems become less centralized, states and districts will also need to preserve quality and comparability, she said.

"We don't need to be overly standardized, but we don't need to be so loose that it becomes meaningless," Pace said.
