# 5 Findings on Gen Z’s Changing Priorities

New research reveals the challenges and opportunities facing young people, from improving mental health to navigating growing AI use and economic pressures.

Recent studies offer a closer look at the experiences and concerns of today’s college students.

The evolving priorities, challenges and expectations of Gen Z are shaping how they navigate college and prepare to enter the workforce.

Recent research shows signs of improvement in college students’ mental health, even as artificial intelligence takes on a growing role in young people’s personal and academic lives. Other findings highlight how economic concerns are shaping their political engagement and how early career exploration may help students build confidence in their futures.

Here are five findings from recent Inside Higher Ed Student Success coverage that shed light on how colleges and universities can better understand and support the current generation of students.

## Student mental health is showing signs of improvement.

More than four in 10 American college students are flourishing—the highest share to report positive mental health in a decade, according to new survey data from the Healthy Minds Network. Meanwhile, rates of depression and anxiety have declined slightly as the stigma around discussing mental health wanes, though significant challenges remain.

The findings come from the 2025–26 Healthy Minds Study, led by researchers at Boston University; the University of California, Los Angeles; the University of Michigan; and Wayne State University. The study includes responses from 67,477 students at 107 colleges and universities; 77 percent were between the ages of 18 and 25.

Among respondents, 43 percent were identified as flourishing, up from 38 percent the previous academic year. Meanwhile, 34 percent reported symptoms of depression, down from 37 percent, and 30 percent reported symptoms of anxiety, down from 32 percent. However, rates of suicidal ideation and self-harm remained largely unchanged, as did the share of those reporting symptoms of disordered eating (28 percent).

Still, the overall trend is encouraging, experts said.

“Just because someone is not reporting depressive symptoms does not necessarily mean that they are functioning at the highest level they can,” said Justin Heinze, a health and psychology professor at the University of Michigan and a co-author of the study. “So to have decreases in loneliness, increases in flourishing, to see decreases in perceived stigma—that’s all moving in a positive direction.”

## Young people see AI as a safer source of emotional support.

Artificial intelligence has become increasingly intertwined with young people’s personal lives, including during their college years, when many are developing their identities and building meaningful relationships. But as AI becomes a source of emotional support and companionship, new research from The Rithm Project suggests that young people don’t necessarily have to trust the technology to turn to it for advice—they just need to perceive it as less risky than confiding in another person.

Earlier this year, the organization released research based on a survey of nearly 2,400 young people ages 13 to 24—including more than 500 young adults ages 18 to 24—examining how their AI use intersects with their social lives, emotional well-being and relationships.

In November, The Rithm Project released new research that builds on those findings through interviews with 29 young people from the original study—including 19 who are either currently enrolled in college or have completed a bachelor’s or associate degree. The interviews focused on two groups identified in the initial survey: those who turn to AI for personal and emotional support and those who interact with AI characters and personas.

Alison Lee, head of research and development at The Rithm Project, said one of the most notable findings was how young people weighed the perceived risks of confiding in AI against those of opening up to another person.

“What they were saying was ‘When I turn to AI, I know it’s hallucinating, I know that it’s glazing me, I know that it’s engaging in all these problematic behaviors,’” Lee said. “‘But those feel way more manageable as a risk than when I turn to a human.’

“Turning to a human is scary, it’s vulnerable, and you’re afraid of their judgment and emotional reactivity,” she added. “But big emotional events are a hallmark of the human experience … so it makes a lot of sense for young people to search for a place where those big emotions have a safe place to land and where they can make meaning of it.”

## AI is changing college, but long-standing challenges remain.

The college experience is evolving as new technologies reshape how students learn, complete coursework and seek academic support. While AI helps some students navigate their education more efficiently, it has also introduced new concerns about academic integrity and the quality of learning.

According to Inside Higher Ed’s 2026–27 Student Voice survey of 5,034 undergraduates from 310 mostly public two- and four-year institutions, 34 percent of students say generative or agentic AI helps them complete coursework and study more efficiently, while 33 percent say it helps them feel more confident understanding and tackling difficult concepts. However, 25 percent say they have changed how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors.

Despite these technological shifts, long-standing challenges continue to affect students’ academic success and persistence. Mental health is the most commonly cited barrier, reported by 41 percent of students, followed by financial pressure at 37 percent. More than six in 10 students have also gone without at least one essential—such as food, housing or transportation—in the past year for financial reasons.

Looking ahead, 54 percent of students want their institutions to prioritize job pipelines and internships to better prepare them for the labor market. At the same time, 80 percent somewhat or strongly agree that their college is preparing them to succeed in today’s job market, even as many report stress about life after graduation.

## Economic concerns are shaping young voters’ priorities.

A variety of issues motivated young voters to head to the polls in the 2026 midterm elections, ranging from the economy and jobs to health care and the war in Iran. Their participation helped fuel historic levels of support for Democratic candidates, contributing to the party’s victories that shifted control of the U.S. Senate and House of Representatives.

New data from the Center for Information and Research on Civic Learning and Engagement (CIRCLE) at Tufts University found that the economy was the top issue for young voters in the 2026 midterm elections. Some 52 percent selected the economy and jobs as one of the three most important issues facing the country. Health care, another issue with significant economic implications, ranked second at 42 percent, followed by the war in Iran (27 percent), immigration (26 percent), education (21 percent) and climate change (20 percent).

Nationally, 66 percent of young voters ages 18 to 29 supported Democratic candidates for the U.S. House of Representatives, compared to 31 percent who supported Republicans. In several key states, the researchers found, young voters played a significant role in Democratic victories.

Leela Strong, the Newhouse executive director at CIRCLE, said during a postelection briefing that young men, rural youth and young people without a college degree—groups that helped President Trump win in 2024—were among those who shifted their support toward Democrats this year. She argued that the swing demonstrates that young voters, particularly Gen Z, are not necessarily driven by party loyalty or a single issue, but rather by a range of concerns.

“Young people, we know from all of our research, are not transactional voters,” Strong said. “They are acting on concerns about the economy and a democracy that isn’t working for their generation, and rewarding candidates who hear and see them.”

## Early career exploration can build student confidence.

Introducing career exploration earlier in students’ academic journeys may help them develop a clearer sense of their professional interests and strengthen their motivation to stay enrolled and complete their degrees. That’s the idea behind Career Discovery, a for-credit course developed by Podium Education that gives first-year students opportunities to explore different industries and potential career paths through hands-on projects with employers.

The course, which eight universities piloted in the spring, connects students with real-world professional experiences, ranging from interviewing students about their consumer preferences for L’Oréal to completing marketing-related projects for other companies. In October, the educational technology company announced that the program had expanded to two additional institutions, bringing the total number of participating universities to 10.

Early findings from end-of-course student surveys suggest promising results. Students’ self-reported clarity about their strengths and interests more than doubled, while 80 percent reported greater career confidence. The share of students who said they intended to persist in college also increased by 15 percentage points.

Chris Parrish, co-founder and president of Podium Education, said the program was developed in response to growing concerns about the return on investment of a college degree and the need to make career exploration a priority earlier on for students.

“Many of the students we serve happen to be Pell [Grant recipients], happen to be first gen, but most freshmen haven’t been part of a boardroom before at a place like L’Oréal,” Parrish said. “Bringing the world of work and what it feels and looks like to them is a really important part of context setting, and that allows students to just have a seat at the table and to feel out what they like.”
