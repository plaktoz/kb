# Google Says Schools Can’t Have Search Without AI. Some Are Finding a Way.

Districts and filtering companies were blocking AI search results long before Google’s new policy.

Months before Google told schools they could keep its search engine only if they accepted its artificial intelligence (AI) tools, Fairfax County Public Schools in Virginia developed its own solution to the problems of AI-infused search: a browser plug-in that hides AI-generated answers in students’ search results.

The district, the ninth largest in the country, wasn’t alone in seeking workarounds. Niles Community Schools in Michigan built a similar tool and posted it publicly early this year. Meanwhile, web-filtering companies have built products that suppress Google’s AI summaries.

Now some of the country’s largest districts, including New York City, Los Angeles and Chicago, are weighing dropping Google and using other search engines, The New York Times reported Nov. 18.

The Los Angeles Unified School District told EdSurge that it disabled Google’s AI Overviews and would “continue to evaluate whether these and any digital tools support student learning.” New York City Public Schools and Chicago Public Schools did not respond to EdSurge’s request for comment.

Under the policy Google announced Nov. 3, its AI tools and Google Search will be turned off by default for K-12 student accounts starting in early 2027, and administrators can turn them back on. But because AI is now built into Google Search technology, the company says schools that block its AI features will lose access to Search, too.

“We believe schools should have control over AI in their classrooms and in providing administrators with controls to help them manage their approach,” Chris Phillips, Google’s vice president and general manager for education, said in its statement.

“We hear parents and teachers' concerns about students using AI to shortcut learning, and we share the same goal: help students learn,” a Google spokesperson said in a statement to EdSurge.

Prominent politicians, including New York City Mayor Zohran Mamdani and Sen. Ed Markey, D-Mass., have cast the move as a monopolist forcing AI on children.

Some education nonprofits advocating for responsible technology use in schools see something else: a company whose products dominate the school market finally giving districts the off switch they asked for, then explaining it poorly.

As Google faces a Nov. 30 deadline to answer Markey’s letter urging CEO Sundar Pichai to reverse the policy, the question for district leaders may not be whether search and AI can be separated, but who should have to do it.

## The Backlash

Backlash from some politicians against the potential Google Search shutdown was fierce. Mamdani said the company “is trying to use its monopoly power to force AI into our public schools.”

New York City Public Schools is months into a one-year moratorium on student-facing generative AI — tools students use directly, as opposed to those built for teachers.

“No corporation should have the power to dictate how we teach our children,” Mamdani wrote on X on Nov. 3. “Our public schools exist to serve students, not to grow the profit margins of Big Tech.”

In his letter to Google, Markey said the policy “places schools in an impossible position: either give students access to potentially harmful technology or lose a critical educational research tool.” Despite growing concern over the risks AI chatbots pose to minors, he added: “Google is using its dominant position in the education technology sector to push AI chatbots onto children and teenagers, whether or not their schools believe the technology is safe.”

The overarching conversation is about who gets to decide what products kids use in classrooms and what they’re exposed to, Patricia Burch, says a professor at the University of Southern California’s Rossier School of Education. And the qualms raised by advocates aren’t specific to Google.

“Those decisions are made way before where we are now,” she says. “They’re made in product design. They’re made before the district decides the contract. It’s in the contract process. So I think that the concessions that Google is making are very late in the game and larger questions about what is the design of these products? What are the safety features? Who owns the data? That’s the important fight.”

## The Workarounds

Fairfax County Public Schools developed its plug-in to remove AI summaries from student search results before Google’s announcement, The Wall Street Journal reported in September. Now the district told EdSurge it is “reviewing Google’s recently announced changes and their potential impact on education technology policy decisions.”

Niles Community Schools took a different approach: The district made its tool public. Jim Tyler, the district’s director of technology, posted a free Chrome extension on GitHub called Hide AI Overview. The repository describes it as a way to remove Google’s AI-generated search summaries and AI Mode while leaving traditional search results in place.

Niles’ software is available to any district (it is unknown whether any schools are using it). The repository includes the extension’s source code, instructions for installing it on individual computers and directions for deploying it districtwide through the Google Admin Console.

It also explains how to keep students from turning the extension off and how to troubleshoot when Google changes its search page, which the instructions warn happens frequently.

Commercial filtering companies are designing their own fixes. Securly, which sells web-filtering and student-monitoring software to schools, announced Nov. 4 that its filter now blocks Google’s AI Overviews as well as AI Mode. The company has been working on AI filters for about two years, says CEO Tammy Wincup.

“I completely understand districts’ desires to want to have control,” Wincup says. “And so I believe that this was honestly a reaction from Google that heard them, that said ‘Hey, we need the ability to have choice and to turn it on or off.’ I guess I was surprised that there was such a big reaction because frankly we’ve been having to hack all of these pieces to make them school appropriate for decades.”

Securly applied the approach it used for cloud-based filtering to AI tools, building an AI interface over large language models. Most of the districts that work with Securly “want something between all or nothing,” she says.

## What Advocates Asked For

Education stakeholders were asking Google for the ability to use Search without AI, the University of Southern California’s Burch says. Google has a significant footprint in schools beyond Search — including the Google Classroom suite, Google Docs, Google Sheets and Google Slides.

“[Google was] early in the school game, and they’ve gotten some flack for being late in the AI game,” Burch says. “They want to make sure that it’s part of their core model inside of schools, and so they have a line in the sand.”

Erin Mote, the CEO of InnovateEDU, a nonprofit organization focused on closing opportunity gaps in education, says it’s incumbent on Google to clearly explain its change to the public, rather than putting the onus on superintendents and districts to explain the new policy to students and families.

Mote’s organization advocates for districts to have more control over the technology deployed within their educational ecosystem — and she says Google’s move is a positive step in that direction.

“We’ve called for technology developers and providers to have their student-facing and educator-facing generative AI tools off by default,” Mote says. “The person who should be making decisions about whether or not AI is used in a classroom or in an educational context for educators and for students is the school district.”

The speed at which AI technology has moved is creating a disconnect between education stakeholders, policymakers and tech companies, says Karim Meghji, president and CEO of CodeAI, the computer science education nonprofit formerly known as Code.org.

“This technology, not only is it moving fast but it has the potential to transform society and work and how we exist as humans,” he says. “And how do you bring education about that into a system that does not move quickly and is, rightly so, fearful, concerned because we don’t fully understand it?”

Backlash against education technology providers such as Google helps provide checks and balances, he says.

“Good pushback helps better focus how the technology is used with educational outcomes, evidence and efficacy in play,” he says.

Google, for its part, says the approach announced this month isn’t final. “We’ll continue to listen to feedback and evolve our approach as we work in partnership with schools, teachers, pedagogical experts, and parents on helping the next generation learn and thrive,” the company said in its announcement.
