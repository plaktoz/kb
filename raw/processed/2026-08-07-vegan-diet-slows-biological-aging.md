# UC San Diego study finds vegan diet may reduce inflammation, slow biological aging

by: [Amber Coakley](https://fox5sandiego.com/author/amber-coakley/ "Posts by Amber Coakley")

Posted: Aug 7, 2026 / 10:53 AM PDT

Updated: Aug 7, 2026 / 10:53 AM PDT

by: [Amber Coakley](https://fox5sandiego.com/author/amber-coakley/ "Posts by Amber Coakley")

Posted: Aug 7, 2026 / 10:53 AM PDT

Updated: Aug 7, 2026 / 10:53 AM PDT

SAN DIEGO (FOX 5/KUSI) — A new study led by researchers at UC San Diego suggests that switching to a vegan diet for just one month may trigger measurable biological changes associated with lower inflammation and slower biological aging.

The research, published Monday in the peer reviewed journal “MedComm,” found that healthy adults who followed a vegan diet for four weeks experienced changes in DNA methylation — a biological process that regulates whether genes are more or less active — compared with participants who ate a meat-rich diet.

Researchers say the findings offer new insight into how diet can quickly influence molecular processes linked to long-term health.

“Our genes are not our destiny,” said Dr. Jerome Mertens, an associate professor of neurosciences at the UC San Diego School of Medicine and co-senior author of the study. “The remarkable finding isn’t simply that one diet outperformed another. It’s that the epigenome responded measurably in just four weeks, showing that our biology is far more dynamic — and responsive to everyday choices — than we often assume.”

The randomized clinical trial included 48 healthy adults. After eating the same standardized diet for one week, participants were randomly assigned to either a vegan diet or a meat-rich diet for one month. Both diets contained similar calorie levels, allowing researchers to focus on the effects of food choices rather than weight loss.

Scientists analyzed more than 800,000 DNA methylation sites across the genome before and after the study.

Among the findings, researchers said participants following the vegan diet showed biological markers associated with reduced inflammation. The study found lower predicted levels of neutrophils — immune cells that drive inflammation — and higher levels of CD4 T cells, which help regulate the immune system.

Researchers also observed changes in pathways related to insulin signaling, DNA repair and cellular stress responses, along with changes in genes associated with cell growth and cancer-related pathways.

Perhaps the most notable finding, according to researchers, involved biological aging. Using specialized “epigenetic clocks” that estimate biological age based on DNA methylation patterns, researchers found evidence suggesting participants on the vegan diet experienced a slowing of biological aging over the month-long study.

However, researchers cautioned that the study does not necessarily prove a vegan diet reverses aging or prevents diseases such as cancer.

The trial involved a relatively small group of participants and lasted only one month, meaning larger and longer-term studies will be needed to determine whether the molecular changes translate into lasting health benefits.

“Our study adds to growing evidence that nutrition can shape biology at the molecular level,” said Dr. Max Storz of the University of Freiburg Medical Center, a co-senior author of the study. “The next step is to understand whether these epigenetic changes persist over time and whether they translate into meaningful improvements in long-term health.”

He says that will require larger clinical studies in the future.

The researchers also noted the observed effects appeared to be related to the composition of the diet itself rather than consuming fewer calories.

The full study, titled “A vegan diet epigenetically modulates inflammatory pathways and biological aging: Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of a one-month isocaloric vegan versus meat-rich dietary intervention,” can be reviewed [here](http://A vegan diet epigenetically modulates inflammatory pathways and biological aging: Genome-wide DNA methylation analysis of a one-month isocaloric vegan versus meat-rich dietary intervention]).
