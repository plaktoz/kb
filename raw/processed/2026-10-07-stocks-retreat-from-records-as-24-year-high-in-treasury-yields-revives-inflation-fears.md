# Stocks Retreat From Records as 24-Year High in Treasury Yields Revives Inflation Fears

Wall Street pulled back from record territory on Wednesday, with all three major equity benchmarks closing in the red as a renewed surge in long-dated Treasury yields to their highest levels in nearly a quarter century rekindled anxiety over inflation and the federal debt load.

The S&P 500 slipped 17.18 points, or 0.22%, to settle at 7,801.75, while the Dow Jones Industrial Average dropped 341.11 points, or 0.66%, to end at 51,180.17. The Nasdaq Composite lost 61.19 points, or 0.22%, to close at 27,538.69. The declines snapped four-session winning streaks for the S&P 500 and the Dow, and marked the Nasdaq's first down day in six.

The small-cap Russell 2000 fared considerably worse, sliding 1.3% and underperforming its large-cap peers.

The retreat came a day after the S&P 500 and Nasdaq both notched record closing highs. That rally had been fueled in part by easing oil prices and slightly softer bond yields, but those tailwinds reversed sharply on Wednesday.

The 10-year Treasury yield climbed as high as 5.365% during the session, its loftiest level since April 2002, while the 30-year yield touched 5.732%, a high not seen since May 2002. The moves extended a global bond sell-off that has rattled markets for weeks amid concerns over rising sovereign and corporate debt levels and the inflationary impact of elevated energy costs.

Brent crude settled above $100 per barrel, with supply concerns tied to the Iran conflict keeping pressure on energy markets. West Texas Intermediate finished 1.3% lower at $88.28 a barrel, while Brent eased 0.4% to $100.20 after the International Energy Agency agreed to accelerate the release of oil stockpiles, prioritizing diesel. The announcement helped stocks pare earlier losses.

The day's trading reflected a market caught between competing forces. Equity investors have been hoping for relief on oil prices, which would translate into relief on interest rates and ultimately support stock valuations. Yet the persistent climb in yields has made bonds increasingly competitive with equities and raised the discount rate applied to future corporate earnings.

## Fed minutes reveal internal debate

The Federal Reserve released minutes from its September policy meeting, at which officials unanimously approved the central bank's first rate increase since July 2023. The record showed divisions over the rationale for the hike, with some participants viewing it as necessary to blunt the impact of energy price shocks while others argued it was needed to curb demand-driven inflation.

Futures markets currently assign a 17.2% probability to a second consecutive rate increase at the Fed's October meeting, down from 37.6% a week earlier, according to the CME FedWatch tool. The sharp repricing suggests investors increasingly expect policymakers to hold rates steady this month even as another hike later in the year remains a live possibility.

"The market's Fed expectations are going to seesaw as it gets new bits of data," said Thomas Martin, senior portfolio manager at Globalt in Atlanta. "There's still an expectation for more rate hikes, but it isn't necessarily going to be a hike every meeting and that's what the market is telling you; October will be a pause."

He added that the third quarter was widely expected to be the weakest stretch of the year and to bring a market correction, but that correction never materialized. "So it's three cheers and let's take some money off the table," he said.

## Sector pressure and mortgage rates

Among the 11 S&P 500 sectors, industrials suffered the steepest percentage decline, while healthcare stocks led the gainers. Eight sectors finished lower on the day.

The climb in benchmark Treasury yields pushed the 30-year fixed mortgage rate to 7.49% last week, its highest level in nearly three years, according to the Mortgage Bankers Association. The move weighed on housing-related shares, with homebuilder stocks dropping 2.9% and the broader housing sector falling 2.3%.

Chip stocks, which have surged more than 80% so far this year, fell 1.2% on the session. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped around 2.3%, with Micron Technology declining roughly 2.3% and Nvidia slipping about 0.7%.

SpaceX lost 2.5% following reports that Elon Musk's aerospace company is seeking $40 billion in financing to fund purchases of Nvidia chips. The fundraising effort underscores the enormous capital requirements behind the artificial intelligence buildout that has driven much of this year's equity gains.

Declining issues overwhelmed advancers on both major exchanges. On the New York Stock Exchange, decliners outnumbered gainers by a 3.34-to-1 ratio, with 115 new highs against 497 new lows. On the Nasdaq, the ratio stood at 2.37-to-1, with 1,421 stocks rising and 3,371 falling. The Nasdaq recorded 32 new 52-week highs versus 250 new lows.

Volume on U.S. exchanges totaled 16.21 billion shares, below the 17.57 billion average over the previous 20 trading sessions.

## Earnings season looms

Investors are now turning their attention to the third-quarter earnings season, which is expected to begin in earnest next week with a wave of high-profile financial firms reporting results. Analysts anticipate S&P 500 earnings growth of 30.6% year over year for the July-through-September period, according to LSEG, with technology and energy among the strongest contributors.

The reports will be scrutinized for evidence that the massive spending on artificial intelligence infrastructure is beginning to translate into actual financial results, as well as for signals about the health of the U.S. consumer amid mounting inflationary pressures.

The 10-year Treasury auction conducted Wednesday drew strong demand, with indirect bidders, a category that includes foreign central banks, taking down 80.3% of the $39 billion offering, well above the 72.4% average of the prior 10 auctions. The robust appetite helped pull yields back from their intraday peaks and allowed stocks to trim losses heading into the close.

Bank shares were among the notable laggards, with Goldman Sachs and Bank of America each falling about 1%, while Wells Fargo, Citigroup and JPMorgan Chase also declined. Technology names sensitive to borrowing costs came under pressure as well, with CrowdStrike dropping about 5%, Palo Alto Networks falling more than 3% and Meta Platforms losing more than 2%.

The market's narrow breadth has become a growing concern. Roughly 73% of S&P 500 stocks were falling on Wednesday, and the number of stocks hitting new 52-week lows vastly exceeded those reaching new highs. The pattern suggests that while the headline indices have repeatedly set records, the underlying participation has been far less broad than the benchmark levels imply.

That dynamic has been particularly pronounced in the technology sector, where AI-related enthusiasm has kept valuations elevated even as bond yields have marched to multi-decade highs. The coming earnings season will test whether the fundamental performance can justify those valuations in an environment of persistently higher borrowing costs and elevated energy prices.
