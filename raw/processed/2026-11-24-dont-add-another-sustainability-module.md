# Don’t Add Another Sustainability Module

The five-dimensional Sustainability Decision Lens expands conventional business analysis to include factors related to environmental footprint, long-term economic value, societal impact, organizational resilience, and strategic implications.

Students develop stronger managerial judgment when they must weigh competing objectives, anticipate changing conditions, and defend decisions that involve difficult trade-offs.

Faculty can use this lens to introduce sustainability into existing cases, projects, and assignments without redesigning courses or sacrificing their primary disciplinary learning objectives.

Business faculty are being asked to incorporate more topics into their teaching than ever. Artificial intelligence, analytics, cybersecurity, geopolitical risk, sustainability, and emerging technologies are all competing for space alongside the fundamentals of accounting, finance, marketing, operations, management, and strategy. When an important new issue emerges, our instinct is often to look at the curriculum: Where can we add content? Do we need another lecture, module, case, course, or certificate?

With sustainability, there may be a simpler approach. Instead of adding another sustainability module, we can change the questions we want students to answer.

Consider an assignment in which students select a supplier. Instead of asking students to look only at the primary criteria of cost, quality, and delivery, we could also ask them to consider the environmental consequences, stakeholder effects, exposure to disruption, and longer-term strategic implications of their recommendations. The original learning objective remains intact, but the decision-making outcome becomes more realistic.

As Erik Foley argues in a March 2022 article in AACSB Insights, “Sustainability Across the Curriculum,” sustainability education should reinforce business fundamentals rather than replace them. Foley’s approach connects sustainability learning objectives to individual business disciplines. The next step is to move beyond simply adding sustainability content to a course. Instead, instructors can incorporate it in ways that develop students’ capacity for responsible decision-making.

To help faculty make that shift, I propose a five-dimensional Sustainability Decision Lens, a practical framework for evaluating business decisions across five thematic areas: environment, economy, society, resilience, and strategy. The lens can be added to an existing case, project, simulation, or assignment without redesigning a course. More importantly, it can help students develop what I call sustainable managerial judgment: the ability to recognize competing objectives, evaluate trade-offs, anticipate consequences, and make defensible decisions under uncertainty.

## Five Questions That Widen the Focus

Most business assignments understandably emphasize a primary objective. A finance problem might focus on return, an operations exercise on cost and reliability, or a marketing case on customer value. The Sustainability Decision Lens does not replace those objectives. It widens the decision-making parameters by asking five additional questions:

1. Environmental: What consequences does the decision create?

Students consider the environmental effects of a decision. Depending on the situation, these effects might be that a new strategy consumes more energy, water, or other resources; produces higher emissions; generates more waste; lengthens transportation routes; or has negative impacts on product life cycles.

The purpose is not simply to identify the option with the smallest environmental footprint but to understand whether reducing one environmental effect creates environmental, economic, operational, or social consequences elsewhere.

2. Economic: Can the decision create sustainable economic value?

Students consider investment requirements, operating costs, productivity, pricing, margins, and long-term financial viability. An environmentally attractive alternative that cannot remain economically viable may never achieve its intended benefits. The reverse can also be true: A profitable business model may draw on scarce resources, depend on vulnerable infrastructure, or generate greater environmental liabilities over time, which risks long-term economic viability.

Students must move beyond asking, “Will this strategy make money?” to asking, “Will this decision continue creating economic value as conditions change?”

The educational value of these five questions does not lie in assigning five scores to discover the “sustainable” answer, but in understanding the trade-offs among them.

3. Social: Who benefits, who pays, and who assumes the risk?

Students identify the people and institutions affected by a recommendation and consider how value, cost, and risk are distributed. They learn that business decisions distribute benefits, costs, and risks across stakeholders, but those effects are not always visible in a conventional financial analysis. The goal is not to find a decision that benefits everyone equally. It is to make visible any consequences that might otherwise remain outside the analysis.

4. Resilience: What happens when conditions change?

Students must determine what level of resilience the situation requires and what the organization is willing to pay for it. In the process, they learn that decisions are too often evaluated only under expected conditions. The resilience question requires them to ask what happens when those expectations are wrong. A low-cost supplier might create geographic concentration. Lean inventory might improve working capital but increase vulnerability to shortages. Redundant capacity might protect against disruption but require additional capital and resources.

In this case, students learn that neither maximum efficiency nor maximum redundancy is automatically sustainable.

5. Strategic: Does the decision strengthen long-term competitive advantage?

Finally, students consider whether the decision strengthens the organization’s ability to compete over time. Sustainability can influence innovation, differentiation, customer trust, reputation, talent, access to capital, regulatory positioning, and organizational capabilities. This question extends the relevance of sustainability beyond corporate responsibility to long-term strategic viability.

Together, these five questions encourage students to consider environmental consequences, long-term economic value, the distribution of costs and benefits, resilience under changing conditions, and strategic competitiveness. The objective is not to assign five scores and add them together to discover the “sustainable” answer. The educational value lies in understanding the trade-offs among them.

## Putting the Lens to Work

Consider a classroom assignment in which students advise the leaders of a pharmaceutical company deciding where to manufacture an essential medicine. They might initially compare potential locations using familiar business criteria such as manufacturing costs, product quality, capacity, labor, logistics, regulatory requirements, taxes, and expected return on investment.

The instructor then asks them to look at the problem more closely through the Sustainability Decision Lens:

The environmental dimension might lead students to discover differences in production processes, transportation distances, emissions, energy use, water consumption, or waste production. Students could also examine whether moving production closer to patients actually reduces the product’s overall environmental footprint once differences in energy sources, manufacturing efficiency, and transportation requirements are considered.

This encourages them to move beyond simple assumptions—for example, that local production is automatically more sustainable—and instead evaluate environmental consequences across the full manufacturing and distribution system.

This exercise presents sustainability not as a set of principles added on to an isolated assignment, but as a lens through which students learn to make responsible strategic decisions.

The economic dimension requires students to make a difficult choice. Suppose it would cost 20 percent more to manufacture a drug at a location closer to U.S. patients. Would greater supply security justify the additional expense? Could automation or productivity improvements offset it? Would some of that cost ultimately be passed to patients or payers?

That leads naturally to the social dimension. A more resilient supply chain could reduce the likelihood of drug shortages, but higher production costs could affect affordability. Students now must consider two legitimate social objectives—access to medicine and continuity of supply—that may point in different directions.

The resilience dimension adds another complication. Suppose the lowest-cost option depends heavily on a single geographic region for a critical ingredient. What happens during a geopolitical conflict, natural disaster, transportation disruption, or regulatory shutdown? A second source could reduce that vulnerability, but it would also require additional investment and resources.

Finally, the strategic dimension asks students to look beyond the immediate transaction. Could a more resilient manufacturing network strengthen supply chain reliability, improve relationships with national healthcare systems or governments, or enhance the company’s ability to respond to future disruptions? If so, resilience is no longer simply an added cost. It may become a strategic capability.

As students work through the five questions, they will come to an important realization: There is no predetermined “sustainable” answer. After this exploration, students on one team might still recommend the lowest-cost option, but they now must defend that recommendation against risks and consequences that were absent from the original analysis. Another team might conclude that greater resilience justifies higher costs. Either recommendation can be credible if students understand the trade-offs and can rationally defend their assumptions.

That is the point of the exercise. Sustainability is no longer just a set of principles that have been added on to an isolated assignment. It’s a lens through which students learn to make responsible strategic decisions.

## Make Assignments More Like Management

The exercise can become more experiential without requiring faculty to develop an entirely new project. As Michael Carrillo explains in an April 2026 article, “How Experiential Learning Signals Career Readiness,” powerful experiential learning requires students to work with ambiguity, respond to feedback, and demonstrate accountability for their decisions. Faculty can use the Sustainability Decision Lens to introduce many of those conditions directly into an existing assignment.

For example, after students make their initial manufacturing recommendations, the instructor could provide new information: A major supplier has shut down, energy prices have increased sharply, a geopolitical conflict threatens a key transportation route, or a hospital system is warning about potential shortages. Students must then determine whether their original recommendations still hold.

Faculty could also assign different stakeholder perspectives or require teams to defend their recommendations before a simulated executive committee. One requirement is particularly useful: Ask students to explain not only what they recommend but what they are giving up by choosing it.

When students must consider sustainability issues, they realize that the strength of their recommendations often depends on how well they weigh competing objectives.

That final question forces students to confront opportunity costs and trade-offs rather than search for a perfect solution. It also moves them from analyzing a problem toward making a decision. Obie Paul Byrum makes a related argument in a June 2026 article, “Teaching Strategy as an Act of Decision-Making.” He emphasizes the importance of moving students beyond analysis toward choices that require explicit trade-offs and resource commitments.

Students can become proficient at analyzing a problem without becoming equally proficient at deciding what should be done. Sustainability provides an especially useful context for developing the latter capability. When they must consider sustainability issues, they realize that the strength of their recommendations often depends on how well they weigh competing objectives—not just on whether they can identify a single “correct” answer.

## Start With One Assignment

The practical appeal of the Sustainability Decision Lens is that faculty do not need to redesign an entire course to use it. They can start with one assignment that already works, keep its primary disciplinary objective, and use the five questions to broaden the analysis.

Faculty who want an even simpler starting point could begin with three questions:

What important consequence is missing from the current analysis?

Which assumption behind the recommendation is most vulnerable if conditions change?

Who benefits from the recommendation, and who bears additional cost or risk?

The goal is not to make every assignment about sustainability. It is to help students recognize when a seemingly straightforward business decision has consequences that conventional analysis may overlook. If faculty across a business school begin incorporating this lens on select assignments, sustainability no longer sits in one course or module. Students encounter it where they will encounter it in their careers: embedded in ordinary business decisions.

## From Knowledge to Judgment

Tomorrow’s executives are unlikely to receive a memo labeled “Make this sustainability decision.” Instead, they will be asked whether to build a factory, select a supplier, launch a product, automate a process, enter a market, invest in artificial intelligence, or allocate capital. They need to recognize that the consequences of their decisions extend beyond economic and strategic considerations to include their organizations’ environmental footprints, societal impacts, and long-term resilience.

Our students therefore need to do more than accumulate sustainability knowledge. They need to develop sustainable managerial judgment: the capacity to see beyond a single objective. They must be able to recognize competing interests, anticipate how circumstances might change, and make defensible choices when there is no perfect answer.

The five-dimensional Sustainability Decision Lens offers one practical way to develop that capability. It does not require faculty to abandon disciplinary fundamentals or become sustainability specialists. Instead, it gives them a way to make the business decisions they already teach more representative of the decisions students will eventually face.

Business schools do not always need their faculty in finance, accounting, marketing, operations, analytics, technology, healthcare, and strategy to teach additional sustainability modules. Sometimes the most meaningful curricular change begins with asking them to do something much smaller.

Change the questions they ask.
