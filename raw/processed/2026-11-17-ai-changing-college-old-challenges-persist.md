# AI Changing College; Old Challenges Persist

The student experience is in transition, but unmet financial needs and mental health continue to limit students’ success, according to new Student Voice data.

Students’ assessment of their educational quality remains high. And while artificial intelligence is introducing new anxieties—and its implementation thus far leaves something to be desired—it’s also empowering some students. Still, out-of-the-classroom issues continue to bear on students’ academic success, according to data from Inside Higher Ed’s main Student Voice survey of 2026–27. The sample includes 5,034 undergraduates from 310 mostly public two- and four-year institutions.

Among the survey’s top-line findings:

About a third of students each say generative or agentic AI helps them complete coursework and study more efficiently (34 percent) and feel more confident understanding and taking on difficult concepts (33 percent). But a quarter say they have changed how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors.

Students’ top priorities for responsible AI use in higher education are to help them learn, not just do the work for them (65 percent) and to be transparent about when and how AI is being used (60 percent).

More than eight in 10 students (81 percent) rate the quality of their education as good or excellent, but just 24 percent are highly confident in their institution’s efforts to keep that education affordable.

Mental health is the most commonly cited barrier to academic success and persistence, cited by 41 percent of students. Financial pressure is a close second, at 37 percent. Six in 10 students (61 percent) have also gone without at least one essential—such as food, housing or transportation—in the last year for financial reasons.

Nearly a third of students (32 percent) haven’t participated in any extra- or co-curricular activities since they enrolled, jumping to 66 percent of community college students. Both two- and four-year students are most likely to agree that introducing financial incentives would boost their participation.

More than half of students (54 percent) want their institution to prioritize job pipelines and internships to better prepare them for the labor market. And while most are stressed about some aspect of preparing for life after college, 80 percent somewhat or strongly agree they’re being well prepared to succeed in today’s job market.

While few students report clear insight into their program’s postgraduate outcomes (17 percent), most value college beyond postgraduate earnings—and personal interest or passion in the subject is still a top driver of choice of major.

Read on for more findings and analysis of the survey, which asked about academics, health and wellness, involvement in campus life beyond the classroom, career readiness, and AI across the student experience.

## Academics, Trust and AI

As in last year’s main Student Voice survey, most students approve of the education they’re getting so far, rating it as good (52 percent) or excellent (29 percent). This is relatively consistent across institution types. Nearly half of students (53 percent) report that they’re very well served by their institution’s course delivery options and schedules; that share increases, to 62 percent among respondents 25 and older and among community college students—those who may be in disproportionate need of extra flexibility. Most of the remainder indicate that they’re somewhat well served (37 percent over all).

About the Survey

Student Voice is an ongoing survey and reporting series that seeks to elevate the student perspective in conversations about student success. These findings are from the main annual survey of our 2026–27 survey cycle, Student Voice: Amplified, conducted in October. Some 5,034 students from 310 mostly public two- and four-year institutions responded.

Similarly, three in four students agree, somewhat (46 percent) or strongly (28 percent), that they trust their institution and believe it is reliable, cares about their well-being and acts with integrity—good news for a sector struggling to regain public confidence. Community college students are especially likely to strongly agree on trust, at 41 percent.

Closer to half of students over all agree that they trust colleges and universities in general (55 percent combined). Students are less trusting of their institution’s efforts to keep education affordable for students, however: Just 25 percent are very or extremely confident that their college or university is taking meaningful steps toward affordability, such as reducing unnecessary costs, limiting tuition increases or pooling resources—though this jumps to 44 percent among community college students. Twelve percent of students over all are highly confident that colleges and universities as a whole are taking meaningful steps to keep education affordable.

Looking more closely at the changing academic experience, eight in 10 students indicate that they use generative or agentic AI routinely. Asked about the biggest ways that AI is changing their approach to college, a third each say AI helps them complete coursework and study more efficiently and feel more confident understanding and tackling difficult concepts. Three in 10 say AI is making them less reliant on professors or tutoring centers for academic help. This can cut positively or negatively, since AI tools can make extra help more accessible while also potentially replacing valuable human interaction. More concretely on the negative side, 25 percent of all students report that they’ve changed how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors, and 16 percent say they find themselves relying too heavily on AI to think or write on their own.

This is the full list of top ways generative or agentic AI has changed how students approach coursework, if at all; respondents could select up to three options:

They complete their coursework and study more efficiently: 34%

They feel more confident understanding and tackling difficult concepts: 33%

They rely less on professors or tutoring centers for academic help: 30%

They change how they write to avoid being falsely flagged by AI detectors: 25%

They do not use generative or agentic AI for college work: 20%

AI helps them be more organized or otherwise boosts accessibility: 19%

They find themself relying on AI too heavily to think or write on their own: 16%

AI has not changed how they approach college: 9%

For Tina R. Austin, an instructor and researcher at the University of California, Los Angeles, focused on AI literacy, metacognition and assessment redesign, the findings reflect what she sees in her own teaching: “Students are really more concerned than anyone else about their own learning,” she said. “They’re really distinguishing between AI just being there as a productivity tool and completing their work, and actually coming home and feeling like they learned something.”

Preserving learning in 2026 and beyond doesn’t mean abstaining from AI, she said, but rather purposely incorporating AI-free moments into the process. Austin has developed a teaching framework called UnBlooms, for example, that rethinks Benjamin Bloom’s taxonomy for an era in which machines can synthesize and generate. Ultimately, it seeks to help students become critical, appropriate users of AI and to help instructors grade students’ thinking, not just their output.

Austin said that faculty members are often introduced to AI tools in professional development settings that feel more like “tool talks,” emphasizing AI as a productivity booster instead of something that can advance their pedagogy. This can lead instructors to equate AI adoption with AI-augmented teaching, and students can lose motivation as a result, she said.

Students’ own definitions of responsible AI use in higher ed settings are similarly nuanced: Nearly two-thirds say it means helping them learn rather than simply doing work for them. Six in 10 say it’s about transparency in when and how AI is used. More than half each cite protecting students’ privacy, keeping humans involved in important decisions and giving students clear guidance on when and how to use AI.

A separate IHE/Hanover Research survey of 1,665 faculty members, published earlier this month, found that 88 percent said AI had somewhat or very negatively affected their confidence in the authenticity of students’ work or communications. Two in three said it had negatively affected trust between professors and students. But Student Voice respondents’ emphasis on transparency—coupled with other recent campus developments—suggest concerns about trust go both ways. At Eastern Illinois University, for instance, students this fall started a petition opposing professors’ use of AI as a “substitute” for learning materials. Supporters said they’re not trying to ban AI use, just to clarify its use as a tool, including through university policy. They want professors to disclose how and when they use AI in the classes. And they want the university to address the “root causes” of AI use, including growing faculty workloads.

Even with their nuanced views on AI, significant shares of Student Voice respondents think it has high or very high potential to improve the student experience in the following areas:

Academic help (e.g., supporting learning through AI-enabled tutors trained on the specific course material): 41%

Career planning (e.g., helping me build résumés, practice interviews and match skills to job openings): 33%

Student success coaching (e.g., giving personalized tips on time management, study habits and deadlines): 31%

Administrative services (e.g., speeding up campus tasks like housing applications, parking or tech support): 28%

Academic advising (e.g., giving instant guidance on major requirements and graduation tracking): 26%

Financial aid guidance (e.g., answering questions about my tuition bill, FAFSA and scholarships): 25%

Wellness support (e.g., connecting me quickly to campus mental health resources, stress-relief tools or coping strategies): 20%

Many student success administrators also see AI as having transformative potential for the student experience, according to the IHE/Hanover 2026 Survey of College and University Student Success Administrators—perhaps especially in light of their institutional capacity concerns.

## Health and Well-Being

Even as AI reshapes academic life, students’ most frequently cited barriers to academic success are familiar: Mental health challenges top the list, with 41 percent citing stress, anxiety or depression that disrupts their focus or academic performance. This increases to 48 percent among respondents who have seriously considered stopping out of college. Financial pressure from insufficient financial aid or the need to work too many hours to cover living expenses follows, at 37 percent over all. This, too, increases to 48 percent for students who have thought about leaving college, underscoring the link between these two issues and persistence in the Student Voice data and in other research. Unengaging classes rank third over all, followed by difficulty staying connected or motivated. Other barriers, such as not seeing the purpose of college or navigating confusing campus systems, fall farther down the list. And even as financial pressures rank high, fewer—one in 10 students—cite a more chronic sense of basic needs insecurity, defined as struggling with stable housing, getting enough food or finding reliable transportation.

Just 11 percent of students over all say they do not experience significant barriers to academic success.

One more note: In this survey, 11 percent of students cite lack of academic preparation as a top barrier to student success. Among the student success administrators in their recent survey, 47 percent cited academic readiness as a top barrier.

Paralleling some of these findings, 28 percent of students rate their mental health as below average or poor. The same share say this of their financial well-being. Both are comparable to last year’s survey.

Sleep may be an underdiscussed struggle: 41 percent of students rate their sleep habits as below average or poor. About three in 10 give similarly low ratings to their nutrition and to their ability to manage stress. Students are somewhat more positive about their academic fit: 38 percent rate it above average or excellent, versus 31 percent for their sense of social belonging.

Just over a third (34 percent) rate their overall well-being as above average or excellent, while 51 percent say it’s average.

Financial strain is also apparent in the things students go without: More than six in 10 say they delayed or went without at least one essential in the past year for financial reasons. Thirty-one percent say they sacrificed sleep or rest because of paid-work obligations, and at least one in five each went without medical care, transportation, mental health care or food. Fifteen percent went without technology needed for school, and 14 percent went without medication. Consistent with the earlier finding on financial pressure versus more chronic basic needs insecurity, fewer indicate they’ve gone without housing or utilities.

For every category of need, students over 25 are more likely to report going without. Some 19 percent of students aged 18 to 24 have gone without food in the last year, for example, versus 30 percent of older students. Just a quarter of students 25 and up have not gone without any basic need, compared with 41 percent of their 18- to 24-year-old peers. And among students working 30 hours or more per week (n=915), just 21 percent haven’t gone without any essential for financial reasons in the last year.

Students also demonstrate limited awareness of emergency financial aid. Although three-quarters of student success administrators said in their survey that their institution offers some form of emergency aid, just 21 percent of Student Voice respondents say their college offers it. Sixty-two percent are unsure whether such aid exists. Only 13 percent say they know how to apply, and 7 percent say they have accessed it. This is similar to last year’s survey, and relatively consistent across respondents and institution types.

Jhenai Chandler, vice president for research, policy and student success at NASPA-Student Affairs Administrators in Higher Education, said her organization’s own top issues data reinforces the idea that student health, safety and well-being can’t rest with a single division or initiative. “Practitioners shared that while emergency aid programs and one-stop-shop models are essential,” she said of NASPA’s survey, “they often lack the capacity and resources to meet the scale of student need.” Supporting student success requires a “more comprehensive, institutionwide approach that integrates student well-being into institutional structures and is sustained through consistent funding and strong campus and community partnerships.”

Policy has an important role to play in helping establish sustainable funding sources, she added.

To the extent that AI can help connect students with resources, the National Student Legal Defense Network recently published guidelines for AI use in basic needs services, emphasizing privacy, accurate information, the continued centering of human staff and coordination with governments.

This year’s Student Voice survey also asked about online sports betting, prediction markets and other digital wagering. Fifteen percent of students say they have participated in such activities since enrolling, and 38 percent agree that digital wagering merits more attention at their institution. Few colleges and university are tracking digital wagering as a student success issue, however, according to IHE-surveyed administrators.

Additionally, relatively few students grasp the full cost of attendance, beyond tuition: 29 percent say they have a clear understanding of all costs, meaning there are no surprises and they can budget appropriately, similar to last year. This increases to 36 percent among students 25 and up. But more understanding of costs doesn’t necessarily equate to more financial security, since older students are also more likely to report unfulfilled basic needs.

## Campus Connections

Despite these challenges, most students describe their campus’s climate positively: 73 percent say most or nearly all students at their institution feel welcomed, valued and supported. About a quarter say that only describes how some students feel. This is relatively consistent across the sample. Most students (76 percent) also say they feel somewhat or very supported in exploring different perspectives, even those that challenge their beliefs. In IHE’s 2026 Survey of College and University Chief Academic Officers with Hanover, 68 percent of provosts rated their campus’s overall climate for civil discourse as good or excellent.

The student survey also asked how recent federal actions seeking to limit college diversity, equity and inclusion efforts had affected the college experience, including respondents’ sense of belonging. Some 27 percent say those actions have negatively affected their experience, including 11 percent who describe the impact as significant. The share is higher among nonbinary students (68 percent negative, n=205) and Black students (35 percent, n=469), compared with 29 percent each of Hispanic (n=796) and Asian American and Pacific Islander students (n=808), and 24 percent of white students (n=2,490). Another 18 percent over all say they don’t feel personally affected but believe their peers have been negatively impacted. Nearly four in 10 (38 percent) report no real impact, while 11 percent say the impact has been positive to some degree.

Participation in campus life remains limited. Asked what would most encourage more campus involvement, if their institution could only invest in one approach, 40 percent over all and 42 percent of community college students endorse financial incentives such as compensation, stipends or tuition credits. (Student success leaders in their own survey tend to favor this approach as well, along with demonstrating the point of participation.)

Twenty percent of students favor more flexible opportunities that accommodate off-campus commitments, while 16 percent want dedicated, campuswide hours without scheduled classes and 14 percent say institutions should do a better job demonstrating the benefits of participation, such as direct career skills gained, networking opportunities or personal growth milestones. Nine percent endorse more virtual or hybrid options, increasing to 15 percent for students over 25.

As for how campus connections could help students falling through the cracks, these are the people students say they would turn to for support if they were struggling academically or personally, or considering leaving college, selecting all that apply:

Someone outside their institution (e.g., a family member or friend): 68%

A peer or fellow student: 46%

A specific academic adviser: 27%

A specific faculty member: 22%

A specific student support staff member (e.g., a counselor, coach or financial aid adviser): 21%

Another staff member they interact with regularly (e.g., a dining hall, custodial or security staff member): 9%

A specific administrator: 8%

No one: 5%

Not sure: 4%

Eleanor Eckerson Peters, senior director of research at the Institute for Higher Education Policy, said it makes sense that many students would turn to someone like a family member if they were struggling or thinking about leaving college. Yet the relatively small shares of students reporting that they would turn to faculty members, advisers or student support staff—and the 5 percent who say they would turn to no one—indicates that there’s “room for institutions to build stronger connections with their students,” she said. AI tools designed to encourage students to contact or connect with the right people and supports on campus—not replace human connection—hold promise here, she added, since nearly half of students already say they’d rely on a peer.

## Preparing for What Comes Next

While outcomes matter more than ever, students’ views of postsecondary education’s value are not limited to earnings: Asked how college programs should be evaluated in light of new regulations tying program-level earnings outcomes to federal financial aid eligibility, 78 percent say programs should be judged primarily by the personal and educational value they provide, even if graduates do not earn higher salaries. Twenty-two percent favor judging programs primarily by their financial outcomes—a figure relatively consistent across the sample. Additionally, asked what informed their own choice of major once at college, 54 percent of students cite personal passion or interest in the field, ahead of factors such as expected salary and earning potential (34 percent), job-placement rates for graduates in the field (18 percent)—and even (and especially) the impact of AI and other technology on their career prospects (6 percent). About one in five cite flexibility for career changes (21 percent). This, too, is somewhat consistent across the sample.

Students’ access to information about postgraduate outcomes remains limited, however: 17 percent say they know detailed employment, salary or graduate school outcome data for their program, up from last year’s 12 percent. Some 46 percent know some general information. Just 14 percent say their college makes this information readily available; 11 percent are unsure where to find it.

Postcollege readiness is also a source of concern: Just 8 percent of respondents aren’t stressed in some way about preparing for life after college (one in five for those over 25), though their biggest sources of stress vary widely. From a long list of options, the largest share, 19 percent, cite worries about affording life after graduation. Some 16 percent cite overwhelming pressure to succeed, while 14 percent worry they don’t have enough internship or work experience.

What do students want their institutions to prioritize more to better prepare them for the job market? Like last year, direct connections between college and work rank highly: More than half want their college to expand job pipelines and internship opportunities.

This is what students say their institution should prioritize to better prepare them for the job market, selecting up to three options:

Job pipelines and internships: expanding company partnerships for internships, co-ops and hiring pipelines: 54%

Applied class projects: integrating more real-world projects and employer-aligned tasks directly into regular coursework: 35%

Résumé and interview training: better help with explaining their specific skills to employers and automated screening systems: 33%

Up-to-date coursework and credentials: updating class materials and credential options faster to match changing industry trends: 28%

Core career/durable skills: more focus on things like critical thinking, communication, ethics and teamwork in classes: 27%

Better career center resources: increasing staff, funding and specialized coaching at the campus career center: 22%

AI and digital literacy: more training on how to use emerging AI tools in their field: 11%

No changes needed: Their institution’s current career preparation is fully sufficient: 8%

Even with those wants, 80 percent of students somewhat or strongly agree that their college is preparing them with the skills, credentials and experiences they need to succeed in today’s job market.

Regarding the career-readiness trifecta of credential attainment, experiential readiness and skills articulation, student success administrators in their recent survey flagged relative student-readiness gaps in the latter two areas.

Amanda Butkiewicz, program director of Work + Learn at Arizona State University’s University College, said that higher ed needs to design the full student experience so that “meaningful work-based learning is built into students’ education, rather than dependent on their ability to seek out additional opportunities.” One way to do that? Embed industry-connected projects into coursework, which requires colleges to find ways for employers to engage beyond traditional internships and offer faculty the “autonomy, support and flexibility to integrate these experiences into their courses without unnecessary administrative barriers.”

Closing the skills-articulation gap also requires opportunities for reflection, Butkiewicz said, as leaders can’t just assume students recognize or can explain the skills they’re learning through campus employment, community engagement, leadership and coursework.

“Guided reflection should help students identify what they learned, how they applied it and what evidence demonstrates their growth,” she said. And pairing that reflection with a “tangible work product or portfolio artifact gives students something concrete to discuss and share.”
