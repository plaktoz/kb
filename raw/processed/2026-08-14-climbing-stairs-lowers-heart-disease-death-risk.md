# Heart disease: Climbing just 6 flights of stairs a day can lower risk

Researchers from the University of East Anglia and Norfolk and Norwich University Hospital (UK) conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis of 9 studies covering 480,479 participants (ages 35-84, 53% female, median follow-up 14 years), published in the American Journal of Cardiovascular Drugs.

Key findings: people who often climbed stairs had a 39% lower risk of dying from cardiovascular disease and 24% lower risk of dying from any cause, compared with those who rarely climbed stairs. Sub-analysis suggested around 6 flights (~60-70 steps/day) was the point at which cardiovascular risk reduction became apparent, with more stairs generally producing greater benefit, though even small changes had a measurable impact. Regular stair-climbers were also less likely to develop cardiovascular disease in the first place.

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death worldwide, with cases nearly doubling between 1990 and 2019 (523 million cases globally). Stair-climbing is a form of vigorous physical activity accessible to the more than a quarter of adults worldwide who don't meet recommended activity levels.

Limitations: most included studies relied on self-report, and measurement of stair-climbing varied (some counted only home stairs, others measured ability rather than frequency); the review is observational, so it supports rather than proves a causal relationship — people who climb stairs regularly may also be healthier in other ways. None of the studies used wearable trackers, which researchers flagged as an opportunity for future, more objective research.

Stair-climbing isn't suitable for everyone — people with osteoarthritis, musculoskeletal leg disorders, or impaired mobility may need alternatives like brisk walking, cycling, swimming, rowing, or sit-to-stand exercises. Experts interviewed noted that even brief (1-2 minute) bursts of movement performed several times a day may lower cardiovascular event and death risk, reinforcing that structured workouts aren't required to gain cardiovascular benefit.
