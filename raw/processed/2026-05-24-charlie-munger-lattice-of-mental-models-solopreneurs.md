# Charlie Munger: How to Use the Lattice of Mental Models to Make Better Decisions

Charlie Munger's "latticework of mental models" is a system for combining ideas across disciplines (psychology, economics, physics, biology, mathematics) rather than relying on a single familiar heuristic. Munger's diagnosis: people fail not from lacking information but from applying one model to every situation (e.g. letting a "friendly client" override economic reality). Solopreneurs and independent operators need this discipline more than large organizations because they lack the structural checks (committees, finance teams, legal review) that catch individual bias.

Selected models and where they bite in practice:
- Psychology (34 tendencies): Liking/Loving Tendency (overlooking a client's red flags because you like them), Anchoring Bias (staying near your first-ever rate for years), Overoptimism (underestimating project time — apply a 2x multiplier), Availability-Misweighing (one bad client experience distorting your view of all clients), Social-Proof Tendency (copying a competitor's pricing model without asking if it's right for you), Lollapalooza Effect (multiple biases compounding at once, e.g. during a product launch).
- Thinking tools (18 frameworks): Inversion (ask what guarantees failure, then avoid it), Circle of Competence (decline work outside your expertise even if offered), First Principles Thinking (build pricing from the actual value delivered, not competitor anchors), Second-Order Thinking (a high-paying client today may consume capacity needed for higher-value work later), Falsification (actively ask ideal clients why they *wouldn't* buy your new offer).
- Economics/business (20 principles): Opportunity Cost, Pareto Principle (a small number of clients often drive most revenue), Margin of Safety (keep 3 months' expenses in cash), Gresham's Law (low-rate platforms crowd out quality — exit them).
- Competitive advantage (19 models): Economic moats via niche expertise, switching costs, distribution you own (email list vs. platform dependency), moat durability (relationships are durable; specific software skills are not).
- Math/probability (12 models): Expected Value, Regression to the Mean (plan conservatively after an unusually strong month), Power Laws (concentrate on the few high-leverage inputs), Kelly-type thinking (never risk a large share of capital on one bet).
- Physics/systems (11 models): Redundancy (avoid single points of failure — one client, one platform, one income stream), Margin/safety buffers (30% timeline buffer, 20% haircut on optimistic cash projections), Positive/negative feedback loops.
- Biology/evolution (6 models): Red Queen Effect (you must keep improving just to hold position), Autopsy Learning (write a one-page post-mortem after every failed launch or lost client).
- Organizational thinking (9 models): treat yourself as your own board via quarterly self-review; documented SOPs reduce cognitive load and make a solo business transferable.

Practical starting technique — "Rule of 3": pick one real decision this week, apply three models from different categories, write one sentence on how each changes your thinking, then decide. Example starting sets: a client proposal → Liking/Loving Tendency + Opportunity Cost + Inversion; a pricing change → Anchoring Bias + Elasticity + Second-Order Thinking; a new offer → Falsification + Base Rates + First-Conclusion Bias. Core thesis: better thinking compounds — the goal isn't perfect decisions, but a track record of decisions you're satisfied with.
