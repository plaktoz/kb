# 5 Mental Models to Remove (Some of) the Confusion from Parenting

Parenting advice is mostly tactics without principles — what works for one child or family may fail for another. Five principle-based mental models offer more durable guidance than specific tactics:

1. **Adaptation** (from evolutionary biology): "Adaptation is as good as it has to be; it need not be the best that could be designed. Adaptation depends on context" (Geerat Vermeij). Reframing "changing the rules" as "adapting to the situation" removes the pressure to be perfectly consistent — different rules for home versus a public space, or different activities for different children's interests, are adaptation, not failure.

2. **Velocity vs. speed**: Speed is movement; velocity is movement with direction. Parents often move fast all day (school runs, activities, dinner) without asking where they're actually going. Defining a goal state for time with your kids — what memories, what kind of parent/child relationship you want — gives a frame of reference for daily choices, and may cut unnecessary activity.

3. **Algebraic equivalence**: Two things need not be the same (x=y) to be equal in value (x+y=5 has many valid solutions). Siblings who count pancakes to check fairness, or accuse a parent of loving one child more, can be addressed by focusing on equal value rather than identical treatment — different interactions with each child can carry equal weight.

4. **Seizing the middle** (from chess strategy, where controlling the center board matters more than the flanks): Parents can't control every single behavior, so focus on holding the "middle territory" that matters most — for the author, this is mutual respect and trust — rather than fighting every battle (e.g., a messy room) at the cost of the child tuning out the parent altogether.

5. **Inversion**: Reaching a goal has two components — adding forces for it, and removing forces against it (Kurt Lewin's force field analysis). Rather than always adding new tactics, look for obstacles to remove. Example: a baby waking four times nightly for nine months was resolved not by a new sleep technique but by making the room warmer — the baby was cold.

Parenting is inherently complex because "the territory" (each child, each situation) keeps changing; a principle-based mental-models approach provides insight to find new solutions as circumstances shift, rather than relying on one-size-fits-all tactics.
