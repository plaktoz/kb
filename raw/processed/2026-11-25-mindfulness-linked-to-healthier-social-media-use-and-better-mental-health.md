# Mindfulness linked to healthier social media use and better mental health

People with higher levels of mindfulness are more likely to experience positive social media use and better mental health outcomes, according to a new UCL-led paper.

Published in *PLOS One*, the review found that people who reported being more mindful in their day-to-day lives were more likely to report healthier social media use and lower levels of depression, anxiety, stress, loneliness and fear of missing out (FoMO).

Mindfulness involves paying attention to our thoughts, feelings and what is happening in the present moment in a non-judgemental way. Previous research has shown that cultivating mindfulness, through practices such as meditation or mindfulness-based therapy, can help reduce symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression.

As part of a systematic review, researchers assessed 43 quantitative studies involving more than 28,000 participants to explore whether mindfulness could play a protective role in the relationship between social media use and mental health.

Researchers say this is important, as in recent years there has been significant debate as to whether social media use poses a risk to mental health or worsens existing struggles. While concerns about harm often dominate public discussion, social media may also offer benefits for some people, such as opportunities for social connection.

Corresponding author Dr Marc S Tibber (UCL Division of Psychology and Language Sciences) said: "Our findings support a move away from reductive questions about whether social media is inherently good or bad, towards an exploration of how complex interactions between individuals, technologies, and the broader social contexts in which they are embedded shape both the potential benefits and harms of social media for mental health.

"Specifically, the review suggests that mindfulness may play a protective role and, whilst further research is needed, its status as a trainable skill means it represents a potential target for interventions aimed at promoting healthier digital engagement."

In the review, the age of participants ranged from 10 to 92 years old, with university students making up the largest group of those studied (46.5%). All studies had participants self-report their own mindfulness or mindful social media use. More than half (24 of 43) of the studies used the Mindful Attention Awareness Scale, a widely used questionnaire that measures how attentive and aware people are of their present-moment experiences in everyday life. Overall, 39 of the 43 studies reviewed supported the idea that mindfulness may help protect against some of the negative mental health impacts associated with social media use.

Dr Tibber said while formal practices such as meditation and yoga are great ways to cultivate mindfulness skills, simple practices that encourage people to pause before engaging on social media, whether they are using it for entertainment or to socialise with friends, can help them make more conscious choices about their online behaviour.

In many of the studies reviewed, mindfulness or mindful social media use appeared to buffer or weaken links between online experiences and poorer mental health outcomes, with higher levels of mindfulness associated with lower problematic social media usage.

In one study, participants were required to complete mindfulness exercises, self-monitor smartphone usage and track their mood, which found significant beneficial impacts on mental health outcomes, specifically stress and anxiety, FoMO, and levels of problematic social media use.

Another study had participants restrict their social media use for eight days, which led to improvements in mindful attention but increased negative emotions, relative to those who had no restrictions.

Dr Tibber said: "Our findings are consistent with my observations as a clinician as well as wider research and suggest that mindfulness may help people develop a healthier relationship with social media. By supporting people to notice their thoughts, feelings, and impulses without immediately acting on them, mindfulness may make it easier to step back from habitual reactions, direct attention more deliberately, and engage with social media in a more intentional way. This matters in an online environment where platforms are designed to attract and retain our attention."

While mindfulness can be cultivated through training and may also represent a promising target for clinical, educational, and public health interventions, further longitudinal and experimental research is needed before firm conclusions can be drawn.

The authors emphasised that promoting healthier social media use is not simply a matter of individual choice or responsibility. The design of platforms and the social environments in which they are used are also important determinants of how social media affects mental health.

**Study limitations**

The researchers note that most studies were cross-sectional, meaning it is not possible to conclude that mindfulness causes better outcomes. Existing research is also biased towards younger, predominantly Western populations, limiting how confidently findings can be generalised.
