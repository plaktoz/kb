# Higher grocery prices linked with worse food insufficiency, mental health

UNIVERSITY PARK, Pa. — As grocery prices continue to rise, a study by researchers at Penn State found that in addition to increased spending, the price hikes also are associated with higher risk of food insufficiency and worse mental health conditions.

The study, published in the journal Applied Economic Perspectives and Policy, used data from the Household Pulse Survey, a large-scale, nationally representative survey collected by the U.S. Census Bureau. Designed to measure how emergent issues, such as the COVID-19 pandemic and related economic disruptions, affect U.S. households, the survey data provided the researchers with information on how households cope with increased prices at the grocery store.

Their analysis revealed that households could be sorted into four distinct profiles, characterized by not changing shopping habits; minor adjustments like swapping brand name for cheaper, generic products; comprehensive adjustments across financial obligations to increase the grocery budget; and going without despite significant need.

While exact effects varied from group to group, the researchers found that households in all four profiles experienced increased food spending, higher risk of food insufficiency and worse mental health.

Yizao Liu, associate professor of agricultural economics and lead author of the study, said the findings could help policymakers better identify which households are facing hardship and suggest that household well-being is shaped by multiple factors.

“Previous work has suggested that when deprivations overlap in this way, effective policy typically requires coordinated interventions instead of isolated programs,” she said. “Bundled support — such as combining food assistance, rent relief and mental health resources — may be most effective at addressing the complex nature of inflation-related distress.”

Recent factors like the COVID-19 pandemic, climate-related supply disruptions and rising energy costs have resulted in food prices that keep rising, the researchers said. In the United States, grocery prices rose by more than 20% between 2020 and 2024, outpacing overall inflation.

While there has been a great deal of attention to rising food prices and the economic forces behind them, the researchers added, less is known about what households actually do when those higher prices start putting pressure on their budgets.

Liu explained that those responses can take very different forms. Some households may switch to generic brands, look for sales, use coupons or eat out less. Others may be forced to make much harder choices, such as buying more affordable but less healthy food, delaying medical care, working more hours, using credit, reducing savings or cutting back on utilities.

“We thought it was important to study these behaviors together because households rarely use just one strategy,” she said. “The combination of strategies can tell us a great deal about the level of pressure a household is experiencing and whether those coping efforts are actually protecting their well-being.”

For the study, the researchers looked at data on four main variables: weekly household spending on food and groceries, weekly household spending on prepared meals, household food insufficiency, and respondents' mental health.

The team found that households fall into four very different patterns when responding to rising prices.

About 44% of households, which the team dubbed "minimal responders," reported relatively few changes in the way they shopped. Another 28%, called "price-sensitive substituters," made common adjustments such as switching to generic products, shopping at lower-cost stores and cutting back on dining out.

About 20% of households were "comprehensive adjusters," using a wider range of strategies to cope including reducing food-related spending, delaying major purchases and — in some cases — delaying medical treatment.

The final 8%, called "resource-strained sacrificers," made some of the most drastic trade-offs. These included taking on additional work, relying on credit or loans, reducing savings or retirement contributions, and cutting back on utilities such as heating or electricity.

Inflation was associated with increased food spending and a higher chance of experiencing food insufficiency and anxiety, but the specific risk levels were different among the groups. For example, the resource-strained sacrificers experienced some of the smallest increases in food insufficiency and anxiety.

“It could be that this group was already at such risk for anxiety and food insufficiency prior to inflation that the needle doesn’t move much with inflation,” Liu said. "These findings suggest that different coping strategies act more as markers of vulnerability than as effective shields against the effects of food price hikes."

Additionally, price-sensitive substituters may use strategies that lower spending but come with more anxiety. Meanwhile, comprehensive adjusters and resource-strained sacrificers try drastic strategies only to remain highly vulnerable to food insufficiency and mental health issues.

“Some strategies, such as switching brands or shopping at cheaper stores, may help control spending in the short run,” Liu said. “But households that started using more intensive coping strategies still showed high levels of food hardship and anxiety. So, in many cases, these behaviors are not signs that a household has successfully adapted. They are signals that the household is already under significant pressure.”

The researchers said that in the future, additional studies could examine how families move between different coping patterns over time and whether certain policies can help prevent households from moving into more severe forms of hardship.

Yuxiang Zhang, doctoral candidate in energy, environmental and food economics at Penn State, co-authored the paper.
