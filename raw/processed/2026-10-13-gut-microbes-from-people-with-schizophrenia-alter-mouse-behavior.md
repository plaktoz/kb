# Gut microbes from people with schizophrenia alter mouse behavior

Researchers at the University of Coimbra in Portugal recently investigated whether gut microbes from people with schizophrenia could affect behavior and immune activity in mice. Their findings, published in *Translational Psychiatry*, hint at a possible immune connection between the gut and brain.

Schizophrenia is an often debilitating psychiatric disorder that can disrupt people's thinking, perception and behavior. While genetic and environmental factors can contribute to its emergence, researchers are still trying to understand its underlying biological processes.

Previous studies have identified differences in the gut microbiome, the community of microorganisms living in the intestine, of people with schizophrenia. Yet how these differences might influence brain function still needs further investigation.

## A microbial swap

In their new study, the team collected stool samples from three people with schizophrenia and three healthy donors. They then fed young mice gelatin portions containing material from individual donors repeatedly over a period of five weeks.

The mice already had their own gut microbial communities, so the transferred material entered an already established environment. The researchers then compared animals receiving samples from people with schizophrenia with healthy-donor recipients and untreated mice.

To assess their behavior, the team introduced the mice into a T-shaped maze. Each mouse explored one arm of the maze, took a brief break and then returned. Mice receiving schizophrenia-donor samples were less likely to choose the other arm than animals in either comparison group.

This test probes short-term working memory and the ability to switch between choices, although reduced alternation does not necessarily reflect forgetting alone.

In a separate test, the mice could approach a cage containing an unfamiliar mouse or an empty cage. Those receiving schizophrenia-donor samples showed less preference for investigating the unfamiliar animal.

## Following the immune trail

The researchers also examined microbes associated with the lining of the ileum, the final part of the small intestine. They observed changes in the relative abundance of several bacterial groups, alongside increases in intestinal inflammatory markers.

Compared with untreated mice, schizophrenia-donor recipients had lower levels of occludin, a protein that helps seal gaps between intestinal lining cells. This suggests a weakened intestinal barrier, although the researchers did not directly measure its permeability.

Blood tests revealed higher levels of two immune signaling proteins, interferon-gamma and interleukin-17, in animals that received gut microbes from people with schizophrenia compared with untreated animals. These increases were not statistically significant relative to healthy-donor recipients.

Immune cells called microglia in the prefrontal cortex, a brain region involved in cognition and social behavior, also exhibited some changes. These cells help maintain brain tissue and remodel connections between neurons.

The branching extensions of these cells were found to be less complex, while molecular measurements indicated a more reactive immune state. Together, the team's findings suggest a possible connection between intestinal microbes, circulating immune signals and brain inflammation, yet they do not establish whether any of these changes caused the others.

## A partial match in human brains

The team examined a separate collection of brain tissue obtained after death from four people with schizophrenia and three people without it. These samples showed some overlapping changes in microglial and inflammatory markers, although the patterns did not match completely.

Both the stool-donor sample and human brain comparison were small, which limits how broadly the study's findings can be applied. The experiment also does not establish that gut microbes cause or prompt the development of schizophrenia in humans.

Future experiments could change the microbes or block particular immune signals in mice, then assess whether this prevents the observed brain and behavioral changes. This would help determine whether the proposed gut-brain pathway could eventually be targeted therapeutically.
