# Our Readers Share Their Best Productivity Tips

Productivity advice is everywhere, but most of it assumes one brain type: focus on a single task, do the most important thing first, ignore distractions. Readers were asked what actually works for them, and the answer was that there is no single perfect method — the best tips are the simplest, most accessible ones that help people start, stay focused, and keep moving without burning out.

**Reduce overwhelm.** Tips included "eat the frog" (do the hardest thing first), prioritizing by importance or deadline, limiting each day to three key tasks, and breaking big projects into small, checklist-sized pieces to get a small hit of accomplishment with each step. One reader uses a random number generator to pick which task to do next, purely to eliminate decision fatigue.

**Manage attention.** Readers rely on the Pomodoro technique, a 40-10-10 method (40 minutes of focused work, 10 minutes for correspondence, 10 minutes for a break), accountability partners, phone-blocking apps like Brick, and simply putting the phone out of reach.

**Use external systems.** Google Tasks linked to calendar reminders, color-coding, emailing to-do lists to oneself, and a sticky-note Kanban board with Now/Near/Later columns (capped at 2-3 "Now" items) were popular systems for keeping projects visible and prioritized.

**Protect energy.** Several readers reframed productivity as energy management rather than time management: screen-free breaks every 30-60 minutes, stepping outside every 15-30 minutes, prioritizing sleep (one reader credited magnesium glycinate), and starting the day with quiet personal rituals before anyone else is awake.

**Create motivation.** Tactics included listening to a productivity podcast or "study with me" video while doing mundane tasks, timer-boxed cleaning sprints, temptation bundling (pairing an undesirable task with a desirable one, like a walk before coffee), and habit stacking a new habit onto an existing automatic one.

**Build sustainable habits.** Long-term systems ranged from monthly chore charts that roll over unfinished items, to fixed weekly task-domains (e.g., "Monday is for emails"), to a strict zero-inbox discipline built on identical daily wind-down and morning routines, to a weekly "harvesting" ritual that pulls key takeaways out of a running notebook.

The overarching theme: productivity is deeply personal, and the right system is the one that reduces overwhelm and protects energy rather than the one that looks most impressive on paper.
