# What Procrastination Really Is and How to Stop Procrastinating for Good

"Procrastination" derives from the Latin procrastinare, to postpone until tomorrow. The act of delaying something that needs to be completed is the definition of procrastination. People who procrastinate often delay priorities to focus on less critical, more pleasurable tasks.

Dr. Tim Pychyl, a procrastination researcher, defines procrastination as a subconscious desire to feel good right now — a problem of self-regulation, not time management. Dr. Fuschia Sirois similarly views procrastination as a voluntary delay individuals resort to despite understanding its detrimental effects; it's more about struggling with mood management than managing time poorly. Ultimately, people who procrastinate don't avoid tasks — they avoid the negative emotions associated with those tasks.

## 10 causes of procrastination
1. Lack of motivation — the task is complicated, frustrating, dull, or repetitive.
2. Fear of failure — avoiding the task prevents the possibility of failing at it.
3. Stress about the outcome.
4. Perfectionism — wanting to complete every task perfectly impedes progress.
5. More pleasing distractions available in the environment.
6. "Present bias" — we're not in touch with our future selves; fMRI studies show different brain regions process our present vs. future selves.
7. Feeling overwhelmed by a task that seems too large or abstract.
8. Social anxiety — fear of judgment makes even minor tasks feel insurmountable.
9. Decision fatigue after lengthy decision-making processes.
10. Different dopamine regulation — chronic procrastinators may have altered dopamine control, making it hard to feel motivated until a task becomes urgent.

## What happens in the brain when you procrastinate
Every day is a struggle between the limbic system (which produces emotions and drives fight/flight/freeze reactions) and the prefrontal cortex (rational decision-making). When the limbic system wins under stress, anxiety, or fear, it makes us freeze rather than act — and the prefrontal cortex can't rationally override it in highly stressful moments. Repeated procrastination then becomes a habit because the avoidance behavior's positive reinforcement (feeling safe and comfortable) gets reinforced.

## Health and social costs
People who procrastinate have an increased risk of cardiovascular disease and hypertension, per research by Prof. Sirois, partly due to long-term stress and partly from delaying doctor visits, tests, and workouts. A 2023 study found the links between chronic procrastination and poor health are accounted for mainly by higher stress.

## 8 tips to overcome procrastination
1. **Forgive yourself** — students who self-forgave for procrastinating on one exam procrastinated less on the next.
2. **Find meaning in the task** — ask "What would the future me want me to do right now?"
3. **Do the task for just five minutes** — starting is the hardest part; a timer often carries you past the five-minute mark.
4. **Eliminate distractions** — a UC study found it takes 23 minutes 15 seconds to get back on track after a distraction.
5. **Break down the task** into smaller, manageable steps.
6. **Block out time** for specific tasks on your calendar — reduces uncertainty about when a task starts and ends, and deadlines improve performance.
7. **Use the 333 rule** or other grounding techniques if anxiety spikes near a deadline.
8. **Make it fun** — set a reward for finishing, since the brain responds to pleasurable incentives.
