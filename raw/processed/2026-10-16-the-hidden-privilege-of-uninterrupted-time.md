# The hidden privilege of uninterrupted time

It sholdn't be afforded to just one parent.

The school calls in the middle of the workday. Your child is sick. That means you have to leave the meeting, cancel the rest of your afternoon, and rearrange appointments for tomorrow. Research shows that when this happens to a father, he has a childcare problem. But when it happens to a mother, she can *become* the childcare problem.

That distinction explains something we don't talk about enough when we discuss working parents. The inequality isn't just about who spends more hours doing laundry, cooking dinner, or making appointments for the family. It's also about which parent gets uninterrupted time to work and what happens when family life (inevitably) disrupts it.

## It's about more than hours

We usually assess the unfairness that occurs with working parents by looking at hours. How many hours is one parent spending over the other. Who makes dinner most nights? Who helps with homework? Who drives the kids to their orthodontist appointments? But another important aspect that gets overlooked is how much time you can devote to work without being interrupted.

Three hours of work is different than three hours of focus. Most default parents tell me there is often some family matter that requires attention during the day. Schedule the parent-teacher conference. Order larger cleats. Get a birthday gift. Fill out that permission slip. Wonder if your child's rash is no big deal or will you be at the pediatrician's office half the day tomorrow. Ok, now back to that quarterly report you are working on.

The problem isn't really the number of minutes taken away from work. It's the projects they interrupt. The tough assignment that gets broken up into smaller chunks. The good idea you have to abandon part way through. The project that is supposed to be done by morning that you now have to take home and finish after the kids go to bed. Yes, you can still get everything done on time and excel at your job. But you've had to work around interruptions that someone else may never have to consider.

## A cumulative effect

None of these items seem too burdensome on their own. Which is the point. Nevertheless, cumulatively they can take up a chunk of your day. This is why I loved the book *Fair Play* by Eve Rodsky. Her argument wasn't simply that women perform more household labor. It was that so much of family life involves noticing, anticipating, planning, and remembering.

In one study, [researchers looked at 322 mothers](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11761833/) with small children and asked them about common household responsibilities. They wanted to understand who did the task but also who did the invisible work of making sure it got done. Mothers did about 73 percent of the planning and organizing and 64 percent of the actual execution. So, your partner might take your son to the dentist, but you found a dentist that takes your insurance, made the appointment, added it to the family calendar, and reminded your husband to leave on time.
