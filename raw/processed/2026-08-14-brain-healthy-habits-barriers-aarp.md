# What's Getting in the Way of Brain-Healthy Habits?

More than 10 years of research shows building certain lifestyle habits — moving more, learning new things, being social, and eating the Mediterranean way — bolsters brain health. The U.S. POINTER study found people 60+ who made positive changes in these areas improved mental sharpness over a two-year trial. In 2025, AARP surveyed adults 50+ to identify common barriers, then asked experts for solutions.

**"I don't have time."** Dr. Kirk Erickson (AdventHealth Neuroscience Institute) recommends small steps: don't jump into an hour of exercise if sedentary; even 10 minutes counts, adding 5-10 minutes weekly. For meal prep, Dr. Annie Fenn (physician-chef, *The Brain Health Kitchen*) suggests strategic shopping — pre-poached salmon, grilled tofu, precut vegetables, and canned fish (salmon, anchovies, sardines) for quick omega-3-rich, brain-healthy meals.

**"I don't enjoy it."** In the AARP survey, 17% skip exercise because they dislike it, and 20% prefer solitude over socializing. Erickson: find activities you actually like — "If you don't like swimming, don't force yourself to go to the pool." Fenn starts MIND-diet conversions with one food group people already like, tailoring to cultural heritage (e.g., leafy-green-and-bean-rich African diets, vegetable-heavy Asian diets). Dr. Seth Gale (Brigham and Women's Brain Health Program) suggests cognitively stimulating hobbies tied to existing interests (e.g., watching history documentaries and discussing them, building models) and just one or two group activities per season for socializing.

**"It's too expensive."** Cost was the top barrier to healthy eating for 30% of survey respondents, but frozen berries/vegetables, dried/canned beans, and bulk whole grains are budget-friendly. Hearing aids are a bigger cost barrier — hearing loss is one of the most treatable dementia risk factors (per AARP's Global Council on Brain Health); the ACHIEVE trial found hearing aids delayed cognitive decline in at-risk people and reduced isolation/loneliness over three years. Cheaper options now include over-the-counter hearing aids and hearing-aid-capable earbuds.

**"It's too late to start" / "I'm not mobile enough."** Fewer than 15% of adults 65+ meet recommended activity guidelines, and mobility was the leading exercise barrier in the 2025 AARP survey. Erickson: "I've never seen anyone who's at an age where they can't get started" — chair, bench, or wall-supported exercises work for those who can't walk safely. Fenn cites a 2022 *Neurology* study of 72,000+ people 55+ who replaced 10% of ultra-processed foods with minimally processed ones and saw a 19% lower dementia risk over 10 years.

**Key takeaways**: Barriers include time, money, enjoyment, age, and mobility concerns. Small practical changes make healthy habits easier to sustain, and it's never too late — exercise can be adapted for mobility limitations.
