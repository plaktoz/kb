# Why Smokers May Have a Lower Parkinson's Risk: It's Not the Nicotine

A study published in *JAMA Neurology* suggests carbon monoxide exposure — not nicotine — may help explain the long-observed link between smoking and reduced Parkinson's disease risk.

## Key Findings

Researchers analyzed data from 512,701 participants in the China Kadoorie Biobank, tracking health outcomes over 12 years. During that period, 1,131 participants developed Parkinson's disease.

- Regular smoking correlated with lower Parkinson's risk, consistent with prior research
- Crucially, **non-smokers with higher exhaled carbon monoxide levels also showed lower Parkinson's risk** — in a dose-dependent pattern
- This protective association was **specific to Parkinson's**, not other neurodegenerative diseases or smoking-related conditions like lung cancer

Earlier research had proposed nicotine as the protective agent, but a randomized clinical trial found nicotine therapy offered no benefit and may have worsened symptoms.

## Expert Perspectives

Toni Pitcher, PhD (University of Otago), noted this is "the first large-scale demonstration" of reduced Parkinson's incidence linked to higher exhaled CO in non-smokers, pointing to carbon monoxide's anti-inflammatory brain effects.

Salvador Ventura, PhD (Autonomous University of Barcelona) cautioned that an association with lower disease incidence reveals nothing about whether administering the substance would slow progression in diagnosed patients — "these are two distinct questions."

## Limitations

- Carbon monoxide and smoking data were collected only at baseline, not throughout the 12-year follow-up
- Parkinson's cases came only from death/disease registries and insurance systems — no primary care data
- The cohort's mean age of 52 is relatively young for a Parkinson's study, limiting case numbers

## Important Caveat

Researchers and independent experts strongly emphasize these findings provide **no justification for smoking**. As Ventura noted, the study itself found higher rates of lung cancer, cardiovascular disease, and mortality among smokers.

Trials are currently underway testing a low-dose liquid carbon monoxide drug formulation for Parkinson's patients.
