# The Innovation Strategy Most Companies Miss

Companies often build products for wealthy markets first and adapt them for the rest of the world. Dartmouth's Vijay Govindarajan and MIT's Amos Winter, co-authors of "Global by Design," argue that designing for the toughest constraints first — affordability, portability, limited infrastructure — produces products that perform better everywhere. They call this "reverse innovation 2.0": Govindarajan's original reverse-innovation concept viewed the opportunity as a business-strategy question; combined with Winter's engineering perspective, it becomes a design methodology.

Winter argues emerging markets present the greatest economic growth and the most compelling human problems, letting innovators create both social impact and viable businesses simultaneously. Govindarajan identifies three converging forces pushing companies toward this approach: slowing growth in rich countries, the risk that emerging-market competitors could disrupt multinationals in their home turf, and a growing affordability crisis even within the US.

They outline three "merits" to evaluate before launching a global-by-design initiative: problem merit (does solving this affect a large number of people in a meaningful, measurable way?), innovation merit (is genuinely new technology required, or can existing solutions be adapted?), and value merit (will stakeholders support both creating and disseminating the solution, i.e., is there a viable business model?). They also describe "elucidating requirements" — deep field research living among target users (Govindarajan's GE Healthcare team spent two months in rural India before designing a $400 EKG machine, versus the $20,000 US version, arriving at four requirements: affordability, portability, no dependence on grid electricity, and easy repair).

On AI's role: Winter says AI is powerful for aggregating background information quickly but is poorly equipped to directly solve these problems, since much relevant information is unrecorded (emotions, tacit knowledge), AI lacks embodied understanding of the physical world, and building stakeholder rapport through face-to-face interaction remains central to good design.

The biggest obstacle to reverse innovation, per Govindarajan, isn't technology — it's organizational "dominant logic": entrenched systems, incentives, and capabilities built around serving rich-country markets first, which is "like moving the Titanic 180 degrees." His advice to under-resourced teams: give innovation teams *less* money, which forces out-of-the-box thinking, and include a few organizational "mavericks" who propose unconventional ideas. Their top sectors craving this approach: education, healthcare, and energy.
