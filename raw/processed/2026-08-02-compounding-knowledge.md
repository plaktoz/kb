# Compounding Knowledge

The article examines how Warren Buffett built extraordinary investment success through deliberately accumulating non-expiring knowledge over decades.

## The Mental Filing Cabinet

Alice Schroeder, author of Buffett's biography *The Snowball*, describes Buffett as having built "immense vertical filing cabinet in his brain of layers and layers...of files of information" spanning 70+ years — assembled through reading extensively across industries, visiting companies, and studying annual reports even for businesses he didn't own.

## Expiring vs. Lasting Information

Most people consume information that won't matter next month. Buffett focused on knowledge about things that "change very, very slowly or not at all," enabling compounding over time.

Signs of expiring information:
- It's marketed to you
- Lacks nuance; tells *what* happened, not *why*
- Irrelevant within a month or year

Durable information, by contrast, accumulates and compounds. Each new piece builds on prior understanding, forming a richer mental model over time.

## Pattern Matching

By doing his own thinking with detailed, slow-changing information, Buffett learned "to see what other people are missing." Independent thinking combined with deep, accumulated knowledge enables pattern recognition that others lack.

## Retrieval vs. Retention

A crucial distinction: "retrieving information is different from having it already in your head." When decisions require 50 pieces of information simultaneously, you need it internalized, not just searchable. Munger and Buffett internalize knowledge rather than looking it up.

## Reflection Questions

- Will you care about what you're reading in a year?
- Are you building cumulative knowledge or spreading too thin?
- What in your life is unlikely to change?
- Are you focused on the durable, or are you consuming the fleeting?
