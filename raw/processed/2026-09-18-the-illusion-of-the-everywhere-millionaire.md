# The Illusion of the "Everywhere Millionaire"

source_url: https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/the-emergence-of-skill/202609/the-illusion-of-the-everywhere-millionaire

---

By Arturo Hernandez, Ph.D. (blog: The Emergence of Skill), reviewed by Ekua Hagan. Updated September 18, 2026.

The piece opens with a classroom exercise borrowed from Kahneman and Tversky, describing a shy, orderly man named Steve and asking whether he's more likely a librarian or salesperson. Most students guess librarian, but the author explains this ignores base rates: "There are about 140,000 librarians in the United States and more than 13 million people working in sales." Students fixate on Steve's personality traits rather than actual job probabilities.

The author connects this bias to a *Wall Street Journal* story about "Everywhere Millionaires," ordinary business owners — like hot dog entrepreneur Dick Portillo — who became wealthy running local companies. The author argues this narrative and the Steve example represent the same error in opposite directions: one ignores base rates by overweighting personal traits, the other overweights a rare outcome without considering how many people tried and failed.

Key statistics cited include the population of "Everywhere Millionaires": "Three million, in a country of about 340 million people," which the author translates to roughly 1–2 people per hundred. He contrasts this with survivorship bias, noting that "roughly a third of businesses survive 10 years," while Portillo's business lasted fifty.

The author also critiques the book's economists for selectively applying base-rate reasoning — correctly noting that most founders come from non-wealthy backgrounds (since most people are non-wealthy), but never calculating the reverse: how many total people attempt entrepreneurship versus how many succeed. He notes the claim that "a rich kid has six times the chance of starting a successful business as a poor kid" lacks context for comparison.

The essay concludes by cautioning readers not to base major life decisions on outlier success stories, closing with the line: "don't expect the yacht."
