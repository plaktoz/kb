# Can AI Actually Improve Decision-Making?

A company can save thousands of hours with AI and still become worse at making decisions. That is the problem with many of the metrics executives are using to judge AI today.

We count tasks automated, hours saved and work produced. But after 25 years working across brand, growth and technology and seeing the real business pain points of thousands of clients, I've learned that efficiency is not the same as effectiveness.

Now, let me ask you this: Are your people making better decisions because AI is there?

If your answer is not really, or you're not 100% confident to say yes, I would question how much value the technology is really creating for your business.

## Why Faster Decisions Aren't Automatically Better Decisions

Companies are now setting the bar too low when they judge AI primarily by hours saved or tasks completed.

You have to remember that productivity metrics only show how AI changes workload, not the work quality. When we look at a [2026 Stanford study](https://www.gsb.stanford.edu/faculty-research/publications/human-ai-accounting-early-evidence-field) of 200,000-plus accounting transactions, it says while AI boosted overall classification accuracy, errors spiked whenever accountants deferred to the system over established industry standards. This only tells us that AI delivers peak value when it sharpens human expertise, not when it replaces it.

That is the same standard I apply in my business. We've worked with clients from across industries and have seen firsthand the bottlenecks, repetitive processes, unnecessary steps and work-arounds that people have learned to live with. This shapes how we approach AI today.

So instead of asking, "What can we automate?" I ask, "What problem should no longer exist?"

If AI gives an employee an answer faster, we have improved speed. If it helps that employee spot a risk, challenge an assumption or choose a better course of action, we have improved the business.

Recognize the difference. Because faster decisions are not automatically better decisions. AI should make good judgment easier to exercise, not make human judgment unnecessary.

## How To Measure AI Success Beyond Productivity

Executives should measure speed. But I'd add three questions to the scorecard:

**1. Did the decision improve?** Speed creates value only when we're moving in the right direction.

**2. Did the employee become more capable?** A good system should help people become better at their jobs, not more dependent on its answers.

**3. Is accountability still clear?** "ChatGPT told me to" or "According to Claude" are not acceptable answers. If the organization cannot identify who verified the recommendation or had the authority to challenge it, that's a leadership problem.

## Balancing AI Automation With Human Judgment

The more routine the situation, the easier it is to automate. But when a situation doesn't fit the standard process, what's going to happen next?

Let's say a longtime client requests an urgent order outside the agreed turnaround time. An automated system might reject it because it doesn't meet the standard requirements. But an experienced account manager understands the client's situation, knows what's at stake in the relationship, and can work with operations to determine whether an exception is possible without compromising other commitments.

That's the kind of decision that requires more than following a predefined process. It requires context, experience and accountability. These exceptions are where experience matters, and this is what I mean when I say AI + Human hybrid workforce.

That also doesn't mean adding a rubber-stamp approval to every AI task. Employees must understand why a situation needs review, have the authority to resolve it and know when to escalate.

Leaders must define which decisions AI can handle, when people should intervene and who takes responsibility. Without those boundaries, employees either trust the system blindly or hesitate to act without management approval. Neither creates a stronger business.

## Protecting Critical Thinking In An AI-Driven Workplace

Polished work does not automatically mean the person who produced it understands the decision behind it at all. Therefore, do not measure the success of AI solely by production. While I want processes moving faster with repetitive work removed by AI, increased production without increased capability means nothing.

[Studies on real-world generative AI use](https://www.microsoft.com/en-us/research/publication/the-impact-of-generative-ai-on-critical-thinking-self-reported-reductions-in-cognitive-effort-and-confidence-effects-from-a-survey-of-knowledge-workers/) reveal that overreliance on AI can erode critical thinking. Without strong subject-matter expertise, team members simply can't evaluate whether AI-generated work is useful at all.

As AI gets better at producing polished answers, I would argue that industry expertise becomes even more important. Someone still has to know when an answer looks right but isn't and have the judgment to challenge it.

If an employee can produce a credible analysis or client response in minutes, reviewing only the final output tells you less than it used to. Leaders need to know what assumptions were challenged, what information was verified, why was the recommendation even accepted over another. Otherwise, AI can only make weak reasoning harder to detect.

## Using AI To Solve The Right Business Problems

AI will likely continue taking over routine tasks, but how can businesses use that freed-up time to improve decisions and solve bigger problems?

In my experience working with clients across industries, most of the biggest opportunities are in the recurring errors that delay tasks or slow down entire teams, or the processes employees have worked around for years because no one has addressed the underlying problem.

Examine these areas before investing in another AI solution. Rather than automating an "inefficient" process, question why that process exists in the first place and whether AI can help improve or eliminate the problem altogether.

So when executives ask whether their AI investment is working, look beyond the hours saved. What recurring problems have been eliminated, what decisions are your people now better equipped to make and what difference, if any, are your clients seeing?

That is the AI + Human hybrid workforce I believe in. AI will not ask you to need fewer people in your company. Your people should be able to think better, improve and solve what matters with the help of AI.
