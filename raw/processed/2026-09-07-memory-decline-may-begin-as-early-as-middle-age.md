# Memory Decline May Begin as Early as Middle Age, a New Study Suggests

Subtle cognitive changes — including forgetfulness and mild memory loss — are common with aging, largely due to changes in the hippocampus, the brain region central to memory formation.

A new study in *Cerebral Cortex* suggests memory decline may begin earlier than previously believed, and offers insight into the underlying mechanisms.

## Study Design

Researchers recruited 60 adults aged 18–74. Participants viewed pictures of objects and scenes paired with specific faces, then after a 5-minute rest, completed a memory test while undergoing MRI scanning.

Co-lead author Ian McDonough, PhD (Binghamton University) explained the task targets the hippocampus's ability to "bind or combine arbitrary pieces of information together," which underlies most human memory.

## Key Findings

Researchers observed a significant drop in memory accuracy between young adults (ages 20–30) and middle-aged participants (ages 50–63), suggesting middle age may be the true memory transition point.

A notable distinction emerged in how errors occurred:

- Young adults made mistakes because original memory patterns failed to activate.
- Older adults (63–74) showed increased hippocampal reactivation during errors — the opposite of what was expected.

McDonough noted this was "the most surprising finding," explaining that hippocampal reactivation in young adults normally signals accurate, vivid recall. In older adults, the same pattern instead correlated with less accurate memories, indicating the hippocampus undergoes fundamental functional changes with age — not simply a reduction in activity.

## Expert Commentary

Manisha Parulekar, MD (Hackensack University Medical Center) said the study confirms that "neurological shifts leading to memory loss may begin much earlier," with meaningful accuracy declines visible by the 50s.

She highlighted that the aging brain doesn't simply "power down" — it remains highly active but begins to misbind information. High activity in young adults produces crisp memories; in older adults, that same activity "jumbles details together."

Andrew Tarulli, MD (Atlantic Health Overlook Medical Center) described the study as "an early step in developing a biomarker" of memory loss anatomy, noting it isn't yet clinically applicable but could eventually supplement existing evaluation tools.

## How to Support Brain Health

Zack Ramilevich, MD (Baptist Health South Florida) noted there is no guaranteed prevention for memory loss, but recommended:

- Mentally stimulating activities (reading, hobbies, learning)
- Mediterranean or MIND-style diet
- Limiting alcohol; not smoking
- Regular physical activity
- Maintaining social connections
- Managing blood pressure, diabetes, and cholesterol
- Consistent, adequate sleep; treating sleep apnea

## When to Seek Help

Dung Trinh, MD (MemorialCare / Healthy Brain Clinic) urged people not to wait until problems become severe. Occasional forgetfulness is normal; what warrants attention is "a persistent decline from a person's previous level of functioning" — such as repeatedly forgetting important conversations, getting lost in familiar places, or difficulty managing finances.

He recommended discussing concerns with a primary-care physician, who can conduct cognitive testing, medication review, lab work, and specialist referrals when appropriate.
