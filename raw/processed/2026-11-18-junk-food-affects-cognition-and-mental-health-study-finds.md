# Junk food affects cognition and mental health, study finds

A new study led by Flinders University has lifted the lid on how bad dietary choices in childhood and adolescence can contribute to mental health and cognitive problems and potentially other diseases.

A comprehensive review of a wide range of data collected around Australia gives a clarion call about the effect of poor diet on cognitive and academic performance – as well as the potential development of other disorders.

The findings show high consumption of energy-dense, nutrient-poor foods and sugary drinks are associated with young people’s mental health, wellbeing and academic results – with experts calling for more awareness and positive change in the community.

“Promoting healthy nutrition and limiting ‘junk’ foods high in added chemicals, preservatives, saturated fats, extra sugars and salt will instil good habits from early in life and promote optimal long-term health,” says research associate Dr Zegeye Abebe Abitew, the first author of the new article published in Current Nutrition Reports.

“We need to educate parents, consumers, educators and young people about the importance of healthy eating in childhood and adolescence,” says Dr Abebe Abitew, from the College of Medicine and Public Health.

“While cost-of-living pressures can make healthy choices challenging, combining nutrition education with structural changes to improve affordability and access to healthy foods is essential for supporting children’s health and development.”

A balanced diet of fruits, vegetables, whole grains, seafood, legumes, and dairy provide essential vitamins and minerals that support children’s growth and development.

Senior author Associate Professor Yohannes Melaku, a nutrition and public health expert at the Flinders Health and Medical Research Institute (FHMRI), adds: “In these vital formative years of children’s growth and development, there are clear benefits of following a healthy diet high in fruit, veg, whole grains and fish compared to eating too many ‘energy dense’ fatty, sweet and highly processed grains, red meat and other foods.”

“These results show a healthy dietary pattern with better mental health and cognitive and academic performance but an unhealthy diet based on fattening and ultra-processed foods is associated with poorer mental health and lower cognitive and academic outcomes,” he says.

While there were mixed outcomes for cardiometabolic outcomes, researchers say there is a need for more standardised methods to define and inform better dietary information and interventions.

The results were drawn from 17 leading studies, including data from parents and child participants in Western Australia’s Raine Study, as well as information collected about children’s diets in South Australia, Victoria and other states.

Researchers say the study found an urgent need for targeted dietary interventions and public health strategies to promote healthy eating from an early age to improve mental and cognitive development of children – including the benefits of a diet rich in antioxidative foods to reduce inflammation.

“It is equally important to address the broader socioeconomic determinants that shape dietary intake and access to healthy foods. Improving these factors is crucial to achieving better diet-related outcomes for Australian children and adolescents.”

The article, ‘What do Australian children and adolescents eat? A systematic review of dietary patterns and adverse health and cognitive outcomes’ (2026) by Zegeye Abebe, Tefera Chane Mekonnen, Amy C Reynolds, Kacie Dickinson, Sarah Appleton, Leila Mohammadi, Zumin Shi, Katherine M Livingstone, Mario Siervo, Blossom CM Stephan, Robert Adams and Yohannes Adama Melaku was published in Current Nutrition Reports. DOI 10. 10.1007/s13668-026-00805-w.

Chronic diseases – including mental health disorders, obesity and type 2 diabetes – are common in Australian children, with one study finding one in three children aged between 8-13 years reports a mental health problem.

A significant burden of chronic diseases among Australian children is largely driven by modifiable behaviours such as poor diet, excessive screen time and insufficient physical activity.
