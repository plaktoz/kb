# Google DeepMind Just Described the Collapse of the Corporate World.

## We are focusing on individual AI models while completely ignoring the swarm that is about to replace entire companies.

Every few months, another AI lab drops a new model, and the internet loses its mind over the same scoreboard.

It scored in the 99th percentile on the Bar Exam!

It solved International Math Olympiad problems!

It codes faster than a senior engineer at Meta!

Cue the panic. Lawyers refresh their LinkedIn feeds with a knot in their stomachs. Software engineers start half-joking about becoming electricians. Economists argue on podcasts about whether white-collar unemployment will hit 15% or 40% by the end of the decade. Everyone is staring at the same terrifying finish line: the day a single AI model becomes smarter than the smartest person in the room.

Here is the problem. Almost everyone is staring at the wrong threat.

A fascinating paper from Google DeepMind researchers on the future of the post-AGI economy just quietly sketched out something far more seismic than a round of corporate layoffs. Buried beneath the careful academic prose is a blueprint for the collapse of the corporate world as we know it—and an insight so unsettling that once you see it, you cannot unsee it.

The biggest economic disruption of our lifetimes won’t happen when individual AI models become smarter than humans.

It will happen when AI agents learn how to work together—forming coordinated digital swarms capable of outcompeting entire human institutions.

Read that again, because it upends almost everything we’ve been told about the future of work, business, and capitalism itself.

For the last few years, we’ve treated AI like a brilliant, socially awkward intern trapped inside a chat window. We grade it on standardized tests. We obsess over its solo IQ. We measure whether one model can beat one radiologist, one copywriter, or one Wall Street analyst.

Yet in the real economy, lone geniuses rarely win.

Nobody builds a commercial airliner alone. Nobody runs a global semiconductor supply chain out of their garage. Nobody scales a bank, an insurer, a logistics empire, or a hospital network through sheer individual brilliance. In the real world, individuals don’t compete against individuals.

Organizations compete against organizations.

Right now, an AI model that can run circles around any individual human on earth can still get completely demolished by a messy, caffeinated, well-organized human team. That fragile advantage is the only thing keeping the modern corporate org chart alive.

Wait until the swarm learns to coordinate.

## The Single-Player Illusion

To understand why today’s AI benchmarks are blinding us to what’s coming, think about basketball.

Imagine you built a robot that could sink a three-pointer from half-court 98% of the time. Put it in a shooting contest against Steph Curry, and the robot wins by a landslide. The headlines write themselves: AI HAS SOLVED BASKETBALL. THE NBA IS DEAD.

Now put five of those robots on a court against a real championship team.

The robots don’t set screens for each other. They don’t anticipate a teammate’s cut to the basket based on a split-second eyebrow raise. When the defense adjusts, they don’t call an audible, cover for a blown assignment, or sacrifice their own shot to break a full-court press. They are five solo gods playing five separate games at the same time.

They would get blown out by forty points.

That is essentially where artificial intelligence sits right now. Every benchmark we worship—coding puzzles, medical licensing exams, logic riddles, essay prompts—is a single-player shooting contest. It measures what happens when you lock a model inside a sterile room with a clean prompt and a tidy grading rubric.

Real corporate work is rarely a clean prompt.

Real work is trying to launch a product when marketing hasn’t finalized the messaging, engineering just discovered a legacy bug from 2019, legal is nervous about an EU regulation nobody fully understands, finance is freezing discretionary budgets, and your biggest client just threatened to churn unless you ship a custom integration by Friday.

Drop a genius solo AI into the middle of that chaos today, and it stalls out. Sure, it can draft a crisp email for the account executive and refactor a Python script for the developer. It makes the individual players slightly faster. But the game itself—the messy, continuous dance of negotiation, context-sharing, prioritization, accountability, and mutual adjustment—still belongs entirely to humans.

In fact, this explains one of the great quiet paradoxes of the current AI boom. Hundreds of billions of dollars have been poured into enterprise AI tools. Models can already write cleaner prose than 90% of corporate employees and generate code faster than almost anyone alive. Yet when economists look at macro productivity across Fortune 500 companies, the numbers haven’t gone vertical yet.

Why? Because making an individual worker 30% faster doesn’t change much when their output immediately hits a two-week approval bottleneck waiting for three vice presidents to “align.”

You can bolt a Formula 1 engine onto a car stuck in bumper-to-bumper highway traffic. It’s still going four miles an hour.

For years, that traffic jam has been humanity’s secret moat. As long as AI remained a superhuman solo performer, companies still needed humans to be the organizational glue. We comforted ourselves with a tidy career thesis: The safe jobs of the future are the ones about collaboration, management, and bringing teams together.

Google DeepMind’s researchers just pointed straight at the trapdoor beneath that assumption. What happens to the corporate world when machines don’t just replace the individual workers—they replace the glue?

## The Hidden Tax That Built the Corporate World

Before we walk through the four post-AGI futures DeepMind mapped out, you have to understand a dirty little secret about modern corporations: they are shockingly inefficient machines.

In 1937, a young British economist named Ronald Coase asked a question so basic that nobody had bothered to answer it before: Why do companies even exist?

If free markets are so magical, why don’t we just contract out every single task to freelancers on the open market every morning?

Why put a designer, an accountant, an engineer, and a salesperson on full-time payroll under one roof instead of hiring each of them gig-by-gig?

Coase’s answer won him the Nobel Prize: transaction costs.

Finding people, negotiating prices, drafting contracts, and checking whether someone ripped you off takes an enormous amount of time and energy. Bringing people inside a single corporation slashes all that external market friction. You just walk over to Sarah’s desk and ask her to tweak the slide deck. No invoice, no NDA, no bidding war required.

Great. Now follow Coase’s logic to its logical extreme: if putting people inside a company reduces friction, why isn’t the entire global economy just one gigantic mega-company that manufactures everything from toothbrushes to fighter jets?

Because of the opposite force: internal coordination costs.

Anyone who has ever watched a scrappy startup grow up has felt this law of physics in their bones.

At 10 employees, magic happens. Everyone sits around the same table. Everyone knows what everyone else is building. You don’t need a “Cross-Functional Roadmap Alignment Committee”—you just yell across the room over cold pizza and push the update live before midnight.

Now jump to 100 employees. Suddenly, you don’t recognize the new hire in finance. One team ships a feature that accidentally breaks what another team spent six weeks building. So you hire middle managers. You introduce weekly standups, quarterly OKRs, and a Slack workspace with 140 channels—half of which exist solely to clarify what was misunderstood in the other half.

Now jump to 100,000 employees.

God help you.

At 100,000 employees, a human corporation stops behaving like a team and starts behaving like a small, paranoid nation-state. Half the workforce spends its day translating information up and down a twelve-layer game of corporate telephone. You hold a 45-minute pre-meeting to agree on what you’re going to say in the main meeting. Departments quietly sabotage each other to protect their annual headcount and budgets. Crucial institutional knowledge lives inside a 47-tab Excel spreadsheet that only Brenda in regional operations understands—and Brenda is retiring next Tuesday.

Computer scientist Fred Brooks famously captured a slice of this curse in Brooks’s Law: adding manpower to a late software project makes it later. Anthropologist Robin Dunbar explained the biological ceiling underneath it: the human brain literally evolved to maintain stable social trust with only about 150 people. Push an organization past that threshold, and organic trust evaporates. You have to replace trust with bureaucracy.

Meetings. Policies. Internal politics. Sign-off chains. Status updates about status updates.

Here is the profound economic reality that almost nobody appreciates: corporate rot is the only thing keeping capitalism competitive.

Human bureaucracy acts like gravity. It places a hard natural speed limit on how big, fast, and dominant any single company can get. Walmart can’t effortlessly become Apple, and Apple can’t effortlessly become Pfizer, because the moment a human company grows too massive and tries to do too many things, its own internal coordination costs crush it under its own weight. It gets slow. It gets clumsy. It gets stupid. And that slowness leaves cracks wide enough for a hungry 12-person startup to slip underneath the giant and steal its lunch.

Every time you groan about a pointless Zoom meeting, remember: that meeting is the reason one mega-corporation doesn’t own the entire planet.

Now ask yourself the question at the heart of DeepMind’s paper.

What happens to the corporate world when someone builds an organization that doesn’t experience gravity?

## The Four Futures of the Post-AGI Economy

This is where Google DeepMind’s framework gets genuinely chilling—and brutally clear.

Instead of treating “AGI” like a single binary switch where machines either usher in sci-fi utopia or wipe us out overnight, the researchers split the post-AGI economy along two distinct axes: individual capability (how good an AI is at executing a task solo) and cooperative capability (how good AI agents are at coordinating with each other at scale).

Plot those two forces against each other, and you don’t get one future. You get four radically different worlds.

## Scenario 1: Minimal AGI — The Squeezed Middle

In the first scenario, AI matches roughly average human capability across most cognitive tasks, without becoming wildly superhuman at either solo mastery or large-scale multi-agent teamwork.

Think of this as the “infinite average intern” economy.

In a Minimal AGI world, employment stays surprisingly stable on the surface. Companies don’t suddenly fire 80% of their workforce, because average AI still requires constant oversight, context, and human coordination to produce reliably useful outcomes. You still need people to check the work, manage client relationships, handle messy edge cases, and keep the company pointed in the right direction.

So what breaks? Wages.

When average cognitive labor becomes abundant and practically free, the floor collapses under routine knowledge work. Entry-level analysts, junior copywriters, basic coders, paralegals, and administrative staff find themselves competing against software that costs twenty dollars a month and never asks for paid leave or health insurance.

People stay employed largely by shifting into adjacent roles—supervising software, doing physical or relationship-heavy work, cleaning up AI mistakes. Yet bargaining power tilts steadily toward capital and away from average labor. Productivity inches upward, inequality grinds higher, and the corporate world looks eerily familiar—just leaner, tighter, and far less forgiving if your skills are merely “good enough.”

Honestly? This is the scenario most politicians, Wall Street analysts, and corporate executives are quietly praying for. It’s uncomfortable, sure, but the traditional corporation survives intact.
