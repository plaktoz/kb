# I Don't Want to Read What You Didn't Write

Breck argues that AI-generated writing has become pervasive and largely unreadable. Design docs, PR summaries, tickets, and even personal messages are increasingly machine-produced — detailed but contextless, exhaustive but meaningless.

The core problem: when you prompt an AI, you already hold all the context. The output works for you. But the recipient has none of that scaffolding, forcing them to read exhaustively and consider every line. The writing fails because the author's perspective, judgment, and voice are absent.

Survey data he cites found 78% of readers stop reading when they suspect AI authorship, and 98% prefer the author's own imperfect voice. As Bjarne Stroustrup noted, the best writing makes readers feel they can hear the author's voice ringing in their head.

Where AI genuinely helped Breck: writing an academic paper, he used AI to verify factual accuracy against source code and logs, handle citation formatting, catch spelling, grammar, and subtle notation errors, and draft the abstract — the most terse, mechanical, abstracted part. AI never wrote a single paragraph of substance.

Deeper concern: Breck draws on Iain McGilchrist and Simon Sarris to argue that summarization erodes complex mental model-building. Narrative resists compression, and outsourcing writing to AI risks training yourself to ask only questions machines answer well — obscuring what you're not asking at all.

Conclusion: authentic, intentional writing will grow more valuable precisely because AI floods the world with hollow text.
