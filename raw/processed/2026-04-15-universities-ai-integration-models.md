# Universities Shift Towards New Models of AI Integration

AI is moving from experimental use to embedded practice in higher education, but coherent institutional response remains uneven. Despite widespread adoption among students and faculty, programme-level integration of AI is still largely fragmented — confined to scattered experimentation rather than coordinated strategy. There is little agreement on what "AI readiness" should mean in practice or how it should apply across disciplines.

Three distinct adoption models are emerging, reflecting different ways humans and AI collaborate: (1) AI as a human-guided tool supporting summarization, content creation, and analysis under clear human direction — the most common form today; (2) collaborative human–AI workflows, where AI takes a more active execution role while humans provide direction and judgment; and (3) a less widespread, more advanced model where AI operates with limited human supervision, with human involvement shifting toward strategic workflow design, monitoring, and intervention.

Institutions are beginning to translate these models into programme design. Northeastern University, a Digital Education Council (DEC) member, focused on embedding AI fluency across programmes and disciplines rather than standalone AI courses, combining classroom instruction with project-based work and industry placements. Javed (Jay) Aslam, Chief of Artificial Intelligence at Northeastern, said: "We need to prepare our students for a workplace with co-intelligence, where we'll be working with and alongside AI tools." Northeastern adapted the DEC AI Literacy Framework to its own context through a faculty-led, bottom-up effort, with representatives across colleges defining discipline-specific AI readiness, then mapping it onto existing programmes. Aslam noted: "AI readiness is going to be inherently discipline-specific, but we really needed an overarching framework that would be common across all colleges and programmes."

For now, most institutions' AI strategies remain at an early stage, but as AI becomes more embedded in professional work, pressure to move beyond experimentation toward systematic approaches is likely to grow.
