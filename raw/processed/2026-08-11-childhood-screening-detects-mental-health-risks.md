# Childhood screen detects mental health risks

Children who experience traumatic events (abuse, neglect, domestic violence) have a higher risk of mental health disorders — but children also face a subtler adversity: unpredictable home environments, such as lack of a bedtime routine, parental mood swings, or unstable relationships between parents. Prior research linked unpredictable parenting to poorer child mental health, but standard adversity screens don't assess unpredictability and are better at identifying risk across large groups than for individual children.

An NIH-funded team led by Dr. Tallie Z. Baram (UC Irvine) and Dr. Laura Glynn (Chapman University) added five unpredictability measures to a standard adversity screen and tested whether they improved detection of childhood mental health conditions. The study covered about 30,000 children across 19 pediatric primary care clinics in Orange County, California, comparing electronic health record mental health diagnoses against screening results. Published in Nature Mental Health, July 21, 2026.

The unpredictability measures performed as well as the traditional adversity screen overall, and were better specifically at identifying children at risk of depression and sleep disorders. Children scoring high on the unpredictability screen had a 12-fold higher risk of developing depression than those scoring zero, versus an 8-fold higher risk for high scorers on the traditional screen.

Combining both screens improved detection beyond either alone: children scoring high on both were at elevated risk for depression, anxiety, behavioral concerns, sleep disorders, and physical symptoms like headaches and abdominal pain.

"Traditional screening has helped our understanding of childhood adversity, but it fails to identify many children who are at risk for mental health problems," said Glynn. "Our findings suggest that unpredictability is a distinct and meaningful form of adversity that contributes to risk in ways that existing screening tools do not detect." Baram added: "For parents, this isn't about creating a perfect home. It's about recognizing that consistency matters. Small routines and predictable patterns in daily life can support healthy development."

Future studies are needed to clarify how unpredictability leads to mental health outcomes and how to reduce its harms.
