# Why Working In 90-Minute Rhythms Is The Secret To Productivity

"There's a state of living and working, where instead of fully resetting, we remain half working, half resting, doing neither well," authors Eric Partaker and Dr. Christian Poensgen write in *Ultraproductive*. When I read this passage, it both horrified me and resonated with me. It's a sadly relatable feeling, a state we've all fallen into as we respond to emails from the couch with a show playing in the background. A dystopian worst-case scenario for hard workers.

The authors' new book, *Ultraproductive: The 90-Minute Method to Optimize Focus, Sleep, and Stress,* explains how you can escape this state by working with your body's 90-minute ultradian rhythm across three areas: focus, rest, and stress. Refreshingly, their approach is expressly anti "hustle culture" and anti "9-9-6." Instead, the authors focus on doing more with less and working with your natural flow of energy, not against it.

I sat down with Dr. Christian Poensgen for an interview, and he walked me through the three areas, sharing one practical strategy per area.

## The Three Areas: Focus, Rest, and Stress

"We're humans, not computers. We're not built to run multiple programs at the same time, work for long periods without rest, or spend all our time being fully on," Poensgen and Partaker write. "Humans are organisms that are designed to pulse. The key to sustainable peak performance, therefore, is to work and live rhythmically."

This attention to our natural daily rhythms is the beating heart of their book.

The authors point out that people tend to struggle across three main areas of productivity:

1. **Focus:** The average person shifts their attention every 47 seconds.
2. **Rest:** 85% of us wake up tired or feel tired throughout the day.
3. **Stress:** 76% of Americans are in a state of chronic stress and have experienced symptoms of burnout this year.

To improve in each area, the authors apply the 90-minute rhythm to each:

1. **Focus:** Deep focus for 90 minutes per day doubles the average person's daily productivity.
2. **Rest:** Take breaks from work every 90 minutes to restore energy and prepare you to refocus later, and protect your deep and REM sleep at night.
3. **Stress:** Since we aren't built to stress all the time, use stress in short bursts and interrupt it regularly so it doesn't turn chronic.

## #1: Deep Focus Has Three Phases, And You Need Each One

Deep focus requires three phases: *Fight, Flow, and Fadeout*. Each component drives your ability to consistently reach a state of deep focus.

**Fight Phase:** Poensgen explained that we're wired to survive in a world of scarcity and danger. This means, for example, that if you're trying to stoke a fire, you actually need to be distractible. Otherwise, your focus might keep you from detecting a prowling tiger. So we evolved to be distractible. "The most difficult part of focus," Poensgen said, "is the first five to 10 minutes. Because just as we cannot fall asleep right after lying down, it always takes five to 10 minutes to get into deep focus right after sitting down." You need to actively fight against your tendency to shift your focus.

**Flow Phase:** After the struggle of phase one, you enter phase two, which is the flow phase. "Here, effortful turns into effortless," Poensgen said. "Even if it's something as mundane as doing your taxes." In the flow phase, you're tough to interrupt. Even primal needs like hunger and using the restroom slip from your attention. Your coworker tapping you on the shoulder might startle you.

**Fadeout Phase:** This last phase is the most likely to get neglected. As you gain momentum, you'll be tempted to sit through your task until it's done. But as your energy starts to wane, it's important to cut yourself off from your work and recuperate so you'll be able to refocus later.

## #2: Work Ethic + Rest Ethic = Maximum Performance

Instead of being "always on," Poensgen and Partaker recommend that you think of yourself as a race car driver. "If a race car driver only used the accelerator, they would either quickly end up in a crash or have to slow down to a crawl," Poensgen explained. "And we believe that in life and in your work, you also need to use the brake for the same reason."

You can think of the accelerator as the stress system and the brake as the relaxation system. Only when you strike the right balance between the two can you hum through the racetrack loop (or achieve your highest possible level of performance).

As Poensgen put it, "You don't just need a great work ethic, you also need a great rest ethic."

## #3: Chronic Stress Is Bad, But Acute Stress Is Good

When we talk about stress, we typically talk about how to avoid, diminish, or eradicate it, but Poensgen and Partaker encourage us to view stress in a more sophisticated way.

"If you think about the things in life that you're most proud of," Poensgen said, "Things like building a career, building a company, getting a promotion, having a good relationship, having good friendships, getting married, or having kids. If you think about these things, all of them come with stress. Stress is a part of most things in life that are meaningful and worth having. And, if you turn this around, most things that don't come with any stress are usually not all that meaningful."

To work with your stress instead of against it, he explained that you actually want to wield shorter-term stress deliberately but then interrupt your stress often. The best stress interruptors harken back to the previous two pillars. You want to get a good night's sleep (90 minutes of deep and REM sleep), and you want to optimize your rest between zones of deep focus (every 90 minutes). In other words, you should allow yourself to feel acute stress during deep focus (it actually helps you focus to be a little stressed about the outcome), but then you want to deliberately toggle that stress off by pulling yourself away from the work—take walks, practice long-exhale breathing, and even plan out quarterly vacations.

Think of stress as a "force," one that you can channel for energy and focus, and one that can overwhelm you and burn you out.

## Each Area Interacts with the Others

These three areas interact. Use your stress in healthy bursts, and you'll focus better. Focus better, and you'll rest better knowing you've accomplished your most important work. Rest and sleep better, and you'll interrupt your stress to ensure it doesn't persist as chronic. Improve in one area, and you'll improve in the others. Slip in one area, and you'll slip in the others.
