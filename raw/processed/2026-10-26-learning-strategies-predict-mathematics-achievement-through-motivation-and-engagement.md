# Learning strategies predict mathematics achievement through motivation and engagement

## Abstract

This study examined the direct and indirect links between learning strategies and mathematics achievement via the mediating roles of motivation and engagement. The study was conducted with 444 middle school and high school students. Hierarchical regression results indicated potential mediating roles of motivation and engagement in the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement. Subsequent mediation analysis showed that learning strategies had a significant positive total effect on mathematics achievement, whereas their direct effect became non-significant when motivation and engagement were included in the model. Both motivation and engagement positively predicted mathematics achievement. The specific indirect effects of learning strategies on mathematics achievement through motivation and through engagement were significant, as was the serial indirect effect through motivation and engagement. Moreover, the indirect effect through motivation was significantly greater than both the indirect effect through engagement and the serial indirect effect. Thus, all three hypothesized indirect effects were supported. The study contributes to the literature by providing an integrated model that helps explain how learning strategies are associated with mathematics achievement. Teachers, teacher educators, and policymakers can use these findings to develop educational practices that support students' learning strategies, motivation, and engagement to promote mathematics achievement.

## 1 Introduction

Mathematics achievement is a central educational outcome and is monitored internationally through large-scale assessments such as PISA and TIMSS. To promote students' mathematics achievement, schooling should support not only the acquisition of mathematical knowledge and skills but also students' capacity to manage and regulate their own learning. Evidence from mathematics education indicates that self-regulated learning is positively associated with mathematics achievement [5]. Similar relationships have been reported among pre-service mathematics teachers, for whom online self-regulation was positively associated with achievement and mediated the relationships of self-management and self-control with academic achievement [13]. More broadly, meta-analytic evidence also indicates a positive relationship between self-regulation and student achievement across educational contexts [11]. Hattie and Donoghue [19], emphasized that the focus of schooling should extend beyond what students learn to how effectively and efficiently they learn. From this perspective, learning strategies are particularly important because they provide students with tools for planning, monitoring, regulating, and evaluating their learning. Thus, an important goal of schooling is to equip students with effective learning strategies and, more broadly, with the capacity to learn how to learn [19].

Learning strategies can be understood as the thoughts and behaviors learners employ during learning to influence how they select, acquire, organize, and integrate new knowledge, as well as to manage motivational and affective processes that may shape learning [47]. More broadly, they encompass the cognitive, metacognitive, and self-regulatory processes through which learners actively manage their learning. Such strategies help learners structure their thinking by planning and setting goals, monitoring progress, making adjustments when necessary, and evaluating both the learning process and its outcomes [19]. Their effectiveness also depends on how appropriately they are matched to the requirements of a particular task or stage of learning [19]. The strategic regulation of learning is also associated with academic achievement. Meta-analytic evidence indicates a positive relationship between self-regulation and student achievement across educational contexts [11], while research with pre-service mathematics teachers similarly shows positive associations among self-regulation, self-management, self-control, and academic achievement [13]. Differences in strategy use are also evident across achievement levels. Among middle school students, those with higher mathematics achievement reported a greater variety of learning strategies and a broader range of strategy categories than their lower-achieving peers, although the groups did not differ in the overall number or reported frequency of strategies used [35]. Similarly, undergraduate students with higher achievement demonstrated stronger use of several learning and study strategies, including information processing, concentration, motivation, self-testing, and test strategies [3]. These findings suggest that successful learning depends not simply on using more strategies, but on developing a sufficiently varied strategic repertoire and selecting and regulating strategies in ways that are appropriate to learning demands.

Learning strategies have been conceptualized in different ways by researchers [7, 16, 42]. According to the classification proposed by Boekaerts [8], learning strategies can be categorized as (i) cognitive strategies, (ii) metacognitive strategies, and (iii) motivational strategies. Cognitive strategies refer to strategies used to learn, remember, and understand content and include rehearsal, organization, and elaboration strategies. Metacognitive strategies refer to strategies used to regulate one's own thinking and learning processes, thereby fostering independent learning, and include planning, monitoring, and regulation strategies. Motivational strategies refer to strategies used to regulate cognitions, emotions, and effort related to learning activities and include self-efficacy-related processes, coping processes, and effort regulation or avoidance.

The literature indicates a close relationship between motivational and self-regulatory processes in learning, suggesting that how students approach and regulate their learning is intertwined with their motivational beliefs. In mathematics, motivational beliefs such as self-efficacy and task interest are associated with self-regulated learning behaviors, while both self-efficacy and self-regulation contribute to explaining mathematics performance [10]. Perceptions of teaching practices and the use of study resources have also been found to be significantly associated with students' motivation in mathematics [20]. Consistent with these findings, self-efficacy and self-regulated learning have been positively associated with mathematics achievement among secondary students [5], while research with pre-service mathematics teachers has similarly linked self-regulation to academic achievement [13]. Learning and studying approaches have also been examined alongside motivational characteristics such as self-reinforcement and persistence among pre-service mathematics teachers, further illustrating the close connection between how learners approach learning and their motivational characteristics [44]. Motivation is also closely associated with mathematics achievement. In online mathematics courses, motivation explained a meaningful proportion of the variance in achievement, although achievement emotions accounted for additional variance when considered in the same model [25]. More recent studies likewise report significant positive relationships between mathematics motivation and mathematics achievement [1, 4]. Importantly, longitudinal evidence suggests that motivation and cognitive learning strategies make distinct contributions to the development of mathematics achievement over time: across Grades 5 to 10, both motivation and learning strategies predicted long-term growth in mathematics achievement [31]. Beyond motivation and strategy use considered separately, the particular ways in which students approach learning also appear consequential. García et al. [17], for example, found that a surface approach to learning negatively predicted mathematics achievement. Related evidence also suggests that individual approaches to learning are associated with important mathematical outcomes. Among fifth-grade students, learning-style scores explained variance in mathematical reasoning and spatial ability, with visual learning emerging as a significant individual predictor of both outcomes [12]. These findings suggest that mathematics achievement is associated not only with students' motivational resources but also with how they regulate, strategically approach, and sustain their learning.

Rather than being passive recipients of information, students are expected to participate actively in mathematics learning by explaining their reasoning, engaging with others' mathematical ideas, and regulating their own learning processes [21, 46]. Within this perspective, student engagement represents an important component of successful mathematics learning. Engagement is a multidimensional construct encompassing cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions, each of which has been associated with mathematics achievement, although the strength and direction of these relationships may differ across dimensions and contexts [27, 36]. Engagement may also help explain how learning strategies are translated into academic performance. Büchele [9], found that learning engagement fully mediated the relationships between memorization and elaboration strategies and mathematics performance, suggesting that the effectiveness of strategy use is closely connected to the regularity and persistence with which students engage in learning. At the secondary-school level, affective engagement was the strongest predictor of mathematics achievement, followed by behavioral engagement, whereas cognitive engagement showed a more complex relationship with achievement [27]. Classroom participation also appears important. Students' explanations of their mathematical thinking and their engagement with peers' mathematical ideas have been positively associated with mathematics achievement [21, 46].

The factors that sustain engagement are likewise closely connected to strategic and self-regulatory learning. Longitudinal evidence from adolescents indicates that metacognitive skills, interest, and self-control each uniquely predict engagement in mathematics learning over time and may interact in shaping students' continued involvement in mathematics [45]. Similarly, mastery-approach goals have been shown to predict subsequent behavioral engagement, which in turn predicts mathematics achievement and mediates the relationship between mastery-approach goals and achievement [40]. Mathematics-specific evidence also links self-regulated learning with achievement among Grade VIII students [5], while research with Grade 11 students shows that instructional practices designed to address learners' needs can be accompanied by improvements in behavioral, cognitive, emotional, and social engagement as well as academic performance [22]. These relationships are particularly relevant during the middle and high school years, when students encounter increasingly demanding mathematical content and are expected to assume greater responsibility for their own learning. At these educational stages, how students select and regulate learning strategies may therefore become closely intertwined with their motivation, persistence, and engagement in mathematics.

Given the advent of newer ways to access information (e.g., the internet) and the vast amount of information now available to students, it is important to understand how learning strategies are associated with students' achievement. Focusing on middle and high school students is especially relevant because these years represent an educational period in which mathematical content becomes progressively more demanding, while students are also expected to become more autonomous in managing their learning. At the same time, maintaining motivation and engagement in mathematics may be particularly consequential during this period, as students' success increasingly depends not only on what strategies they know but also on whether they are motivated and sufficiently engaged to use them effectively. Examining these variables together across middle and high school levels can therefore provide a more comprehensive understanding of the mechanisms through which learning strategies are associated with mathematics achievement during adolescence. From a theoretical perspective, including motivation and engagement as mediating variables extends previous research that has primarily examined the direct associations of learning strategies, motivation, or engagement with mathematics achievement. Testing these variables within an integrated mediation model can help clarify whether learning strategies are associated with achievement partly through students' motivational and engagement-related processes. In this way, the study contributes to a more process-oriented explanation of the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement rather than treating these constructs as independent predictors. From a practical perspective, identifying such indirect relationships may help educators determine whether efforts to improve mathematics achievement should focus not only on teaching effective learning strategies but also on creating conditions that support students' motivation and active engagement in learning. This may be particularly useful for designing instructional practices and interventions for students who possess learning strategies but do not use them consistently or effectively because of low motivation or engagement.

The synthesis of research indicates that learning strategies are critical factors associated with mathematics achievement. Moreover, motivation and engagement may help explain how learning strategies are related to mathematics achievement. Hence, this research aims to examine the direct and indirect relationships between learning strategies and mathematics achievement by testing the mediating roles of motivation and engagement among middle and high school students. According to the hypothesized model, which was constructed as a serial mediating effect, three mediating effects were tested: (H1) learning strategies predict student achievement through motivation, (H2) learning strategies predict student achievement through engagement, and (H3) learning strategies predict student achievement first through motivation and then through engagement.

## 2 Method

## 2.1 Research design

This descriptive study is based on the relational survey model as it examines the mediating roles of motivation and engagement on the relationship between the learning strategies and achievement. The predicting (X) and predicted (Y) variables of the study are learning strategies and achievement, respectively, while the mediating variables (M) are motivation and engagement.

## 2.2 Participants

A convenience sampling method was used to determine the sample of the study. The participants of this study included 458 secondary school and high school students. The data obtained from 444 students have been analyzed after eliminating 14 outliers according to z-scores [43]. The participants included 207 (96 of who are female and 111 of whom are male) secondary school and 237 (124 of whom are female and 113 of whom are male) high school students.

## 2.3 Data collection

The data collected through the use of Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire, the Engagement and Disaffection Scale, and students' self-reported mathematics achievement.

### 2.3.1 Motivated Strategies for Learning Questionnaire (MSLQ)

The MSLQ was originally developed by Pintrich et al. [37] and adapted into Turkish by Karadeniz et al. [23]. The questionnaire is used to measure students' motivational orientations and use of different learning strategies.

It contains 81 items which are scored on a five-point Likert scale from 1 to 7. The motivation section consists of 31 items, and the learning strategy section includes 50 questions.

The scale is composed of a 6-factor motivation subscale and a 9-factor learning strategies subscale. Both subscales are modular meaning that they can be used single or together depending on the researcher's purpose. The motivational subscale consists of expectancy, value, and affect. The learning strategies subscale consists of cognitive, metacognitive, and resource management. Karadeniz et al. [23] reported that The corrected item total correlations ranged from 0.15 to 0.58 to for Motivation Scale, and from 0.19 to 0.68 for Learning Strategies Scale. In the present study, the overall reliability coefficients were 0.86 for the Motivation Scale and 0.94 for the Learning Strategies Scale. The reliability coefficients for the respective subscales ranged from 0.53 to 0.86 for the Motivation Scale and from 0.47 to 0.82 for the Learning Strategies Scale.

### 2.3.2 Engagement and Disaffection Scale (EDS)

The EDS was developed by Güvenç [18] to measure the students' engagement and disaffection levels for the tasks. The scale includes 16 items on a five-point Likert scale and four factors as behavioral engagement, emotional engagement, behavioral disaffection and emotional disaffection. Cronbach's alpha internal consistency coefficients of the subscales range from 0.66 to 0.83. In the present study, the overall reliability coefficient of the scale was 0.82, while the reliability coefficients for the subscales ranged from 0.69 to 0.73.

### 2.3.3 Achievement

The participants were asked to report the mathematics grades for that year as a determinant of this variable, as the grades are one of the reliable reference indicators of a student's academic performance [32]. The grades reported ranged from 28 to 100. The grades of the participants were standardized before computation by transforming them into z-scores for the analyses.

## 2.4 Data analysis

Preliminary analyses have been run to check the suitability of the data (e.g. wrong encodings, missing values, outliers, normality, and multicollinearity) for multivariate analyses. The outliers have been eliminated and the normality assumption has been confirmed. A four-step hierarchical regression analysis was conducted to examine the direct effects of motivational strategies, learning strategies and engagement on mathematics achievement. In the first step, grade level and gender were entered as control variables. Learning strategies was added in the second step. Motivational strategies and engagement were added to the model in the third and fourth steps, respectively. In addition, the bootstrapping method was employed with 10,000 bootstrapping samples to test the significance of the serial mediation effects [38, 39]. All data were analyzed using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 22.0.

OpenAI's ChatGPT was used exclusively for linguistic refinement. The authors reviewed and verified all outputs produced by the AI tool.

## 3 Results

## 3.1 Preliminary findings

The data first were checked for wrong encoding and missing values were assigned using the series mean method. Fourteen outliers have been identified according the z-scores. These outliers whose z-scores were below |3.29| have been removed from the data set [43]. Table 1 presents the descriptive statistics and correlation coefficients among the variables.

Table 1 shows that the data do not violate the multicollinearity assumption as all the correlation coefficients among the variables are lower than 0.80. Also normality assumption has been met according to the skewness and kurtosis values.

Hierarchical regression analysis results conducted to examine the explained variances of the independent variables on mathematics achievement are presented in Table 2.

In the first step, gender and grade level (controlling variables) explained 3% of the variance in mathematics achievement. After controlling the effects of gender and grade level in the first step, learning strategies positively predicted achievement explaining additional 9% of the variance. In the third step, the contribution of the motivational strategies to the explained variance was 9%. Inclusion of engagement to the model resulted an additional 2% of the variance in the final step. When motivational strategies variable was added to the model, the effects of learning strategies became non-significant. In addition, the effects of learning strategies and motivational strategies on math achievement diminished after the inclusion of engagement. These results indicated potential mediating effects of motivational strategies and engagement. However, further analyses were needed to examine the significance of mediating effects. Hence mediation analysis was conducted to test the effects of mediating variables.

## 3.2 Testing the serial mediating effect of motivation and engagement

A mediation analysis using bootstrapping with a 10,000 random resampling was conducted to test the serial mediating effects of motivational strategies and engagement using SPSS macro-PROCESS (Model 6) program. Indirect effects with 95% bias-corrected confidence intervals not including zero indicated significant mediating effects.

## 4 Discussion

This study examined the effects of learning strategies, motivation, and engagement on students' mathematics achievement. The findings of the research show that motivation and engagement directly predict student achievement positively, while learning strategies do not predict achievement in the same model. All hypotheses regarding the mediating effect were confirmed. In other words, one of the confirmed hypotheses is the mediating effect of motivation (H1: learning strategies ◊ motivation ◊ mathematics achievement), the second is the mediating effect of engagement (H2: learning strategies ◊ engagement ◊ mathematics achievement), and the other is the serial mediating effect of motivation and engagement (H3: learning strategies ◊ motivation ◊ engagement ◊ mathematics achievement). On the other hand, learning strategies were found to have no direct effect on mathematics achievement. When mediating variables were included in the model, the indirect effect of learning strategies on mathematics achievement was significant.

The hierarchical regression results indicate that learning strategies make a significant unique contribution to mathematics achievement. This finding is consistent with mathematics-specific research linking students' strategic and self-regulatory learning to academic performance. Roick and Ringeisen [41], for example, found that cognitive and metacognitive strategy use was associated with performance in a two-semester university mathematics course, although the strength and direction of these associations varied depending on when the strategies were measured. Similarly, self-regulated learning has been found to be directly associated with mathematics achievement among Grade VIII students [5], while self-regulation has also been positively linked to achievement among pre-service mathematics teachers [13].

Complementary intervention research further indicates that strategy-oriented instructional approaches can support mathematics learning. Peer-Assisted Learning Strategies have been associated with benefits across different achievement groups in elementary mathematics [15], while cooperative learning has produced better mathematics performance than competitive learning in secondary-school settings [26]. Cooperative-mastery learning has likewise been shown to outperform traditional instruction [28], and cognitive strategy instruction has produced greater growth in mathematical problem-solving performance among middle-school students, with particularly strong benefits for lower-achieving students [29]. Meta-analytic evidence also indicates that the use of educational materials can enhance mathematics achievement [14]. More recent evidence also indicates that cooperative learning can improve mathematics achievement in secondary education [34]. Reinforcement-based instructional strategies, including praise, token reinforcement, and exposure to successful mathematicians, have similarly been associated with improved mathematics achievement [33].

Importantly, the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement may not operate only through a direct pathway. Engagement has been shown to mediate the relationship between students' use of memorization and elaboration strategies and mathematics performance [9], while mathematics motivation has been found to partially mediate the relationship between collaborative learning and mathematics achievement [1]. Accordingly, the present study extends this line of research by examining whether motivation and engagement help explain the relationship between students' learning strategies and mathematics achievement.

When motivation and engagement were introduced into the mediation model, the previously significant association between learning strategies and mathematics achievement was no longer statistically significant, whereas the indirect effects through motivation and engagement were significant. This pattern is consistent with full mediation, suggesting that the association between learning strategies and mathematics achievement is accounted for by motivational and engagement-related pathways. In this sense, motivation and engagement may function not merely as independent correlates of mathematics achievement but as mechanisms through which students' strategic approaches to learning are associated with their achievement. This interpretation is consistent with previous evidence showing that learning engagement can fully mediate the relationship between learning strategy use and mathematics performance [9]. More broadly, both motivational constructs and student engagement have been associated with mathematics achievement across different educational settings and populations [24, 48]. Mathematics-specific mediation studies also indicate that motivation can mediate the relationship between learning-related experiences and mathematics achievement, including collaborative learning and peer teaching [1, 4]. However, mediation findings are not uniform across models. For example, mathematics motivation did not mediate the relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and achievement in Al Umairi [2], suggesting that the explanatory role of motivation may depend on the antecedent variable and learning context. Moreover, longitudinal evidence indicates that the direction of the relationship between motivation and learning-strategy use is not necessarily from strategies to motivation; motivation has also been found to predict subsequent strategy use in mathematics learning [6]. Accordingly, the present findings extend the literature by examining motivation and engagement simultaneously as indirect pathways linking learning strategies with mathematics achievement, while the observed mediation should be interpreted as statistical rather than definitive evidence of temporal or causal ordering.

The support of the first hypothesis, which states that motivation plays a mediating role in the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement, can be interpreted as that learning strategies increase student motivation and thus increase mathematics achievement. Research suggests that affective-motivational variables and approaches to learning predict students' mathematics achievement [17]. Motivation (including perceived control, intrinsic, and extrinsic motivation) and cognitive learning strategies predict long-term growth in mathematics achievement. While intelligence is related to initial achievement levels, motivation and learning strategies are key predictors of growth over time [31]. The support of the second hypothesis, which states that engagement plays a mediating role in the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement, can be interpreted as that learning strategies increase students' engagement in the lesson and this engagement results in an increase in mathematics achievement. Being a multifaceted construct encompassing cognitive, affective, and behavioral dimensions, engagement is found to mediate the effectiveness of various learning strategies on students' mathematics performance [9, 36]. Additionally, learning strategies are significant predictors of engagement in mathematics [45], and mathematics achievement is positively correlated with engagement [40]. The support of the third hypothesis, which states that motivation and engagement play a mediating role in the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement, can be interpreted as that learning strategies increase student motivation, motivation positively effects student engagement and this results in an increase in students' mathematics achievement. As discussed earlier, this result is also in line with the literature, as motivational constructs and engagement greatly impact students' mathematics achievements [24, 30, 48].

A notable finding of the present study is that the previously significant relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement became nonsignificant after motivation and engagement were introduced into the model, while the indirect pathways remained significant. This pattern is consistent with full mediation and suggests that the association between learning strategies and mathematics achievement may operate primarily through students' motivational and engagement-related processes. Previous research has established that strategic and self-regulatory learning is associated with academic achievement. Meta-analytic evidence indicates a positive relationship between self-regulation and student achievement across educational contexts [11], while mathematics-specific studies have similarly linked self-regulated learning and cognitive and metacognitive strategy use with mathematics performance [5, 41]. Related research with pre-service mathematics teachers further shows that self-regulation can function as an indirect pathway linking self-management and self-control with academic achievement [13].

The present findings also converge with emerging evidence that the relationship between learning-related processes and mathematics performance may be explained through intermediate motivational and engagement mechanisms. Büchele [9], for example, found that learning engagement fully mediated the relationship between memorization and elaboration strategies and mathematics performance. Mathematics-specific studies have also shown that motivation can mediate the relationships of collaborative learning and peer teaching with mathematics achievement [1, 4]. At the same time, such mediation effects do not appear to be universal; Al Umairi [2], for instance, found that mathematics motivation did not mediate the relationship between mathematics self-efficacy and achievement. Against this background, the contribution of the present study lies not simply in identifying associations among learning strategies, motivation, engagement, and mathematics achievement, but in examining motivation and engagement simultaneously as indirect pathways through which learning strategies are associated with mathematics achievement. The disappearance of the direct path after these mediators were incorporated therefore provides a more differentiated account of how students' strategic approaches to learning may translate into mathematics achievement.

### 4.1 Implications

Motivation and engagement appear to play important roles in the relationship between learning strategies and mathematics achievement. The findings suggest that simply teaching students how to use learning strategies may not be sufficient; attention should also be given to whether students are motivated to use these strategies and actively engaged in the learning process. Therefore, mathematics instruction may benefit from integrating strategy instruction with practices that support students' motivation and engagement. For example, teachers can encourage students to set learning goals, monitor their own progress, reflect on the effectiveness of the strategies they use, participate actively in mathematical problem solving, and discuss different solution approaches with their peers. Providing meaningful tasks, constructive feedback, and opportunities for active participation may also help students use learning strategies more consistently and purposefully.

These implications may be particularly relevant for middle and high school students, for whom mathematics achievement and examination performance often become increasingly important. During periods of examination preparation, students may know appropriate learning strategies but may not use them effectively when their motivation or engagement is low. Accordingly, mathematics teachers may consider incorporating motivational and engagement-supportive practices into both regular classroom instruction and examination preparation. Rather than relying predominantly on repetitive practice or strategy instruction in isolation, teachers can help students establish attainable goals, monitor their progress, persist with challenging mathematics tasks, and evaluate which learning strategies are most useful for particular types of problems. Practice activities that resemble examination demands can also be combined with opportunities for reflection and feedback so that students learn not only how to solve mathematical problems but also how to regulate their learning while preparing for assessments.

The findings may also be useful for schools and teachers in identifying students who possess knowledge of learning strategies but nevertheless show low motivation or limited classroom engagement. For such students, providing additional strategy instruction alone may have limited value unless motivational and engagement-related difficulties are also addressed. Thus, interventions aimed at improving mathematics achievement, including examination-preparation programs, may be more comprehensive when they simultaneously address students' learning strategies, motivation, and engagement. However, given the correlational nature of the present study, these practical implications should be interpreted as directions for instructional practice and future intervention research rather than as evidence of causal effects.
