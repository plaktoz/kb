# Corporate Giants Are Staying on Top Longer. Should They Feel Safe?

## Key Takeaways

* The churn in market value leadership declined in 10 out of 15 industries from 2005 through 2025, compared with the prior 15 years.
* Industries, however, varied significantly on the share of new value created that was captured by valuation leaders.
* Value leaders and upstarts alike should step back and assess their best opportunities to extend or usurp leadership.

That leadership positions are fragile is a tenet of the popular business press. Disruption lurks at every corner. Only the paranoid survive. But a careful look at the 2,000 most valuable companies across 15 industries over the past 35 years offers a different picture—at least at the very top: leaders are staying leaders for longer.

What is driving this counterintuitive finding? In some industries, advantages such as scale, network effects, data, or brand and distribution may be becoming more self-reinforcing. In others, incumbents may be getting better at adapting and moving into new value pools. And still in others, leaders may simply be between waves of disruption—with the digital wave maturing and the impact of AI still gathering force.

In this article, we describe the phenomenon of increasing leadership persistence, offer some perspectives on how to distinguish durable stability from more precarious forms of lock-in, and consider what that means for leaders and challengers as they chart their value creation strategies.

## Leadership Positions Have Increasingly Locked In

When we studied the shifting roster of valuation leaders from 1990 through 2025, we saw a notable shift toward leadership lock-in. We looked at a metric we call giant churn—the percentage of the combined market capitalization of a sector's ten most valuable companies in a given year represented by firms that were absent from the top ten five years earlier. It offers a value-weighted view of how much leadership has shifted at the summit.

Across industries, median giant churn fell from 24% from 1990 through 2004 to 13% from 2005 through 2025. Looking deeper, 10 of the 15 industries saw a decline in giant churn, while consumer staples and communications infrastructure saw relatively modest increases. (See Exhibit 1.)

Digital platforms showed the largest increase in leadership longevity, with giant churn falling from nearly 100% to roughly 5% in the later period. But the shift is atypical as it reflects the formation of the category as much as increasing competitive stability. As scaled search and social platforms emerged, leadership consolidated around a small number of very large players and has since become highly persistent.

In industries where leadership tenure has lengthened, there is no single driver. In tech sectors, for example, network effects and ecosystem scale tended to reinforce the position of successful leaders over time. By contrast in retail, where giant churn fell from roughly 10% to 4%, successive waves of disruption displaced weaker formats and shifted advantage toward scaled operators whose purchasing, logistics, data, and ecosystem advantages can reinforce one another. In other words, disruption can itself produce greater stability, as successful disruptors become incumbents and established leaders adapt. (For more background on the analysis, see "About Our Research.")

### About Our Research

Our analysis is based on the top 2,000 companies by market capitalization that were listed on US exchanges, captured at five-year intervals from 1990 through 2025. Data was sourced from S&P Capital IQ. Private companies, as well as public companies that trade exclusively on non-US exchanges, were excluded. For period comparisons, giant churn is reported as the median across the five-year windows within each period.

Industry groupings are based on the Global Industry Classification Standard (GICS) developed by S&P Global with the following adjustments to better reflect competitive boundaries that standard GICS groupings obscure.

The communications services sector was split into three subsectors:

* Communications infrastructure, which covers all of the telecommunication services subcategory
* Media and entertainment, which covers all media and entertainment except interactive media and services
* Digital platforms, which covers the interactive media and services subcategory

A broader retail sector was created by shifting out and combining the following retail-related subcategories from consumer discretionary and consumer staples:

* Broadline retail
* Specialty retail (excluding automotive retail)
* Consumer staples distribution and retail (excluding distribution)

A broader auto and auto retail sector was created by shifting auto retail out of consumer discretionary.

Importantly, it's not that leadership has become permanent—just more persistent. Over the full 35 years, 62% of companies that reached the top ten fell out at some point during the period. Among those exits, 78% reflected displacement by rivals, while just 16% resulted from M&A. But the companies that make it to the top, on average, stay there longer.

Stability is not necessarily strength though. Leaders need to ask themselves whether a longer time at the top signals a durable advantage—or simply a lull before the next wave of change.

## Not All Lock-In Is Equal

Tenure tells us which companies have endured. But is that endurance still being rewarded? For this analysis, we zoomed in on the top five players by value in each sector as of the end of 2025.

To see in which industries long-term winners were still winning—or not—we looked at the share of new value created in the industry from 2015 through 2025 that was captured by its top five players. We then viewed that through a tenure-based lens: the proportion of 2025's five most valuable companies that had been in the top five longer than the industry's median leadership tenure from 1990 through 2025. (See Exhibit 2.)

For industries in the upper left quadrant, companies' leadership may be more exposed. The top players, while still highly valued, are capturing a smaller share of the new value being created, with more of that value accruing to companies outside the top five. In the lower left, leadership is less settled: new value is more broadly distributed and most of the top five are more recent entries.

In the lower right quadrant, newer leaders are capturing a disproportionate share of new value, which may signal a more recent reset in leadership. By contrast, the upper right combines long tenure with continued value capture—arguably the strongest form of lock-in. These leaders may be genuinely advantaged, or they may be extraordinarily well adapted to the current rules of competition. The question is whether the sources of advantage that got them here will still matter when those rules change.

Importantly, competitive advantage is an attribute of a company, not an industry. The auto industry, for example, would seem well positioned in the aggregate, but at the company level, a different picture emerges. Nearly all of the new value created was captured by just one company, Tesla. At year-end 2025, its $1.5 trillion valuation was nineteen times that of the second most valuable company, O'Reilly Automotive. Moreover, among long-tenured top ten companies, only the parts retailers O'Reilly and AutoZone and tier one supplier BorgWarner delivered above-average value creation.

By contrast, leaders in the consumer staples industry show much greater stability. Over the period of our research, just 42% of the companies that made the top ten were ultimately displaced—the lowest across all industries. Brand and distribution strength likely play a big role. And three of the four most valuable companies in 2025—Procter & Gamble, Coca-Cola, and PepsiCo—have been in the top ten since 1990.

While players should keep these high-level perspectives on industry dynamics in mind, it's also crucial for them to focus on specific opportunities and challenges—and what they imply for the company's strategy to defend or seize a leadership position.

## Navigating Risk and Assessing Opportunity

Many companies no longer compete within clean industry definitions. Technology, regulation, and shifting customer needs open new adjacencies and redraw those boundaries. So, the starting point is to understand the relevant competitive arena and the broader forces reshaping demand.

To assess whether a company's leadership is durable, business leaders should ask themselves a few questions that may be more revealing than rank or tenure alone.

* **Are we capturing the next value pool or simply holding our position in the current one?** Tenure is not the same thing as relevance. A company can remain among the largest in its industry even as growth, investment, customer demand, and value creation begin shifting elsewhere. The question is whether a company is still positioned where the next pool of value is forming.

  Utilities are a case in point. The sector's top ten captured just over half of the new value created over the past decade. That is meaningful, but it also means that nearly half was created elsewhere, as AI-driven power demand and the energy transition opened new pools of value that were captured by others.
* **Is our advantage still compounding—or has it not yet been tested?** Some advantages strengthen over time. More users make the product more attractive. More data sharpens the offering. Scale improves the economics. Integration makes switching harder. Other advantages persist for less reassuring reasons: market entry remains difficult, customers switch slowly, or no challenger has yet to find a way around the incumbent. While these two types of advantage can feel similar, it's important not to mistake endurance for strength.

  Consider IT. Churn among the largest players fell from 14% to roughly 8%, while the top five captured nearly 70% of the new value created over the past decade. But with the rise of AI, the strategic question is whether those advantages continue to compound or whether AI changes one of the assumptions on which they rest.
* **What if the next challenger does not play our game better but changes the game itself?** Every leadership position is also a bet—often an implicit one—on what customers will value, how the industry will operate, and which capabilities will matter. A company should determine which of those bets are embedded in its advantage, and what happens if they turn out to be wrong.

  Automotive is an example. Tesla did not outperform incumbents by becoming a better version of a traditional automaker. Its model differs in important ways, including a direct-to-consumer distribution model rather than the traditional franchise-dealer model. Part of its valuation likely also reflects investors viewing it as an AI company and not just a car manufacturer. The broader point is that a challenger can change the basis of competition rather than simply outperform incumbents on the old one, making traditional strengths like manufacturing scale and dealer reach matter less than before.

  For challengers, the inverse question can be just as revealing: Where are incumbents least willing to compete because the customers, margins, channel, or business model look unattractive today? Most of those spaces will remain marginal. But some can become footholds from which a challenger builds the capabilities and economics to compete for tomorrow's value pools.

The answers to these questions will differ by company. In some cases, stability will reflect an advantage that is still compounding. In others, rank may simply have outlasted relevance or a sector may look stable even as value is being redistributed within it.

Greater leadership stability is not always the calm before the storm. It can reflect durable or even compounding advantage. But it can also reflect a market that has not yet felt the next wave. Longevity alone is an incomplete signal: it tells us who has endured, not whether the conditions supporting that position will continue to hold. The more useful question for leaders is what has to remain true for today's position to keep creating disproportionate value. Ultimately, leaders need to treat stability as something to test, not something to trust.
