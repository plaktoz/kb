# How to Use Occam's Razor Without Getting Cut

Occam's razor — also called the "law of parsimony" — is a problem-solving principle and mental model used to eliminate improbable options. Its core idea:

> "Among competing hypotheses, the one with the fewest assumptions should be selected."

## The Basics

The simplest explanation is generally preferable to a complex one. Simple theories are easier to verify; simple solutions are easier to execute. The model works best for forming initial conclusions before complete information is available.

Nature supports this principle. The second law of thermodynamics shows that energy use is minimized wherever possible — a ball rolls downhill to reach minimum potential energy. Similarly, repeated behaviors become habits as the brain forms neural pathways requiring less energy over time.

## Historical Background

The concept is attributed to William of Ockham, a 14th-century friar and philosopher. However, the idea is ancient — Aristotle argued for demonstrations derived from fewer postulates. Robert Grosseteste expanded on this in the 1200s, arguing that conclusions reached from fewer premises produce knowledge more efficiently.

## Applications

### Science
Scientists favor simpler hypotheses because they're easier to test and replicate. Einstein distilled it as: *"Everything should be made as simple as possible, but not simpler."* Newton similarly sought theories with only the minimum necessary assumptions. Stephen Hawking advocated cutting theoretical features that cannot be observed.

### Medicine
Doctors apply a practical version: seek the fewest causes for a patient's symptoms, favoring the most likely. The teaching aphorism — *"when you hear hoofbeats, think horses, not zebras"* — reflects this. Minimal diagnoses reduce over-treatment and dangerous treatment interactions.

### Punishment and Justice
Jeremy Bentham applied parsimony to criminal punishment, arguing penalties shouldn't cause more pain than they prevent. This thinking influenced prison abolition movements and modern rehabilitation approaches.

## Limitations and Caveats

Occam's razor is not foolproof. Key cautions:

- **Never blindly follow it** when logic, experience, or evidence points elsewhere (e.g., think "horses" — unless you're on the African savannah)
- Simplicity alone isn't enough; conclusions still require empirical backing
- Confirmation bias can distort which explanation *seems* simpler
- As physicist Lisa Randall noted, *"the world is more complicated"* than we'd expect, and "sometimes the simplest model just isn't the correct one"

Author Harlan Coben clarifies the common misreading: people reduce it to "the simplest answer is correct," but the real point is to avoid stacking unnecessary complexity when a simpler explanation suffices — *"pare it down, prune the excess."*

Occam's razor works best alongside complementary mental models: Hanlon's razor, confirmation bias awareness, availability heuristic, and hindsight bias. Mental models interlock and function best together.
