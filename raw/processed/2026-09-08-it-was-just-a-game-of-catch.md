# It Was Just a Game of Catch

Bloom recounts his family's annual trip to a small Connecticut community where childhood memories live — including his very first game of catch with his father at age five.

Their daily catch routine persisted through all seasons and life stages, from backyard pitching mounds to Stanford baseball. When his playing career ended due to shoulder wear and natural limits, those sessions stopped too.

He reflects on how time moves invisibly until something forces it into focus. Seeing his 70-year-old father alongside his own young son created that collision. He still pictures his dad as the strong, athletic man from childhood, but now that impression carries an asterisk: "for his age."

His central insight: "The most beautiful things in life are made beautiful by their impermanence" — sunsets, laughter, silence after rain.

The piece closes with Bloom retrieving two dusty gloves from an old cabinet — a small act that collapses decades. Father and son walk out together, and throwing the ball back and forth in overgrown grass, time dissolves. As he puts it: "It was just a game of catch. But it was so much more."
