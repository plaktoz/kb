# Expert consensus report: Ways generative AI can support and threaten learning in K-20 U.S. education

This report documents the findings and methodology of the consensus research project on Generative AI's impact on learning in K-20 U.S. education. It is intended for researchers, educators, policymakers, and other stakeholders seeking evidence-based guidance.

Generative AI (GenAI) has been described as both the solution to humanity’s problems and also its greatest threat. In the context of education, those who look to research find mostly low-quality studies. Recent expert reports describe the risks of GenAI use but don't tie them to specific learning processes. Experts in learning and education examined where GenAI helps learning, where it harms it, and what to do about it. They identified 65 learning processes GenAI can affect and rated how much GenAI could disrupt or enhance each one. Consensus was reached on 42 of the 65 processes.

## What are the experts saying?

We asked 30 learning scientists and educational researchers how GenAI can affect learning in K-12 and higher education. Here’s what they agreed on:

## Within the “acquire - practice - apply” cycle, GenAI works best in the middle.

True learning takes time and effort—two things GenAI tools are designed to reduce. GenAI shows most promise when used for practice to strengthen and consolidate understanding, not making initial learning acquisition or later application easier. Students with strong content knowledge, critical thinking, and self-regulation skills could use GenAI at the practice stage, as a tutor, study partner, or editor, for example. When acquiring information or applying what they understand to new problems and challenges, all students are susceptible to cognitive offloading and skill decay when they use GenAI to think for them.

## Experts recommend “using GenAI well” and “thinking well with GenAI.”

Using GenAI well includes AI literacy efforts focused on teaching students about how AI works, as well as integrations to enhance existing classroom routines (e.g., to brainstorm lesson ideas, generate practice and study materials quickly, and practice fact-checking). Thinking well with GenAI includes learning how to use GenAI to promote one’s thinking (rather than offloading thinking) and knowing when not to use it (e.g., when they are engaging in initial acquisition or practicing high-order thinking skills). Instructional design changes should prioritize the learning process over efficiency and polished products, so that students maintain cognitive independence and own their ideas.

## GenAI’s promise and pitfalls are most precarious in the elementary grades.

Students must be able to learn independently before using GenAI. Developmentally, elementary students are in the earliest stages of building social-emotional, collaboration, and self-regulation skills, and have not yet acquired robust content knowledge to evaluate GenAI outputs for accuracy. This makes them particularly vulnerable to GenAI misuse.

## Human-like conversations can’t replace real human connection.

GenAI can produce human-like text, but it lacks personal experience, empathy, and perspective-taking, all of which underpin the authentic human relationships that education and life require. Replacing collaborative peer work and teacher feedback with GenAI interactions may reduce students’ opportunities to hone their social-emotional development and collaborative learning skills. GenAI can augment what teachers do, but it cannot replace them.

## Beware of simple summaries and hyperbolic headlines—strong evidence is still lacking.

Much of the existing research on GenAI in education does not meet high scientific evidence standards, making it hard to reliably evaluate effects. Many studies rely on small samples, short timeframes, or unhelpful “AI vs. no-AI” comparisons. Reliable evidence requires long-term research with diverse student populations and thoughtful comparisons against existing, evidence-based practices.

## Executive Summary

Generative AI (GenAI) has been described as both the solution to humanity's problems and also its greatest threat (Stanford Digital Economy Lab, 2026; Yudkowsky & Soares, 2025; Zuckerberg, 2026). In the context of education, policy-makers, educators, parents, and students who look to the academic literature for guidance find mostly a deluge of low-quality research (Bauer et al., 2025; Lawson et al., 2026; Weidlich et al., 2025). Recently published expert reports have focused on illuminating vulnerabilities of GenAI use in education (Burns et al., 2026; Lodge & Loble, 2026; Pearson & Jensen, 2026), but stop short of mapping them to specific learning processes or mechanisms. We sought to bring new insights to this issue by asking 30 experts in learning and education to come to consensus on what aspects of learning could be enhanced by GenAI, which aspects were vulnerable to disruption by GenAI, and what these experts recommended to promote effective learning with GenAI. What we found was sometimes surprising, often concerning, and yet hopeful for the future.

Our experts identified 65 distinct learning processes that could be affected by GenAI, and then came to consensus on the vulnerability and enhancement potential for each. This process-level granularity reveals precisely where GenAI can enhance learning and where it can disrupt it, leading to four core findings.

First, the experts' ratings revealed what we are calling the "GenAI Paradox." Casual, careless use of general-purpose GenAI (e.g., popular models from large corporations) can threaten the very learning processes necessary for committing new ideas to memory, and also the higher-order thinking needed to deeply understand and use these ideas in novel ways. However, seemingly-paradoxically, GenAI can enhance the "middle step" between initial acquisition and high-level use of new ideas: the step where learners strengthen and consolidate their initial acquisition of those ideas. In short, people should (1) initially learn without GenAI assistance, then (2) use GenAI to strengthen their initial understanding, and then (3) prioritize their own thinking when using those learned and strengthened ideas in new ways, in new contexts, and for new discoveries.

Second, the experts reached strong consensus that the disruption risk to higher-order cognitive and metacognitive processes is severe and one-sided. Specifically, excessive use of GenAI can threaten people's ability to develop critical thinking and self-regulated learning skills, or cause existing skills to decay due to not being practiced.

Third, the experts reached consensus on how GenAI can disrupt the social aspects of learning, such as human connections, social-emotional development, and collaboration skills. In an era of concern about declines in the time young people spend with each other, GenAI must not further impede the development of the social skills necessary for learning, positive relationships, life success, and civic engagement.

Fourth, among the strongest areas of consensus among our experts was that the existing empirical literature on the effects of GenAI on learning is seriously lacking in rigor and quality. Existing literature on technology's effects on learning, the small number of high-quality studies on GenAI and learning, and our experts' experiences all support the "GenAI Paradox" but there is a clear need for better research, prioritizing rigor over a rush to be "the first" to publish in this area.

To help stakeholders navigate these risks, the experts produced a prioritized recommendations action map. It begins with immediate, high-feasibility wins focused on using GenAI well and then shifts to thinking well with GenAI, prioritizing recommendations that protect students' cognitive independence, agency, and ethical norms. Finally, it highlights high-value but difficult long-term priorities that face significant systemic and institutional barriers.

## What Do We Mean By Consensus?

In this report, the “consensus” was created around ratings of the likelihood of each learning process being enhanced by GenAI or being vulnerable to it, as well as each processes’ importance to learning and the strength of the empirical evidence for the effects of GenAI on that process.

For the Enhancement and Vulnerability ratings, expert consensus was defined as a mean rating ≥ 3.0 on the 1-5 scale with at least 75% of respondents rating it ≥ 3.0. Of the 65 learning processes, 42 reached consensus on either Enhancement, Vulnerability, or both (see Figure 1).

For the Importance and Evidence ratings, expert consensus was defined as ≥ 75% of respondents responding with a 3 or 4 on a four-point scale (i.e., rating it as "important" or "pivotal" on the Importance to Learning scale and "moderate" or "strong" on the Evidence Strength scale).

The experts were also asked to offer recommendations on responsible and effective GenAI-supported learning that the lead coordinating authors grouped into 11 themes. These recommendations were subsequently rated by all experts on perceived Impact (How large and meaningful would its impact be if implemented?) and Implementation Realism (How realistic is the recommendation?)

## Our Definition of "Generative AI"

The focus on this project was the most common use-case for GenAI: via access to general-purpose large language models produced by large companies (e.g., ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini). Given their variety and novelty, we did not ask our experts to comment upon use-cases where GenAI was incorporated into an existing application (e.g., a word processing application with additional GenAI features) nor did we ask them to comment upon specific tools that use GenAI to enhance learning (e.g., MagicSchool). Nonetheless, we believe our recommendations apply to such applications and tools, and developers would be wise to incorporate those recommendations into their designs.

## Headline Findings

## Vulnerabilities

The foundations of memory are vulnerable to disruption by GenAI.

Encoding, Retrieval, and Information Acquisition each reached the Vulnerability consensus threshold (75%) but did not reach consensus on Enhancement, indicating expert concern that GenAI may interfere with how learners form memory traces in the first place. The only positive signal in this category was the idea that GenAI is likely to enhance students' ability to manage the Mental Load that comes with learning complex material, such as by helping humans access supporting information quickly.

GenAI can disrupt learners' deeper processing, including thoughtful, critical, and evaluative engagement.

In Higher-Order Thinking, 10 of 18 items reached consensus on Vulnerability only: Creativity, Critical Thinking, Epistemic Agency, Epistemic Cognition, Epistemic Metacognition, Evaluation, Inquiry Learning, Intellectual Virtues, Learning From Diverse Perspectives, and Sourcing. Enhancement ratings for these same items were correspondingly low, meaning experts do not see a trade-off — rather, they see one-sided risk. Experts also identified a handful of enhancement-only cognitive processes (i.e., Brainstorming, Curiosity, Debugging, and Information Search) but these were exceptions.

GenAI can be a resource for those students who know how to seek help but is otherwise likely to disrupt the enactment of self-regulated learning processes.

Of the seven learning processes in the "Self-Regulated Learning and Metacognition" category, our experts reached consensus regarding Enhancement for only one process: Help-Seeking. Conversely, they reached consensus on Vulnerability for Metacognition, Self-Regulated Learning in general, and Motivation Regulation. Thus, overall, many of the very self-regulation skills needed for productively engaging with GenAI were the ones our experts thought were most vulnerable when students use GenAI.

GenAI may disrupt the social fabric of learning, with no clear enhancement pathway.

Experts reached consensus on five Collaboration items for Vulnerability only: Social Connection, Socio-Emotional Development, Social Learning, Collaborative Learning, and Sense of Belonging. Across the cluster, mean Enhancement ratings were consistently low. This pattern is more prominent than patterns in any other set of processes and reflects experts' concerns about the isolation that GenAI can aggravate.

Affective-motivational processes are vulnerable to GenAI use.

All four items reached consensus threshold on Vulnerability - Agency, Engagement, Motivation, and Persistence. Experts were concerned that key aspects of learning, emotions and the processing they support, may be disrupted by GenAI.

Writing is the only domain learning item experts agree on, and it is at high risk.

Writing reached high consensus for Vulnerability. All other domain learning processes — Math Learning, STEM Reasoning, Reading to Learn, and Language Proficiency — did not reach consensus, which reflects genuine heterogeneity of expert opinion rather than agreement that no effect exists.

There is greater concern for disruption at the elementary education level.

In their initial ratings of the core cognitive and motivational processes that GenAI is likely to enhance or disrupt, experts viewed students in elementary education as more vulnerable to disruption than students at the secondary and higher education levels. At the same time, the processes most often endorsed as potentially enhanced or vulnerable were largely consistent across all three levels. This pattern suggests that even though the learning processes most vulnerable to disruption are the same across development, they are the most at risk in younger learners.

## Enhancement

GenAI can enhance the strategies learners use to refine and consolidate acquired information.

The strongest enhancement signals clustered in Learning/Studying Strategies: Learning From Feedback, Self-Testing, Learning From Examples, Tutoring, Scaffolding, Learning from Questioning, and Deliberate Practice. These processes represent the ways learners consolidate the acquisition of acquired information and experts agree GenAI can enhance them.

## Nuanced Findings

Motivation can be both enhanced and threatened by GenAI.

The motivation learning process was one of the few where experts reached consensus that GenAI can both enhance and disrupt it. When GenAI is used to personalize instruction to students' knowledge and interests, it can strengthen motivation. However, when GenAI is used to replace student learning, effort, and investment, it can deprive students of the experiences needed to develop productive, long-term motivation for learning and success.

The negative and positive effects of GenAI on learning depend heavily on how it is used.

In their qualitative responses, several experts mentioned that whether GenAI enhances or disrupts learning depends greatly on how people use it. These responses push back on simplistic discourses (such as, "Is GenAI good or bad?") and highlight the need for more nuanced research into the conditions under which and for whom GenAI affects learning. What is notable is that, according to the experts, GenAI may disrupt the foundational memory processes that support learning as well as the self-regulated learning and metacognition processes needed to use it in non-disruptive ways.

## The GenAI Paradox

The findings reveal a "paradox" of GenAI-assisted learning: initial acquisition of information is highly vulnerable to GenAI, subsequent consolidation is significantly enhanced by it, and then more advanced forms of higher-order thinking become vulnerable. The vulnerabilities to both foundational memory and advanced higher-order learning processes stem from cognitive offloading. That is, GenAI may tempt learners into forgoing the effort necessary to initially acquire information as well as the effort needed to think in a higher-order manner. However, the experts agree that once information has been acquired, GenAI as currently deployed can help learners consolidate that information with existing knowledge, leading to better recall and comprehension (e.g., by developing quizzes and probing for explanations). Nonetheless, there are significant differences between prompting consolidation versus higher-order thinking. Higher-order thinking is nuanced and often domain-specific, and experts agree that GenAI, as currently deployed, does not yet reliably prompt or support such thinking. Thus, GenAI may disrupt both initial information acquisition as well as the deployment of higher-order thinking, and its facility at prompting productive thought seems limited to knowledge comprehension and consolidation.

## Big Feelings, Little Evidence

Across 42 of the 65 items that reached consensus, expert ratings of Importance to learning were high (most items above 3.0 on a 1–4 scale, i.e., "Important" or "Pivotal") whereas ratings of Evidence Strength were low (most items below 2.5, indicating "Promising evidence" or only "Demonstrates a rationale"; see Figure 3). Experts agreed these learning processes matter and that GenAI is likely affecting them, but the empirical base for those judgments is thin.

This is the most important meta-finding of the report. It is not a caveat about the consensus; it is a call to action. Experts rated many of the learning processes most likely to be enhanced by or vulnerable to GenAI as also some of the most important, and they were concerned that the evidence base has not caught up to the hype and panic cycles. Even though GenAI has been integrated in various learning spaces at great speed, the pace of research on how GenAI affects learning simply has not kept up. Limitations around funding foci for this research (e.g., prioritizing tool development and innovation) and the long timeframes of existing incentives for research (e.g., publication in peer-reviewed journals) have led to a wanting empirical literature. Retractions of high-profile meta-analyses have provided further evidence that quality research takes careful effort, with the public's need for guidance running ahead of the best available evidence at this time.

## Key Themes from Recommendations Ratings

Start Here: High Impact, High Feasibility. Three recommendations were rated high on both impact and implementation realism (2, 5, 7), providing a clear set of short-term priorities that share a common logic: they work within existing instructional structures by enabling learners and educators to engage GenAI more skillfully. In other words, the focus is on how to use GenAI well.

The Bigger Goal: Thinking Well with GenAI. A broader set of recommendations fell above the midpoint on both dimensions (1, 3, 4, 8, 9), suggesting both value and feasibility, including approaches and strategies that protect learners' cognitive independence and tolerance for struggle, approach AI as a partner to interrogate rather than defer to, and operating within ethically and socially grounded norms. In other words, the focus is on how to think well with GenAI.

Worth Pursuing, But Hard: Where Systemic Barriers Remain. Finally, three recommendations were rated as the hardest to implement (6, 10, 11), pointing to the tension between transformation potential and systemic barriers in education policy, institutional infrastructure, and equity.

## Situating Findings in the 2026 Evidence Landscape

This consensus report mirrors other recent major reports (Burns et al., 2026; Fesler et al., 2026; Lodge & Loble, 2026; Neugnot-Cerioli, 2026; OECD, 2026; Pearson & Jensen, 2026) that used very different methods and came from different disciplines (e.g., global stakeholder consultation, causal-evidence review, cognitive theory, developmental science, and large-scale teacher surveys). Together, they paint a notably consistent picture of how to think about GenAI and learning. Here, we compare and contrast our findings against the findings from these other reports, noting where they agree, where they complement and extend one another, and where there are remaining points of disagreement.

## Where the Literature Converges with Our Consensus

The enhancement-disruption split we observe is the same "performance paradox" others report. Our experts determined GenAI can facilitate the deployment of study strategies that can consolidate and strengthen learning (e.g., Learning from Feedback, Self-Testing, Tutoring, Scaffolding) yet also threatens the initial acquisition of information and its availability during future learning. [Of note, this applies to learners using “retail” GenAI on their own, rather than use-cases involving teacher- or expert-developed cases of GenAI.] Lodge and Loble (2026) focused on the distinction between students’ performance with GenAI, which can be enhanced, versus their long-term learning and use of information, which GenAI can impede. Fesler et al. (2026) made similar arguments that GenAI can promote immediate performance but impede the productive struggle needed for transfer and long-term use. The OECD Digital Education Outlook 2026 also cited evidence that GenAI can enhance short-term performance but impede the internalization of information needed for deep learning and transfer. These findings all converge on the idea that learners who wish to develop knowledge and skills successfully must be thoughtful about how they use GenAI to consolidate, and not rely on it to acquire information.

The disruption signal concentrates in the same higher-order and self-regulatory processes. Our experts agreed that the development and deployment of higher-order thinking (e.g., critical thinking, epistemic cognition) and self-regulation (e.g., self-regulated learning, metacognition) are threatened when learners rely too heavily on GenAI. This matches concerns raised by Lodge and Loble (2026) regarding detrimental offloading, which replaces the desirable difficulties required to build durable understanding as well as the acquisition and practice of metacognition and self-regulated learning skills. Whether called metacognitive laziness or the illusion of competence, the end result is that learners are deprived of the opportunities to develop, test, and refine the very skills that would, among other desirable outcomes, help them use GenAI more effectively. The OECD report had similar language regarding overreliance on GenAI, which can reduce learners’ active engagement, weaken learners’ skills, and impede long-term learning. Pearson and Jensen (2026) shared similar concerns.

The one-sided disruption of the social fabric is echoed wherever the reports address it. The strong consensus among our experts that GenAI can problematically replace the social engagement necessary for learning and long-term success was mirrored by the OECD report with their emphasis on motivation, relationships and social-emotional learning as desirable processes and products of learning. Burns et al. (2026) also warned that extensive GenAI use can threaten learners’ social and emotional well-being, either preventing or damaging the supportive relationships made with teachers and peers. Neugnot-Cerioli (2026) shared concerns about GenAI use disrupting entire social and relational systems in learning environments. In sum, there was consensus across reports, and among our experts, that the social and affective dimensions of learning are at great risk of being disrupted by GenAI.

The "wrong question" reframing is widely shared. Across the reports, there was agreement that asking whether GenAI is either good or bad for learning was overly simplistic and simply the wrong question to ask. Burns et al. (2026) argued that GenAI’s effects on learning and learners depends on how it is used. The OECD report adopted language about treating GenAI as a learner partner rather than a learning shortcut, also suggesting a nuanced view. Lodge and Loble (2026), as well as Pearson and Jensen (2026), argued the implementation of GenAI into education is where gains or harms will be realized. A common theme was that simplistic “yes or no” views of GenAI do not match the evidence and risk easily-accepted but ultimately-harmful views on this technology.

## What This Consensus Adds, and Where It Diverges

A process-level resolution the other reports do not provide. The consensus across reports is encouraging; where this report provides unique value is in how our experts think GenAI affects specific learning processes and mechanisms. Burns et al. (2026) focused at a more global level for their recommendations whereas OECD (2026) included use-cases involving tutoring, collaboration, and assessment. Other reports focused on causal evidence (Fesler et al., 2026), connections to existing theory (Lodge & Loble, 2026), and age-based recommendations (Neugnot-Cerioli, 2026). Our report complements these reports by identifying where experts achieved consensus on enhancement and disruption across 65 individual learning processes, grouped into four dimensions. This enabled us to provide more fine-grained guidance that aligns with our recommendation to avoid overly-simplistic “is GenAI good or bad” questions and answers.

A real divergence on how recoverable the deep-process risk is. Some of the other reports argued for potential positive effects of GenAI on higher-order thinking processes (e.g., Fesler et al., 2026; OECD, 2026). Our experts, at the level of consensus, disagreed despite some empirical evidence in the literature supporting such positive effects. The literature on the effects of GenAI remains inchoate; as such, it would be premature to state that the empirical evidence conclusively argues that GenAI either enhances or disrupts such processes. Clearly, more research, and more rigorous research, is needed to identify whether, how, and under what conditions GenAI can support learners’ acquisition and successful deployment of higher-order thinking processes. Given current evidence and theory, our experts are not yet optimistic.

From process mapping to action. Beyond mapping which learning processes are at risk, this report also produced a prioritized action map that translates the process-level findings into actionable mitigation priorities. Recommendations rated high in both impact and feasibility correspond to processes AI can enhance, such as study strategies, whereas recommendations focused on protecting cognitive independence directly target higher-order thinking, where experts identified the highest risk of AI disruption. To that end, we answer OECD and Fesler et al.'s optimism about pedagogical design through specific design recommendations that prioritize cognitive and metacognitive independence.

## Guidance for Researchers, Funders, and Policymakers

## What learning processes does GenAI support, for whom, under what conditions?

More rigorous research is needed to understand the nuanced ways GenAI affects learning processing. GenAI may serve as an adequate tool for information consolidation, including self-testing and providing feedback on students' understanding of basic concepts. However, even these enhancements must be further nuanced based on student factors (e.g., developmental level, prior knowledge) and contextual factors (e.g., availability of educators or other adults who can support student learning with GenAI). Again, GenAI is neither "good" nor "bad" but rather a multifaceted tool with differential effects across learners and contexts.

## How can GenAI help students learn rather than simply perform?

Experts' concerns about the effects of GenAI on learning arose from cognitive offloading and metacognitive laziness threats. If students use GenAI to merely perform at school, such as by asking GenAI to write papers and answer problem sets, then they are not actually learning how to do those things themselves. Deep learning is and must remain the primary goal of education, and GenAI must be positioned to support rather than replace that learning. A key part of that positioning is likely explaining to students how short-term gains in performance likely short-cut the learning needed for long-term success.

## What is lost if GenAI disrupts the very learning processes needed to use GenAI responsibly?

In concurrence with prior reports, our experts found GenAI strengthens the operational mechanics of study: practice, feedback, and scaffolding. What is novel here is where they diverged from that consensus. The processes they flagged as most vulnerable, including higher-order thinking, self-regulated learning, and collaborative work, are precisely the ones the GenAI future most requires in lifelong learning, civic participation, and adaptive expertise. Collaboration illustrates why this is an opportunity rather than only a threat. Collaborative problem solving is anchored to a shared product, so partners must build common ground and can agree on whether progress is being made; collaborative learning has no such visible referent, so its erosion can go undetected. That asymmetry is a design target. Tools that make the process of thinking, regulating, and collaborating visible, rather than only its output, could support capabilities that have historically been difficult to observe or assess at all. The risk is real, but it is a risk of default design, not of GenAI itself. Research must prioritize GenAI that teaches and provokes these processes rather than performing them.

## How much of what we believe about GenAI and learning is actually supported by evidence?

Experts voiced the whispered secret that most in the field of education research know: current empirical research on GenAI effects on learning has, overall, poor quality. Meta-analyses of poorly designed or poorly analyzed data are moot: garbage-in means garbage-out. Resources must be directed toward groups with the expertise needed to ask the right questions and conduct informative studies about GenAI effects on learning.

## What would responsible guidance on GenAI in education actually require?

At minimum, responsible guidance would require moving beyond the false choice between unrestricted adoption and blanket prohibition. Experts were clear that the effects of GenAI on learning are not uniform: some processes benefit (e.g., study strategies) whereas others face genuine risk (e.g., higher-order thinking, self-regulation, social learning, transfer). Effective policy must be calibrated to these differences. Nuance here is not optional, but rather it is the whole point.

## How can GenAI help us achieve our educational objectives?

The public's and policy makers' interest in whether and how GenAI can be incorporated into education is understandable, but such questions must be investigated with care. There is a best tool for every job, but no tool is best for every job. Rather than asking broad questions (e.g., "Can GenAI replace teachers?"), we should be asking, "What educational objectives can be reached better or more quickly or more easily if we augmented existing educational supports and structures with GenAI?"

## What is the role for GenAI in education?

The answer depends on what we take the objective to be. If the goal is mastery of well-defined curated content, assessed by tests, the case for GenAI looks different than if the goal is helping learners build things, individually or in groups. Our experts were asked primarily about the former, so the conclusions in this report should be read as bearing on learning processes in curricular contexts rather than on GenAI as a tool for creative production, where its value may be greatest and where evidence remains thin. In current workforce development efforts, there is growing evidence that GenAI works best when it augments experts' abilities, rather than replacing them. Therefore, GenAI in education may be best positioned for supporting teachers' expertise in implementing, personalizing, adapting, and delivering curriculum that enhances student learning processes. Given the limited resources available for incorporating GenAI into K-20 education, we may need to focus those resources where GenAI produces the "biggest bang for the buck" and it might just be first and foremost in the hands of teachers, rather than students.

## Supplementary Materials

The project employed a rigorous Delphi methodology to synthesize perspectives about the ways GenAI may enhance learning, as well as which aspects of learning may be most vulnerable to GenAI use, across experts from cognitive science, educational psychology, learning sciences, and educational technology.

## Key project goals

• Identify learning processes that can be meaningfully enhanced by GenAI in K–20 settings

• Identify learning processes most vulnerable to GenAI use

• Generate evidence-based, actionable recommendations for educators and policymakers • Produce an open, transparent consensus document for broad dissemination

## Preregistration and Open Science

The full methodology was preregistered on AsPredicted.org prior to data collection (available at: https://aspredicted.org/dj47ne.pdf). The anonymized expert rating dataset, all survey instruments, and analysis code will be posted publicly on the Open Science Framework (OSF) following publication of this report. All contributing experts disclosed financial interests in AI companies and educational technology; disclosures are reported transparently.

## Expert Identification and Recruitment

The CLAs assembled a team of contributing authors using the following steps: Interested experts from the Oregon Learning Sciences meeting where the idea for a project like this originated. The CLAs also independently generated other candidate names based on their own knowledge of the field. This list was then discussed among CLAs to finalize. Contributing experts were selected against three primary criteria: (a) active research in learning sciences, cognitive psychology, educational psychology, or related fields; (b) a peer-reviewed publication record in relevant areas; and (c) experience with educational applications of technology. Representation was prioritized across cognitive science and learning theory, educational technology and AI, K–12 and higher education practice in the U.S., and equity and accessibility in education.

## Survey 1: Nomination and Categorization of Processes and Recommendations

Each contributing author independently nominated up to 10 learning processes or principles they believed may be impacted (as vulnerable or enhanced) by GenAI. For each nomination, authors provided a brief description (~50 words), approximately three key supporting references, the educational level(s) where the principle applies, and an indication of whether the nominated principle emerged from the author's own research. Authors also nominated up to five specific recommendations for effective, responsible, and ethical learning with GenAI. 27 experts participated in the first phase along with three lead coordinating authors (i.e., n = 30), nominated learning processes likely to be affected by GenAI. The lead coordinating authors consolidated all process nominations into 65 unique learning processes grouped into eight categories (i.e., affective-motivational processes, memory processes, self-regulated learning and metacognition, higher-order thinking processes, collaboration processes, learning/studying strategies, domain-specific learning processes, transfer processes).

Finally, in phase one the experts were also asked to offer recommendations on responsible and effective GenAI-supported learning that the lead coordinating authors grouped into 11 themes.

## Survey 2: Consolidation and Rating of Processes and Recommendations

26 of the 27 experts who participated in phase one of the project, along with the three lead authors (i.e., n = 29, see note below), rated each of the 65 learning processes on four dimensions:

Enhancement Likelihood (1–5): How likely is GenAI use to enhance/support this process?

Disruption Likelihood (1–5): How likely is GenAI use to disrupt/hinder this process?

Importance to Learning (1–4): How important is this process to learning?

Not important — learning is unaffected by this process

Worth considering — learning may benefit from this process

Important — process is helpful to learning

Pivotal — process is crucial to learning

Strength of Evidence (1–4): How strong is the existing evidence for GenAI's influence on this process?

Demonstrates a rationale — conceptual or theoretical grounding exists with logical mechanisms proposed; empirical evidence is insufficient, limited to pilot studies, or yet to be gathered.

Promising evidence — supported by emerging research showing consistent associations (e.g., correlations) or preliminary effects, but lacking sufficient replication or methodological rigor for stronger conclusions.

Moderate evidence — supported by a body of high-quality studies showing generally consistent patterns, though they may include some mixed findings or methodological limitations (e.g., quasi-experiments, small samples, etc.) that prevent definitive conclusions.

Strong evidence — supported by multiple high-quality studies with consistent findings, including systematic reviews, meta-analyses, or replicated experimental studies demonstrating robust effects.

The experts also rated the 11 themed expert recommendations on perceived:

Impact (1–5): How large and meaningful would its impact be if implemented?

Implementation Realism (1–5): How realistic is the recommendation?

Note: one of the 29 experts did not provide quantitative ratings, so percentages are calculated out of 28.

## Consensus Determination

A process item was considered to have reached consensus if, on either Enhancement or Disruption, it met both of the following criteria:

Mean rating ≥ 3.0 on the 1–5 scale

≥ 75% of respondents gave a rating of 3, 4, or 5

For Importance and Evidence (1–4 scales), consensus was determined if ≥ 75% of respondents gave a rating of 3 or 4 (i.e., "Important" or "Pivotal" for Importance; "Moderate evidence" or "Strong evidence" for Evidence Strength).
