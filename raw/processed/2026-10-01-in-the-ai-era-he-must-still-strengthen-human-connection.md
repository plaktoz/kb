# In the AI era, HE must still strengthen human connection

In higher education setting, artificial intelligence (AI) is changing education, businesses, economies, politics, and social life. Its influence is increasingly visible in African higher education, where universities are experimenting with AI in teaching, research, administration, health sciences, agriculture, engineering, and public service.

Some students are already using generative AI to explain difficult concepts, summarise readings, improve academic writing, translate material, and support research. Faculty members use it to prepare learning resources, analyse information, and manage growing workloads.

These uses of AI offer real benefits. However, the use of AI can also reproduce bias, compromise privacy, facilitate academic misconduct, and widen inequalities between institutions and students.

A study conducted by Florah Modiba, Amanda van den Berg and Stephen Mago in 2025 entitled 'Opportunities and challenges of generative artificial intelligence supporting research in African classrooms' and published in the South African Journal of Higher Education noted that AI has benefits such as being a means of improving access, feedback, learner autonomy and academic productivity, but they also identify problems related to digital exclusion, insufficient institutional preparation, weak governance, and overreliance on automated output.

In fact, a study by Babawane Olawale, and Bonginkosi Mutongoza, in 2024, 'Artificial intelligence: An empirical survey of student and staff perspectives' and published in the Interdisciplinary Journal of Education Research came up with similar findings.

## What kind of university is AI creating?

The central question is, therefore, not whether African universities should use AI. Students and educators are already using it. The more important question is what kind of university its use will create.

The world over, higher education faces two closely related responsibilities. The first is to preserve genuine relationships among students, educators, staff, and communities. The second is to keep critical thinking, empathy, academic integrity, and well-being at the centre of education.

AI should enhance human capability without displacing the relationships through which students learn to reason, cooperate, care, and participate in public life. Thus, African universities must pay attention to these critical issues when it comes to the use of AI for teaching and research purposes.

## Nurturing human relationships in the AI era

University education has never consisted solely of transferring information. Students also learn through conversation, mentorship, disagreement on content being discussed, teamwork, observation, and participation in communities of inquiry.

An AI system can produce an explanation in seconds, but it cannot fully reproduce the trust that develops between a student and a lecturer or the sense of solidarity created when classmates struggle with a problem together and come up with a solution.

This distinction matters in African higher education. Many students depend on peer networks, study groups, faculty mentorship, family support and community relationships while navigating academic demands and financial pressure.

If AI becomes a substitute for these relationships, rather than a tool used within them, it could intensify isolation and reduce education to a series of automated transactions.

## Using Ubuntu in HE during the AI era

An African framework for responding to this risk can be found in Ubuntu, commonly expressed through the idea that a person becomes a person through other people.

Zuleika Suliman, Ntshimane Mohale, Kgabo Maphoto, and Kershnee Sevnarayan, in their 2024 study, 'The interconnectedness between Ubuntu principles and generative artificial intelligence in distance higher education institutions' published in Discover Education, connected Ubuntu principles with the use of generative AI in distance higher education.

Their analysis suggests that AI adoption should be assessed in relation to collective well-being, mutual care, human dignity, and participation – not simply efficiency. They also warn against allowing automation to weaken empathy, communication, creativity and critical thought.

Ubuntu does not require universities to reject AI. It offers a way to decide how AI should be used. An AI tool is educationally valuable when it helps people participate more fully in a learning community. It becomes harmful when it reduces students to data points, isolates them from one another, or removes human judgment from decisions that affect their lives.

A lecturer at an open or distance-learning institution, for example, might use an AI tutor to answer routine questions after working hours. That service could be useful to students who work during the day or live far from campus.

It should not replace lecturer consultations, tutor-led discussions, peer-support groups, or other opportunities to form relationships. The stronger model combines continuous digital assistance with scheduled human interaction.

This balance is just as important in residential universities. For example, agriculture students at Sokoine University of Agriculture in Tanzania, might use AI to examine soil records, rainfall patterns, or signs of crop disease. They could then work in groups to assess whether the recommendations fit local conditions before meeting farmers and agricultural extension officers.

The AI system would help organise information, while students, faculty members, and community partners would supply contextual knowledge and make the final judgment.

The same principle applies in health education. Medical or nursing students at the College of Medicine and Health Sciences at the University of Rwanda could use an AI system to evaluate a simulated patient case. Rather than accepting its proposed diagnosis, students could assess its reasoning in teams, identify missing information, and consider the patient's language, income, location, family responsibilities, and access to treatment.

They could then practise explaining their recommendations with compassion. The purpose would not be limited to reaching a technically plausible answer. It would include developing clinical judgment, ethical responsibility, and the ability to care for their patient.

Human connection is especially important when AI systems are unreliable or culturally limited. Models trained largely on material produced outside Africa may misunderstand local practices, omit African scholarship, or perform poorly in indigenous languages.

Local educators, professionals, and communities are not obstacles to automation in such cases. They are essential interpreters and decision-makers who can identify errors and judge whether a recommendation is appropriate.

Universities should, therefore, design AI-assisted learning around interaction. Group investigations, peer review, oral defence, community-engaged projects, mentorship, and collaborative problem-solving can prevent AI from becoming an isolated shortcut. These activities also help students develop the communication skills they will need in workplaces where AI systems are common but responsibility remains human.

## Keeping humanity at the centre of higher education

As outlined already, AI can expand access to information, provide rapid feedback, support research, improve accessibility, and help African universities respond to resource constraints. It can also weaken interpersonal connection, reproduce bias, threaten privacy, enable misconduct, and deepen existing inequalities. Both possibilities are real.

African universities should, therefore, judge AI, not only by what it can produce, but by what it contributes to human development. Does it strengthen relationships or leave students more isolated? Does it deepen thought or reward intellectual shortcuts?

Does it extend access or exclude students with limited resources? Does it give educators more time to teach and mentor, or reduce education to automated delivery? Does it recognise African knowledge, languages, and priorities, or treat the continent as a passive market for systems designed elsewhere?

Answering these questions requires institutional research. Universities should monitor patterns of access, student learning, staff workload, well-being, integrity cases, language performance, and community impact.

Evidence from one institution or country should not automatically be generalised across a continent as diverse as Africa. Local evaluation must accompany broader regional cooperation.

## Do students become more connected to their communities?

Universities in Africa have a responsibility that extends beyond preparing students to use the latest AI models and tools. They must prepare their students to decide when a tool should be used, whose interests it serves, what assumptions it contains, and when human judgment should take priority. That work depends on critical thought, moral imagination, empathy and relationships.

African universities can approach AI from a position of agency. They can adopt useful tools while contesting harmful assumptions, expand digital access while protecting human contact, and draw on Ubuntu and other African indigenous knowledge systems when defining responsible technology use.

They should also develop systems grounded in local knowledge rather than accepting imported models as universally valid. The university's core mission of teaching, research and service remains a human one.

AI may support that mission, but it cannot determine its purpose. The measure of successful adaption of AI will not be how much activity universities automate. It will be whether students become more thoughtful, connected, critical, capable, and responsible to the communities they serve.
