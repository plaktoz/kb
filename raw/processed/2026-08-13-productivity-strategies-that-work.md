# 9 Expert-Backed Productivity Strategies to Help You Finally Get Things Done

If you've ever found yourself swearing you'll start that task right after this one video (or snack, or scroll), you're not alone. Sometimes the best way to jump-start productivity is to lean into unconventional strategies that work with your brain rather than fighting against it.

## 1. Trick Your Brain with Tiny Steps
Therapist Risa Williams, LMFT, author of "The Procrastination Playbook," makes the first step laughably small. "Make it sound so easy that your brain has trouble resisting doing it," she says. "Instead of telling yourself you have to write a chapter, tell yourself all you have to do is open the document and type the title." Most people end up doing more than planned, because getting started is the hardest part. Williams calls this the "Ridiculously Easy First Step" technique.

## 2. Blast Your 'Hype Playlist'
Sam Dylan Finch, a neuro-affirming peer support coach, calls this the "Hype Playlist" strategy. "They need to be songs that, if you heard them at a wedding, you'd be dashing to the dance floor instinctually," Finch explains. The combination of music and nostalgia engages the reward-processing system and supports emotional regulation, helping jolt you out of a distraction loop like doomscrolling.

## 3. Count Down Like You're About to Dance
Finch encourages verbal cues like "3, 2, 1 — LET'S GO!" paired with a physical motion. This is similar to the five-second rule popularized by Mel Robbins: an "interstitial action" that physically signals to your brain that you're ending one activity and moving to another.

## 4. Ditch the To-Do List for a Calendar
Instead of working from a to-do list, spend an hour a week scheduling tasks directly into a calendar — a method called Power Planning, designed by perfectionism coach Sam Laura Brown. It resembles time blocking but also schedules rest, socializing, and exercise, helping prevent burnout.

## 5. Use Music Like a Task Timer
Williams recommends a "task playlist" — one or two upbeat songs — to give yourself a set amount of time to clean, sort paperwork, or push through emails. Pairing a specific "task anthem" with a chore builds musical muscle memory.

## 6. Rate Tasks by 'Intensity,' Not Importance
Williams suggests breaking a task into smaller pieces and rating them by "intensity" rather than importance, then tackling the low-intensity parts first. This builds momentum before facing harder parts of an overwhelming task.

## 7. Move First, Think Later
Overthinking often sabotages productivity. Using movement — walking in a circle before writing an email, or a body shake before folding laundry — disrupts the "I'm stuck" loop, a strategy many neurodivergent people use instinctively.

## 8. Narrate Your Day Like a Sports Commentator
Narrating your actions aloud, like a cooking show host, adds novelty and engagement to boring routines and keeps the brain focused on one step at a time.

## 9. Celebrate Every Micro-Win
Small celebrations — clapping, a little dance, saying it out loud — reinforce positive behaviors and tap into the brain's reward center, providing a dopamine boost for completing hard tasks. As Williams puts it, the key to motivation is "making it feel good to do things that are hard for you."
