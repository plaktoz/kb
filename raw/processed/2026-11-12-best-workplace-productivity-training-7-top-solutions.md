## How we chose the best productivity training

A productivity course is easy to buy and hard to make stick, so we judged each option the way an HR or L&D leader has to: by what happens after the first session.

Four questions looked at the learning itself. Does it cover the human skills that drive productivity, such as focus, planning and communication, rather than a single technique? How broad is it, and can people go further as their needs change? Does it build lasting competence through practice and return visits, or is it finished and forgotten? And does the format fit into a working day, because training that needs a free week gets postponed?

Three more looked at rollout. Can it reach a whole team or organisation, not just volunteers? Can you see whether people are using it? And does the value hold up against the budget? On measurability, we looked for adoption and engagement you can report on, and we did not treat promised ROI figures as proof.

Finally, fit. No option wins every category, so each entry says who it suits and where another choice is stronger. We checked formats against each provider’s own pages in November 2026. Prices and plans change, so confirm them before you buy.

## Coursera: Best for accredited, in-depth hard skills

Coursera is the clearest pick when the goal is a credential. It brings university-backed courses, professional certificates and specialist programmes together, which makes it strong for deeper technical skills such as data, analytics or project management, where people want something recognised on a CV or a profile. Learners can go as deep as they like, then show the result with a certificate.

For teams, Coursera for Business adds shared access, with a Team plan for smaller groups and a custom-priced Enterprise plan for larger organisations. Pricing is per seat, so costs scale with headcount. Capterra lists the Team plan from around $399 per user per year, but check the current quote.

Where it is less suited is continuous, whole-team behaviour change. Coursera is built around courses that people choose and complete, which works well for a defined skill gap and a motivated learner. Shifting how a whole department prioritises, communicates and protects its focus is a different job. It needs practice that carries on after the course ends, which is where a system built around adoption, such as GoodHabitz, tends to fit better.

Choose Coursera if your budget is aimed at a named qualification or a technical capability, and pair it with something broader for the habits underneath. A library gives your people access. A system gives them a path.

## FranklinCovey: Best for in-person, facilitated workshops

FranklinCovey’s The 5 Choices to Extraordinary Productivity is one of the best-known workshops in this space. It runs as a live programme, either in person or live online, and a 5-Week Quickstart helps participants apply the five choices once the session is over. It is aimed at business professionals at every level, so it can work for individual contributors and senior leaders alike.

It suits cohorts that benefit from a facilitator and discussion in the room, such as a leadership team or a group of new managers. The trade-off is reach. Workshops teach people in groups and at set moments, so keeping the habits alive across a large, distributed workforce takes extra effort. Pricing varies by format and group size, so request a quote for your headcount.

## David Allen Company (GTD): Best for a proven productivity method

Getting Things Done (GTD) is a method more than a course. Created by David Allen and set out in his New York Times bestselling book, it gives people one clear way to capture commitments, decide next actions and review their work every week. The David Allen Company teaches it through courses and coaching, delivered with certified partners around the world, and GTD Connect, a subscription learning centre, helps people reinforce it afterwards.

It suits teams that want everyone to share one language for managing work. Because GTD is a single method, it covers organising and planning well, but not the wider human skills, such as communication, that sit beside them.

## Alison: Best for free, self-paced individual courses

Alison offers free courses across a wide range of topics, including time management and office productivity. Most certificate courses take around two to three hours, learners can complete them at no cost, and a digital or printed certificate is an optional purchase.

It suits individuals who want to try a topic without spending anything, or small teams with no training budget. It is built around individual learners, so check what rollout and reporting you need before relying on it for a whole organisation.

## Think Productive: Best for practical workshops on everyday pain points

Think Productive, the team behind the Productivity Ninja approach, runs workshops on the problems that eat time: email, meetings, hybrid working and focus. Sessions range from two-hour topics to a full-day programme, and its workshops can be delivered privately for your organisation. Participants also get access to the Productivity Ninja Academy afterwards, to help the learning stick.

It suits teams with a clear, specific frustration and a wish for an interactive session. Like other facilitated formats, it reaches people in groups, so plan how you will keep the momentum going after the day.

## Microsoft Learn: Best for mastering the tools you already use

Productivity is partly a question of tools, and Microsoft Learn is the official place to answer it. It offers free, self-paced modules and learning paths, including a path for getting started with Microsoft 365 Copilot across Word, Excel, Outlook, Teams and more.

It suits organisations rolling out Microsoft 365 or Copilot that want everyone confident with the software. What it teaches is how to use the tools, rather than the human skills behind focus, planning and communication, so many teams pair it with something that covers those.

## How to pick the right productivity training for your team

Start with people, not the programme. Ask who your employee productivity training is for: a handful of managers, one department, or everyone. The answer decides whether you need a cohort-based workshop, a credential, or something that reaches the whole organisation.

Next, decide between a one-off and something ongoing. A workshop can start a conversation, but habits need repetition, so ask what keeps people practising once the session has finished.

Then think about measurement: do people find it useful enough to come back to? From there, look at the numbers you can report, such as how many people have started, how often they return and where engagement is growing. Be wary of any provider that promises ROI figures without showing how they were reached.

Finally, the budget. Free options such as Alison and Microsoft Learn cost nothing to start. Per-seat plans scale with headcount, and workshops are usually priced per session or per participant. Weigh the price against how many people will actually use it, because the cheapest option is poor value if nobody opens it. And if one team needs an energetic half-day on email and meetings, a workshop will do the job.

## Turning training into lasting productivity

The training that moves the needle is the kind people keep using. That is a matter of habit as much as knowledge: a UCL study found a new behaviour took 66 days on average to become automatic, so a single course cannot do the job alone.

Research on the skills themselves points the same way. In an MIT Sloan study of garment factories in Bangalore, a 12-month programme covering communication and time and stress management returned 256% on investment within eight months of finishing, mostly through higher productivity. It was one setting and a long programme, so read it as evidence that these skills can be taught and matter, not as a promise for your organisation.

So build training into the workday, keep it going beyond launch week, and watch whether people come back to it. Good habits are rarely built in a day, but teams that are given the time and support to keep going tend to get there.

## Workplace productivity training: FAQs

## What is workplace productivity training?

Workplace productivity training helps people get more of the right work done. It covers hard skills, such as using Excel, Copilot or project management tools well, and human skills, such as focus, prioritising, clear communication and managing energy. Tools matter, but habits decide whether they get used. That is where GoodHabitz excels: it develops the human skills behind productivity across the whole team, with a route to building human skills online.

## Does productivity training actually work?

Yes, when it leads to behaviour change and is measured. As a one-off, it tends to fade, because new habits take time to form. The training that works brings people back to practise, sits inside the workday and shows you who is using it, as the Securitas UK rollout did. Look for adoption data and a plan for what happens after the first session.

## Is online or in-person productivity training better?

It depends on the need. Online, continuous learning suits whole teams in large, multinational organisations: people learn at their own pace, in their own language, and the learning carries on after launch. In-person workshops suit facilitated cohorts, such as a leadership team working through a shared challenge. Many organisations combine the two, using a workshop to start the conversation and continuous learning to keep it going.

## What is the best productivity training for managers in large corporations?

Managers need skills that multiply across a team: prioritising, delegating, running good meetings and spotting early signs of overload. Those habits spread: Gallup found that managers account for at least 70% of the variance in employee engagement. In a large corporation, choose something that reaches every manager, not a nominated few. GoodHabitz fits when you want continuous development at scale, while a facilitated programme suits a single leadership cohort. Our guide to manager burnout covers the warning signs.

## What is the best productivity training for employees in multinational companies?

Multinationals need consistency without a one-size-fits-all feel. Look for productivity training for employees that is available in their own languages, fits around their working day and can be reported on across countries. GoodHabitz is built for that kind of rollout, and Microsoft Learn adds free tool training alongside it. Pairing it with stress management training helps protect the energy productivity depends on.
