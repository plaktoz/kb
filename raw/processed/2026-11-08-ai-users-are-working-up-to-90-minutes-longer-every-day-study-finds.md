# AI Users Are Working Up to 90 Minutes Longer Every Day, Study Finds

A new analysis found that people who use AI at work have longer workdays than those who don't.

AI proponents like Bill Gates and Jensen Huang have suggested that AI could one day lead to [shorter workweeks](https://fortune.com/article/zoom-ceo-eric-yuan-agrees-with-bill-gates-jensen-huang-jamie-dimon-3-day-workweek-coming-soon/).

Even critics like Sen. Bernie Sanders have renewed calls for a 32-hour workweek as AI threatens to transform the labor market.

"At a time when artificial intelligence and robotics will radically transform our economy, it is imperative that the financial gains from this new technology benefit working families, not just a handful of billionaires and corporate CEOs. One important way to do that is through a 32-hour workweek with no loss in pay or benefits," Sanders [said](https://takano.house.gov/newsroom/press-releases/rep-takano-sen-sanders-reintroduce-32-hour-workweek-act) in September.

But for now, new research suggests people who use AI work longer days than those who don't.

A new analysis published last week by workplace productivity software company ActivTrak found that AI users work up to 90 minutes longer each day than non-users.

The analysis examined digital work activity from more than 9,000 employees at 467 companies between April and September. It found that AI users' workdays spanned an average of 8.4 to 9.1 hours, compared with 7.6 hours for non-users.

Heavy AI users, defined as employees who use AI for more than 45 minutes across at least five sessions a day, also spent an additional 18 minutes working after hours compared with non-users. They also switched between applications more frequently than non-users and had shorter periods of uninterrupted work than light AI users.

"AI may help employees complete tasks faster, but our research shows it contributes to longer workdays, more application switching and shorter focus sessions," Gabriela Mauch, chief customer officer and head of the ActivTrak Productivity Lab, said in a [press release](https://www.activtrak.com/news/productivity-lab-ai-users-longer-workdays/).

There are a few ways to interpret the findings. It could be that employees who were already working longer hours are simply more likely to adopt AI. Or that AI is actually encouraging people to take on more work. Or that sorting through AI slop takes extra time.

"We're in an extremely productivity-driven economy, where if we get some, we want more," Mauch also [told Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/news/newsletters/2026-11-02/ai-is-making-employees-work-longer-days-new-data-shows).

Separate research published in February also suggests that AI can lead employees to take on more work.

Researchers at the Haas School of Business at the University of California, Berkeley, conducted an eight-month ethnographic study at a tech company with 200 employees. They found that AI wasn't freeing up employees' time. Instead, it was making their work more intense.

"...Employees worked at a faster pace, took on a broader scope of tasks, and extended work into more hours of the day, often without being asked to do so," the researchers wrote.

Xingqi Maggie Ye, one of the study's authors, warned that while this might look like a productivity boost for employers in the short term, it could be difficult to sustain over time.

"As task scope expands and multiple AI-assisted workflows run in parallel, the workday becomes denser and more cognitively demanding. Because this expansion often feels self-driven and even exciting at first, expectations can gradually reset, and what was once extra effort becomes standard performance," Ye said in an [interview](https://newsroom.haas.berkeley.edu/ai-promised-to-free-up-workers-time-uc-berkeley-haas-researchers-found-the-opposite/?trk=public_post_comment-text) with the business school.
