# South Korean Workers Spend approximately $2.1 Billion a Year on AI, but Productivity Stays Flat

South Korean workers are spending about 2.8 trillion won (approximately $2.1 billion) a year on generative AI, yet corporate productivity indicators show no clear improvement. Work hours have fallen noticeably, but the path from those savings to higher organizational output is not yet working properly, the analysis finds.

According to the Bank of Korea's report "BOK Issue Note: AI Diffusion and Productivity Effects: Wider, More Efficient, but Still Shallow," released on the 17th, the average work-time reduction rate among workers who used AI on the job last week expanded 2.4-fold, from 3.8% last year to 9.2% this year. Converted to a 40-hour workweek, that means saved time rose from 1.5 hours to 3.7 hours. The share of all tasks with a time-savings rate exceeding 20% also jumped from 4.7% to 17.4%.

The survey was conducted from August to September on 16,342 employed people aged 18 to 64, and panel data was built by re-surveying 2,024 respondents from last year. The work-related AI usage rate rose 14.7 percentage points, from 51.8% to 66.5%. That is the highest among major comparison countries, including the U.S. (43.0%), and about double the average of the U.S. and six European countries (33.7%). The combined usage rate for work and non-work purposes was 81.1%, far exceeding the internet usage rate at a comparable point after its commercialization. Average weekly AI usage time among AI users increased 36%, from 5.9 hours to 8.0 hours, equivalent to 22.4% of actual weekly working hours.

A significant share of the cost burden has also been shifted onto individuals. Among workers who use AI for work, 28.4% were paid subscribers paying out of their own pockets, with average monthly spending of 49,000 won (approximately $37) (median 29,000 won). On that basis, the estimated annual AI usage fees borne by all workers come to about 2.8 trillion won (approximately $2.1 billion). The potential value of the saved work hours, converted into money, was estimated at about 61.1 trillion won (approximately $45.6 billion).

The problem is converting this into performance. The correlation coefficient between the time-savings rate and the increase in work throughput was just 0.08. That is higher than last year's 0.00 but still low, and the correlation coefficient with the quality-improvement rate stayed at 0.09. The researchers said this does not mean there is no increase in throughput at all, but explained that if the assigned workload stays the same even when work is finished faster, output may not rise much. They noted that linking individual efficiency gains to organizational performance requires improved work processes, changes to organizational structure, and workforce redeployment.

The potential output-boosting effect, assuming all saved time is reinvested in productive activity, grew from 1.0% to 2.6%. However, this does not mean an actual increase in South Korea's GDP growth rate.

Where the saved time goes was also often not directly tied to performance. Based on first-choice responses, focusing on core tasks (33.8%) and handling additional work (29.3%) accounted for 63.1%, followed by learning and self-development at 13.2%. On the other hand, 23.7% of respondents said the time was used for non-work purposes — leisure at work (10.0%), personal errands (6.3%), simply letting it pass (5.1%), and leaving work early (2.3%) — roughly one in four.

The depth of AI use also remains shallow. Among workers who handled tasks last week, those with a higher share of core tasks were less likely to use AI. Even in job categories where AI could replace a substantial portion of the work, such as managerial, professional, and clerical roles, actual usage fell far short of what is technically possible. However, when workers with a high share of core tasks did use AI, the quality-improvement rate was relatively high. The interpretation is that in core tasks, AI's effects are more likely to show up in the quality of output than in speed or throughput.

Oh Sam-il, head of the Bank of Korea's employment research team, said, "Academia views time savings itself as a productivity gain, but total factor productivity (TFP), an observable indicator, is not increasing significantly." He explained, "For technology to realize its potential, complementary assets such as compensation systems and organizational restructuring must be in place, and because these are not yet supported, the effect of translating into performance may be slow." He added, "Cautiously, I would expect that by next year, time saved on work could approach at least a full day."

The same diagnosis has emerged in the U.S. Kevin Warsh, chair of the U.S. Federal Reserve, said in opening remarks at the Fed's "Productivity and Jobs Task Force" conference held at the Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco on the 16th (local time) that the level of productivity improvement hoped for by AI optimists has not yet materialized. U.S. productivity growth has been solid in recent quarters but has shown no clear leap, he said. In the U.S., 65% of companies that have adopted AI were found to use it for only three or fewer tasks.

The findings carry significant implications for South Korea's market, where AI investment is flowing in one direction only. That is because quantitative indicators such as usage rates and usage hours outpace major countries, but the speed of conversion into companies' real productivity has been confirmed to be slow. The researchers stressed that policy attention should focus on raising the quality of use rather than on AI diffusion itself. Workers need to develop the ability to find usage methods suited to their tasks and to verify results, while companies need to embed AI into core tasks and change how they work and how they run their organizations, they said. The researchers characterized the results as a transitional snapshot of the AI transformation and said it is necessary to keep checking whether individual efficiency gains translate into organizational productivity with a time lag.
