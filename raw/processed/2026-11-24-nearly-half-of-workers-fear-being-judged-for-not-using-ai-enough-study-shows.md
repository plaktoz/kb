# Nearly Half of Workers Fear Being Judged for Not Using AI Enough, Study Shows

Nearly half of employees worldwide fear being judged for not using artificial intelligence enough at work, more than twice the proportion worried about being judged for using it. New research reveals growing pressure to demonstrate AI proficiency, uneven access to training and the risk that productivity gains are simply creating more work.

Nearly half of employees (48%) worry they will be judged for not using artificial intelligence (AI) enough at work, according to new global research revealing how rapidly workplace expectations are changing. The findings from The Inclusion Initiative at the London School of Economics and Political Science (LSE), in collaboration with Protiviti, suggest that employees increasingly believe AI proficiency is becoming an important measure of their professional capability.

By comparison, just 20% worry about being judged for using AI. The findings represent a significant change in how employees perceive the technology. When generative AI first entered workplaces, concerns frequently centred on whether using it might be viewed as cheating, cutting corners or avoiding work. Now, many employees appear more concerned about being perceived as falling behind colleagues who embrace the technology.

However, the research also highlights a striking contradiction. While 74% of surveyed employees now use AI at work, compared with 29% in 2024, greater AI adoption does not necessarily translate into higher perceived job performance. And although employees using AI report saving an average of 19% of their working week, almost three-quarters of those time savings are being reinvested into additional work.

The findings raise important questions about workplace culture, productivity and whether employers are placing increasing pressure on employees to use AI without clearly defining what effective adoption should look like.

## WORKERS INCREASINGLY FEAR BEING LEFT BEHIND BY AI

The third annual GENERATIONS report, Beyond the Technology: Who Really Captures AI’s Gains at Work, surveyed 3,001 professional employees globally between March and May 2026. It found that 48% were concerned about being judged for not keeping up with AI, while 20% worried about negative perceptions associated with using the technology.

The difference suggests that AI is becoming more closely associated with professional credibility. Employees may increasingly believe they must demonstrate familiarity with AI tools to be viewed as productive, adaptable or valuable. However, the report cautions that there is no universal benchmark for appropriate AI use. An employee working in research or data analysis may benefit from using AI frequently, while another professional may have responsibilities where its use is limited or inappropriate.

The question is therefore not simply how often employees use AI, but whether the technology adds meaningful value to their work. The findings also connect with Fair Play Talks‘ previous coverage revealing that half of Gen Z workers felt guilty using AI, while more than four in 10 concealed their use from employers.

That separate research highlighted the contradictory expectations some employees face: develop AI skills to remain competitive, but navigate unclear rules about when using those capabilities is considered acceptable. Together, the findings suggest that employers need to establish clearer expectations about responsible and appropriate AI use.

## MILLENNIALS FACE THE GREATEST PRESSURE TO KEEP UP

The LSE–Protiviti research found that concerns about being judged for insufficient AI use were evident across all generations. However, the proportion varied:

51% of millennials worried about being judged for not using AI enough.

47% of Gen Z employees shared this concern.

47% of Gen X employees were similarly worried.

35% of baby boomers and older employees expressed concern.

Interestingly, younger employees were more likely to worry about being judged for using AI. Some 27% of Gen Z employees reported this concern, compared with 20% of millennials, 15% of Gen X and 13% of baby boomers. These findings suggest younger employees may be navigating two competing expectations. They may feel pressure to demonstrate AI proficiency while also worrying that relying on the technology could undermine perceptions of their independent capabilities.

For employees at the beginning of their careers, the distinction may be particularly important. Developing professional judgement, problem-solving abilities and technical knowledge requires experience. AI may support that development, but employees also need opportunities to build their own expertise. Employers should therefore avoid assuming that younger workers are automatically more confident or comfortable with AI simply because they are familiar with digital technology.

## AI USE HAS SURGED FROM 29% TO 74%

The research demonstrates how quickly AI has become embedded in professional work. In 2024, just 29% of employees surveyed reported using AI at work. That proportion rose to 70% in 2025 and reached 74% in 2026. The latest findings suggest that many organisations are moving beyond the initial phase of AI experimentation. However, widespread adoption does not necessarily mean employees are using the technology effectively or consistently.

Some workers may use AI for relatively simple activities, while others integrate it into more complex workflows. The research also identifies differences in the proportion of daily tasks employees complete using AI. Among people who used AI in both the 2025 and 2026 survey waves, average use increased from 27% to 29% of day-to-day tasks. The findings indicate that adoption is increasingly established, although the intensity and effectiveness of use vary.

For organisations, this shifts the challenge from encouraging experimentation to understanding where AI genuinely improves the quality and efficiency of work. That requires clear guidance, relevant training and appropriate human oversight.

## AI SAVES TIME BUT MOST OF IT GOES BACK INTO WORK

One of the most important findings concerns how employees use the time they save through AI. Among employees using the technology, the average reported time saving was 19% of their working week. For someone working a conventional five-day week, that is approaching the equivalent of one working day. However, employees reported reinvesting 72% of the time saved into additional work.

The findings suggest that AI may be changing the volume and type of work employees undertake rather than necessarily reducing their workloads. Some 82% of AI users said the technology helped them complete tasks more quickly. Other reported benefits included:

70% said AI reduced mental effort.

63% said it helped automate manual tasks.

62% said it improved the quality of their work.

Among employees reporting time savings, 65% said they spent at least moderately more time on higher-value activities, while 50% said they used saved time to take on new responsibilities or projects. These outcomes can be positive where employees have opportunities to undertake more meaningful or rewarding work.

However, there is also a risk that efficiency gains simply become the justification for increasing expectations. Dr Grace Lordan, Founding Director of The Inclusion Initiative at LSE, highlighted the importance of employers deciding how AI-generated capacity should be used. “Where those time savings go matters as much as the size of the savings,” she warned.

Without clear direction, employees may find themselves expected to deliver more simply because technology enables them to complete some tasks more quickly.

## THE HIDDEN COST OF WORKLOAD CREEP

The researchers describe this emerging challenge as workload creep. As AI makes certain activities more efficient, organisations may increase output expectations, introduce additional responsibilities or expand workloads without fully considering the impact on employees. This can create an uncomfortable situation. Employees may be working more efficiently while feeling that the overall demands of their jobs remain unchanged or have increased.

The findings reinforce a recent study revealing that 46% of surveyed AI users faced rising workload expectations. That separate Hubstaff research also found that 45% spent at least an hour each week correcting colleagues’ AI-generated work, highlighting additional tasks that may be overlooked when organisations assess productivity. Similarly, a previous study found that seven in 10 workers felt more productive with AI, yet workloads were not falling. The latest LSE–Protiviti findings add further evidence that AI-driven time savings do not automatically translate into reduced working pressure.

However, the study does not demonstrate that every additional task is harmful or that workload creep is occurring equally across organisations. The wider question is whether employers are making deliberate decisions about how productivity gains should benefit both the business and its employees.

Matt Duncan, Managing Director at Protiviti, emphasised that organisations should consider how AI-created capacity can support higher-value work, innovation and better outcomes. The opportunity, he argued, extends beyond simply completing more tasks at greater speed.

## AI TRAINING IS LINKED TO HIGHER ADOPTION

The research found a particularly strong relationship between AI training and technology use. Among employees who had received AI training, 93% reported using AI at work. That compared with just 50% of employees who had not received training. Training was also associated with greater reported time savings. Employees who had received training reported saving an average of 22% of their working hours through AI, compared with 13% among those without training. The differences suggest that organisations providing relevant training may be better positioned to help employees use AI effectively. However, these are observational associations.

The research does not prove that training alone caused the differences, because employees receiving training may also work in organisations that have invested more heavily in AI tools and infrastructure. Nevertheless, the findings reinforce the importance of developing employee capabilities alongside technology adoption.

As Fair Play Talks previously reported, many employers are preparing employees to use today’s AI tools without adequately planning for the jobs those technologies will create or transform. The challenge is therefore not simply offering training, but ensuring it prepares employees for changing responsibilities and longer-term careers.

## TRAINING QUALITY MATTERS

The LSE–Protiviti report also found that the usefulness of training has a substantial association with employee confidence. Among employees who considered their AI training beneficial, 92% felt confident using the technology. That compared with 74% of those who had received training but did not consider it beneficial.

Among employees who had received no training, 66% felt confident using AI. The findings suggest that completing an AI course does not automatically translate into practical confidence. Training needs to be relevant to employees’ responsibilities and the tasks they perform. Generic introductions may provide useful awareness but fail to address the specific challenges employees encounter.

Organisations may therefore need to offer practical learning opportunities, opportunities to experiment and guidance on using AI appropriately within particular roles. The report also found that employees whose organisations had standardised AI workflows reported saving 27% of their working week, compared with 14% among those without such workflows. The difference reinforces the importance of organisational support and clear working practices.

## OLDER EMPLOYEES LESS LIKELY TO RECEIVE AI TRAINING

The research identifies important differences in access to learning opportunities across age groups. Some 66% of Gen Z employees had received AI training during the previous year, compared with 40% of baby boomers and older employees. The difference was greater for employer-provided training. Some 37% of Gen Z employees had received company-provided training, compared with just 19% of baby boomers.

These findings raise questions about whether organisations are providing equitable access to the skills required for changing workplaces. Older employees may bring extensive industry knowledge, professional judgement and experience that can complement AI capabilities. However, they may have fewer opportunities to develop those capabilities if employers concentrate training on younger staff.

The report cautions against assuming that generational differences automatically reflect age-related ability or willingness to learn. Access, confidence, workplace opportunities and the relevance of training can all influence AI adoption. This is particularly important as businesses navigate ageing workforces and longer working lives. The objective should be to provide employees across all generations with the resources they need to adapt, rather than reinforcing assumptions about who is technologically capable.

## MORE AI DOESN’T ALWAYS TRANSLATE INTO BETTER PERFORMANCE

Perhaps the most important finding challenges a common assumption about technology adoption. The researchers found no consistent association between AI adoption and employees’ perceptions of how their managers rate their job performance. Nor did the study find consistent positive relationships between perceived performance and AI training, access to employer-provided AI tools or integration into standardised workflows.

Instead, higher perceived performance was more consistently associated with human capabilities and workplace conditions. These included conscientiousness, resilience, confidence in solving problems and a willingness to learn. Psychological safety also emerged as an important factor. The findings suggest that organisations should avoid using AI adoption rates as a substitute for measuring meaningful performance. An employee using AI frequently may not necessarily produce better work than someone who uses it selectively.

In some situations, AI may improve speed or quality. In others, human expertise, independent analysis or careful professional judgement may be more appropriate. This connects with an earlier study revealing that six in 10 workers worried AI was weakening their skills.

That separate research highlighted the importance of maintaining critical thinking and the ability to challenge AI-generated information. It also reinforces Fair Play Talks’ investigation showing that 42% of surveyed US workers using AI had accepted answers they suspected were wrong. Together, the findings suggest that responsible AI use requires more than simply encouraging employees to use the technology more often.

## PSYCHOLOGICAL SAFETY IS CENTRAL TO AI ADOPTION

The research also identifies a relationship between workplace culture and employees’ attitudes towards AI. Workers who felt they could speak up, ask questions and acknowledge uncertainty were more likely to report positive perceptions of their own performance. Among employees in the highest third for psychological safety, 89% rated their performance highly, compared with 66% in the lowest third. Both psychological safety and performance were self-reported, so the figures should not be interpreted as evidence that one directly caused the other.

Nevertheless, the findings suggest that workplace environments may play an important role in helping employees adapt to technological change. AI-specific concerns, including fear of being judged for using too much or too little AI, were associated with lower psychological safety. The strongest negative association involved workplaces where employees believed colleagues were judging how much AI they used.For employers, this raises important questions about management behaviour. If employees worry that experimenting with AI could damage their reputation, they may conceal their use or avoid seeking help. Conversely, workers who fear being perceived as technologically behind may feel compelled to use AI even when it does not improve their work. Neither outcome necessarily supports responsible or effective adoption.

As Fair Play Talks previously reported, nearly half of surveyed companies lacked clear leadership responsibility for coordinating how employees and AI work together. That separate research highlighted the risks associated with introducing new technology without sufficient workforce planning or management accountability.

## LEADERSHIP TRANSPARENCY CAN REDUCE UNCERTAINTY

The findings also highlight the importance of communication. Employees need to understand which AI tools are appropriate, how they should be used and what the organisation expects. Without clear guidance, staff may draw their own conclusions about how AI adoption influences performance assessments, career opportunities and job security. That uncertainty can create unnecessary pressure.As Fair Play Talks previously reported, four in 10 employees believed CEOs were not sufficiently transparent about how AI would affect their careers. The wider issue is not simply communicating a technology strategy. It is ensuring employees understand how their work may change, which skills will become more important and how they will be supported. Clear expectations may also reduce the risk of employees feeling obliged to demonstrate AI use simply to prove they are keeping pace.

## WHAT CAN EMPLOYERS DO TO REDUCE AI-RELATED PRESSURE?

The findings suggest that organisations need to move beyond encouraging widespread adoption and focus on creating the conditions for effective, responsible use.

1. Define what good AI use looks like. Explain where AI adds value, which tasks require independent human judgement and when using AI may be inappropriate.

2. Avoid rewarding AI usage for its own sake. Evaluate the quality, accuracy and outcomes of employees’ work rather than the frequency with which they use technology.

3. Provide relevant training. Offer practical, role-specific learning opportunities that help employees develop confidence and apply AI appropriately.

4. Ensure training is inclusive. Review access across generations, job levels and working arrangements rather than assuming some groups are naturally better equipped to adapt.

5. Protect psychological safety. Encourage employees to ask questions, discuss mistakes and acknowledge uncertainty without fear of unfair judgement.

6. Review workload expectations. Assess whether AI-generated time savings are being used for higher-value work, professional development or wellbeing — or simply creating additional demands.

7. Maintain human oversight. Ensure employees understand their responsibilities for checking AI-generated information and identifying errors.

8. Communicate openly about career impacts. Explain how AI may change roles, skills and progression opportunities, and provide realistic pathways for development.

9. Involve employees in decisions. Workers can provide valuable insight into which processes benefit from AI and which create additional work or risk.

10. Measure meaningful outcomes. Examine work quality, employee experience, effectiveness, development and sustainable performance alongside adoption and efficiency.

These measures can help organisations support AI adoption without creating unnecessary anxiety or competition.

## HOW CAN EMPLOYEES BUILD CONFIDENCE WITH AI?

Employees may also benefit from approaching AI development as a practical learning process rather than a competition. Identifying a small number of relevant tasks can provide an opportunity to understand where AI genuinely improves work.

Employees should also consider the limitations of the tools they use and ensure that important outputs are checked appropriately. Asking for guidance, participating in relevant training and discussing expectations with managers can help reduce uncertainty.

However, employees should not feel that successful professional development requires using AI in every situation. Human judgement, industry knowledge, communication and problem-solving remain important capabilities. The objective should be to build confidence in deciding when AI is useful, how to apply it responsibly and when independent expertise is essential.

## USING AI WELL – NOT JUST MORE

The LSE–Protiviti findings reveal how rapidly workplace expectations around artificial intelligence are changing. Nearly half of professional employees worry about being judged for insufficient AI use, while adoption has increased substantially over the past three years. Employees using the technology report meaningful time savings, but much of that capacity is being absorbed into additional work.

Meanwhile, access to training remains uneven, and greater AI use does not consistently correspond with stronger perceived job performance. The findings suggest organisations need to reconsider how they define successful AI adoption.

Encouraging employees to use new tools may help increase familiarity, but it does not necessarily improve the quality of work or support employee wellbeing. For employers, the challenge is to provide clear expectations, relevant training and workplace environments where employees feel able to learn without fear of judgement.

It also means ensuring that AI-created capacity is used deliberately rather than automatically increasing workloads. Ultimately, the organisations that benefit most from AI may not be those where employees use it most frequently, but those that create the conditions for people to use it effectively, confidently and responsibly.
