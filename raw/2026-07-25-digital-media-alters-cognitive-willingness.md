# Digital Media Alters Cognitive Willingness

source_url: https://neurosciencenews.com/digital-media-cognitive-willingness-31099

---

Neuroscience News, source: Estonian Research Council

A new scientific framework proposes that digital media usage alters human focus through a mechanism called "effort recalibration." Rather than destroying raw attentional capacity, continuous exposure to immediate, low-effort rewards (infinite scroll, algorithmic feeds, notifications) reshapes the brain's internal cost-benefit valuation of cognitive effort — shifting the balance from deep task engagement ("exploitation") to perpetual novelty-seeking ("exploration").

"The central idea of our paper is that repeated exposure to immediate, low-effort rewards may change how valuable our brain perceives cognitive effort to be. As a result, we may increasingly prefer activities that provide immediate gratification, even when dedicating ourselves to a more demanding task would be more beneficial in the long run," said Jaan Aru, Associate Professor of Computational Neuroscience and AI at the University of Tartu.

The framework offers a possible explanation for a long-standing puzzle: if digital media really harm cognition, why do laboratory studies of attention and memory show so little evidence of it? According to Aru, the key question isn't whether people can concentrate under controlled, high-motivation lab conditions — they generally can — but whether they remain willing to invest sustained effort in real-world tasks over long, unconstrained periods, like studying or deep reading.

Exploration (seeking new information) and exploitation (sustained engagement with existing knowledge to build mastery) are both essential learning strategies, but acquiring complex skills ultimately requires prolonged focus on a single task. The authors argue digital media use may gradually shift this balance toward continuous exploration, making the sustained engagement school learning requires increasingly difficult.

The researchers call for experimental, neurobiological, and longitudinal studies to test the framework and better understand how digital environments shape learning habits and sustained attention over time. The paper, "An effort recalibration framework for digital media use and cognition" by Wisnu Wiradhany, Douglas Parry, and Jaan Aru, appears in Nature Human Behaviour (DOI: 10.1038/s41562-026-02500-w).
