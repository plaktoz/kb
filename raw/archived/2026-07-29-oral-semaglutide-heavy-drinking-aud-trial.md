# Can Oral Semaglutide Reduce Heavy Drinking in Alcohol Use Disorder?

Oral semaglutide significantly reduced heavy drinking days, drinking intensity, craving, and alcohol-related consequences among treatment-seeking adults with moderate to severe alcohol use disorder (AUD) in a phase 2 randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled trial published in the American Journal of Psychiatry. However, the drug missed its primary endpoint — a lab-based measure of cue-elicited craving — and did not significantly reduce drinks per calendar day, one of two key secondary endpoints.

Investigators at the University of Colorado Anschutz Medical Campus randomized 50 adults with DSM-5-defined moderate or severe AUD to oral semaglutide (3 mg/day for 4 weeks, then 7 mg/day for 4 weeks) or placebo for 8 weeks. The trial paired an in-clinic alcohol cue-reactivity assessment with naturalistic drinking measures via Timeline Follow-Back interviews. 47 of 50 participants (94%) completed treatment, with high medication adherence verified through urinary riboflavin, video-monitored dosing, and plasma drug concentrations.

Semaglutide did not significantly separate from placebo on cue-elicited craving (the primary endpoint) or drinks per calendar day, but it did significantly reduce heavy drinking days per week relative to placebo. It also showed benefit across secondary and exploratory outcomes during the last 4 weeks of treatment: drinks per drinking day decreased significantly, naturalistic craving (Penn Alcohol Craving Scale) decreased significantly, alcohol-related negative consequences (PROMIS) decreased at a significantly greater rate than placebo, and among baseline cannabis users, semaglutide significantly reduced cannabis use days. Semaglutide-treated participants were also significantly more likely to drop their WHO risk drinking level by a full category or more.

Effect sizes for heavy drinking days and drinks per drinking day were medium to large, which the study authors noted may exceed effects reported for currently approved AUD medications. Lead author Joseph Schacht, PhD, said the results "suggest oral semaglutide may help reduce heavy and harmful drinking, even in people who are not trying to quit alcohol entirely," and could help patients who haven't responded to existing therapies.

Adverse events were mostly mild and occurred at similar rates in both groups, though events "definitely" related to study medication were more common with semaglutide; one participant discontinued due to a rash. The two groups did not differ significantly in weight change over the 8 weeks.

The trial was conducted at a single site with a modest sample (N=50), and the authors describe the results as hypothesis-generating rather than confirmatory; larger, longer-term trials are planned. The sample was 94% White and, by design, entirely overweight or obese, limiting generalizability. No biological marker of alcohol consumption (e.g., phosphatidylethanol) was used to corroborate self-reported drinking, and placebo-group participants were more likely to correctly guess their group assignment, raising the possibility of functional unblinding.
