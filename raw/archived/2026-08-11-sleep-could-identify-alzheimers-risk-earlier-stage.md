# Sleep could help identify people at risk of developing Alzheimer's disease at an earlier stage

A team from the University of Liège (GIGA Neurosciences), supported by the Stop Alzheimer's Foundation, analysed sleep patterns of more than 500 healthy people — mostly young adults (18-31) but also older participants (50-69). Using each participant's polygenic risk score (a single figure summarizing genetic influence on Alzheimer's probability, which remains low and cannot itself predict disease), researchers correlated genetic risk with sleep characteristics.

**Finding**: Among middle-aged/older participants, a higher frequency of nocturnal micro-awakenings — brief periods of brain activity that don't fully wake the sleeper but disrupt the sleep cycle — was associated with greater genetic risk of Alzheimer's. No such link was found in younger participants. "These micro-awakenings are therefore not insignificant," says Puneet Talwar (GIGA ULiège). "Certain profiles could promote the accumulation of proteins involved in Alzheimer's disease and be associated with increased vulnerability."

**Mechanism**: The research points to the locus coeruleus, a brainstem region about the size of a grain of rice involved in wakefulness, attention, and sleep regulation. Gilles Vandewalle (GIGA CRC In Vivo Imaging) notes it's one of the first regions where abnormal protein deposits appear, sometimes as early as adolescence. A 2025 preliminary study using a 7-Tesla MRI scanner showed sleep quality (falling-asleep speed, depth) was linked to brainstem state from a young age, and locus coeruleus function depended on REM sleep quality.

**Implications**: "Sleep could become an accessible marker for the early identification of vulnerable individuals," Vandewalle says. Improving sleep quality could also be a lever to prevent or slow disease progression in genetically predisposed individuals. Lucie Leroux (Stop Alzheimer's Foundation): "This research shows that sleep is not only an indicator of health, but also a potential lever for intervention."

Alzheimer's affects more than 220,000 people in Belgium. The findings are statistical associations requiring confirmation and cannot yet predict the disease in an individual, but highlight the value of basic research toward earlier anticipation of the disease. Published in the journal *SLEEP* (article title: "Sleep arousals are associated with the polygenic risk for developing Alzheimer's disease and with cognitive change in healthy late middle-aged individuals").
