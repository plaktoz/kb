# When Time Management Was Easy

In 1973, Alan Lakein published *How to Get Control of Your Time and Your Life*, arguably the first book to describe time management in a recognizably modern way — with a focus on personalized tools like daily to-do lists. It sold more than three million copies and was cited by Bill Clinton as an influence on his early career.

Lakein's core system: write everything you need to do on a single task list, then label each task "A" (important and urgent), "B" (important but not urgent), or "C" (small, easy, doesn't need attention now). You complete the A tasks first, then B, then C, and priorities can shift as deadlines approach.

What's shocking about revisiting this system today is its finitude. A single list that could hold everything you needed to do is hard to imagine now — an email inbox alone can produce hundreds of items at any moment. Lakein also assumed task stability: that your list would stay roughly the same as you worked through the day. Modern work instead brings constant new demands — chats, meeting invitations, requests to "jump on a call" — that require immediate answers.

Newport argues that the fact modern workflows would swamp Lakein's simple system says more about how work has changed than about the system's quality. Having more work, arriving with more urgency, than anyone can realistically manage isn't a recipe for effective work — it's a recipe for burnout.

Newport notes he describes complicated time-management strategies "with reluctance": his bigger goal, discussed in his book *Slow Productivity*, is to reform office work so that something as simple as Lakein's ABC system becomes sufficient again. "We're not there yet, but in the meantime, it helps to realize where we are now isn't working."
