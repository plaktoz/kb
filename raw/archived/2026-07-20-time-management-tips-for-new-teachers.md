# Time Management Tips for New Teachers

Teachers juggle teaching with the administration of school life, and it's easy to become overwhelmed. Two actionable strategies, drawn from wider productivity advice, can reduce the frenzy.

**Practice Full Capture**: A startlingly simple insight from David Allen's *Getting Things Done*. The brain hates open loops — it can't tell the difference between doing something and trusting it's stored somewhere safe, so writing a task down is, to the brain, the same as doing it. Have one trusted system (notebook, digital task manager like Todoist or Trello, planner, index cards) to capture every task or idea the moment it arises. Time-sensitive items go straight into a digital calendar; everything else into a task manager. Review the capture system daily (10-30 minutes).

**Treat Every Task Like a Lesson (Multi-Scale Planning)**: Adapted from Cal Newport's *Deep Work* and his blog *Study Hacks*, this works across three time scales:
- **Semester plan**: At the start of each term, sketch commitments and what success looks like by term's end.
- **Weekly plan**: Each week, identify deadlines and how to make progress on semester goals; carve out calendar time in advance.
- **Daily plan**: Each morning, give every minute a job — add meetings, lessons, and pre-allocated task time from the weekly plan. What's left is free time; use it deliberately rather than defaulting to reactivity (email-checking).

Treat non-teaching tasks (marking, planning, reports) like lessons: specify a time, then work with total focus, just as you would when teaching — this is what Newport calls "timeblocking." Crises will pull you away; when that happens, just update the plan and sketch a new block. Timeblocking isn't martyrdom — allocate time for lunch, breaks, and reading; the goal is being intentionally strategic, not working every minute.

The daily plan feeds from the weekly plan, which feeds from the semester plan, all underpinned by the trusted capture system. Nothing gets overlooked; everything gets worked on — it doesn't reduce the workload, but it can make teachers feel a lot less busy.
