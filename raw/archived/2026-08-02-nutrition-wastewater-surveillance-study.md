# 'Peering into the black box of nutrition': Study tracks diets

Technology originally used to track COVID-19 in municipal wastewater is now being repurposed to track population-level dietary habits, according to a new paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences. Dr. Rachel Noble at UNC Chapel Hill's Institute of Marine Sciences, working with Duke University colleagues, developed FoodSeq-FLOW ("Food Landscape Observation in Water") — a DNA-sequencing method that identifies plant and animal species, down to the specific species eaten, from wastewater samples. Researchers gathered data from 21 wastewater treatment plants across North Carolina, covering more than 2 million people, at a cost of less than a cent per person.

Unlike traditional dietary questionnaires — which are time-consuming, costly, often inaccurate, and hard to use with children — wastewater surveillance captures everyone connected to the sewage system regardless of demographics, and does not depend on self-report. Noble said the approach could improve nutrition policy, particularly in food deserts where fresh produce access is limited, and that food-scarcity issues in North Carolina cut across race and demographics.

Limitations: sewage can be contaminated with environmental/industrial runoff, DNA can degrade under varying pipe conditions, and people on septic systems (common in rural areas already underrepresented in nutrition research) aren't captured. Seasonal tourism can also mix visitor and resident waste in coastal towns. Noble's next goal is a multi-year, statewide study to capture seasonal dietary patterns, since current data are single snapshots rather than year-round trends.
