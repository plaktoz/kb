# Staff Work, Part 3: Staff Work Is Everyone's Problem

source_url: https://zixianchen.com/blog/staff-work-is-everyones-problem

---

This is the third of three posts on staff work — the first two cover doing the work and preparing the package. This one argues that staff work isn't just an officer's problem, because decisions depend on everyone who touches the work.

Staff work means the thinking, analysis, and communication that help someone understand an issue and make a decision. An officer may produce the first draft, but everyone who comments, rewrites, or clears it becomes part of the work — nobody can clear a bad paper and later claim they had nothing to do with it. Both sides have lazy shortcuts: officers can click "accept all" without studying changes; managers can rewrite the whole thing without tracked changes, or leave broad comments like "sharpen this" and expect the officer to reconstruct the full thought process. The paper may still get cleared, but nobody learns anything. Borrowing from Josh Seiden's framing, it should be outcomes over output — the corrected paper is the output, but the outcome should be a better decision and an officer who can do the next piece of work better.

## A. For officers: comments are part of the work

**Red comments can teach a lot, if you look.** A document covered in tracked changes shows what was removed, which point moved forward, where a claim was narrowed, how a recommendation became clearer — but only for someone who studies the changes rather than clicking "accept all" and moving on.

**Look at the context, not only the words.** Consider what's behind a comment: does the person know something you don't, is there a wider issue beyond the exact sentence, could the real problem be evidence, phrasing, clarity, or level of detail — while avoiding inferring an entire hidden agenda from a short remark.

**A small comment can expose a bigger problem.** A question about one statistic may mean a wider claim has no real evidence. "Can we say this?" may mean a sentence is too absolute, or the reader knows an exception that was missed. The useful fix may sit elsewhere in the document than the sentence that attracted the comment.

**Reply with the answer and the edit together**, rather than "noted, will check," which avoids a second round where the reviewer has to ask again after the answer was already found.

**The suggested words aren't always the fix.** Bosses sometimes type a proposed sentence while thinking aloud; it may solve the concern or create a new one elsewhere. The officer usually knows the detailed context better, having spent days on the work. Clarify when an interpretation would change the recommendation or send the paper in a very different direction; for smaller choices, use judgement.

**Don't go completely overboard** reopening everything for what might just be a request for a bit more detail — tweak what needs changing, stay calm, and clarify before doing a large amount of unnecessary extra work.

## B. For middle managers: you are part of the work too

**Rewriting everything is also a shortcut.** When a draft is bad and the deadline close, rewriting it yourself is often faster, but the officer then sees only the final answer without seeing how the manager got there — the objective that changed, the better angle found, the irrelevant background removed, the claim that got softened. The next paper comes back in its original form. There are times a manager genuinely needs to take over; the point is not to confuse rescuing the paper with developing the officer.

**Broad comments can be lazy.** "What's the angle?" or "please sharpen" may be enough for an experienced officer who understands the issue, but for others it's too vague — they can't tell whether the problem is the objective, evidence, structure, or wording, and rewrite blindly into a different version of the same problem. Explaining the problem clearly enough for the officer to work on it takes longer than typing a short comment, but is often the more useful investment.

**Go through the thinking when needed.** The useful lesson is often not the sentence that changed but how the manager arrived at the change — what the paper's objective is, which facts matter to the decision, where the recommendation needs more nuance. This can take longer than writing one's own version and can't be done for every draft, but if the same problem keeps recurring, another broad comment probably won't help.

**Sometimes edit it together**, explaining choices in real time — why the conclusion moved forward, why a technically correct answer was still inadequate, what the boss was actually asking. This also reveals how the officer approached the task in the first place: misunderstood assignment, unclear sense of which facts mattered, rushed work, or simply not caring enough (which requires a different conversation).

**Let the officer see the important changes** via tracked changes and comments explaining larger edits, rather than a clean rewrite that leaves the officer concluding only that the manager prefers a different writing style.

**See whether anything transfers** to the next piece of work — did the officer put the point first unprompted, check the obvious precedent, stop compiling everyone's input into one long paper. If the same problem appears a fourth time, repeating the same comment probably isn't the answer; a live edit or clearer example may work better.

**Match guidance to the officer** — some learn well from written comments, others need to talk through the issue first, others understand the principle but struggle with politically sensitive cases. Newer officers usually need more direction; give more room as judgement improves.

**Your steer can be wrong.** Managers sometimes infer what a senior person wants and guide the team accordingly, and that inference can be completely off — own the mistake, correct the work, and don't let the officer appear to have misunderstood an instruction that actually came from the manager.

## C. The decision depends on the work

A polished paper doesn't prove good staff work happened — a manager may have simply rewritten everything, or a disagreement may have been worded away instead of properly addressed. The officer owns the first draft, but not the entire responsibility for what eventually goes up; everyone who reviews, changes, or clears the paper affects the resulting decision. Officers need to care enough to learn from comments; managers need to know enough to guide them while remaining willing to learn themselves. The paper is the output — the quality of the decision, and whether the people doing the work improve, are the outcomes. That's why staff work is everyone's problem.
