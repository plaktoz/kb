# For long-term health, diet quality matters more than food processing level

source_url: https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/1136477

---

**Harvard study finds healthy diets lower risks while unhealthy diets raise them, regardless of the level of food processing**

National Harbor, Md. (July 28, 2026) — What makes a diet healthy or not? According to a new study, nutritional quality matters more than processing level when it comes to long-term health. The study of over 200,000 people found that overall diet quality was strongly associated with risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes, and death — but these associations did not substantially differ based on food processing levels.

"While many ultra-processed foods tend to be of poor nutritional quality and should generally be limited or avoided, processing itself is not inherently harmful," said Xiaowen Wang, PhD, a postdoctoral research fellow in the Department of Nutrition at Harvard T. H. Chan School of Public Health. She said individuals should "focus on what you're eating overall, not just whether it's processed. A healthy plant-based diet rich in whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, legumes and healthy plant oils is beneficial, even if it includes some processed foods." Wang presented the findings at NUTRITION 2026, the American Society for Nutrition's flagship annual meeting, held July 25-28 in National Harbor, Maryland.

The nutritional quality of a diet and the amount of ultra-processed foods consumed are often intertwined — many ultra-processed foods are high in added sugars, sodium and refined carbohydrates. But ultra-processed foods like fortified breakfast cereals, whole grain products, canned beans, or plant-based dairy can still provide important nutrients, fiber, antioxidants and other beneficial compounds.

Researchers used data from three studies that collectively followed over 200,000 U.S. adults from the 1980s through the 2020s, applying a plant-based diet index framework to assess the balance of plant versus animal foods, overall diet quality, and consumption across processing levels (using the Nova classification system). Participants were grouped into four categories:

- Healthy/ultra-processed (e.g., whole grain cereal with fortified plant-based milk, plant-based yogurt, whole-grain bread, canned fruit)
- Healthy/minimally-processed (e.g., oatmeal with fresh fruit and nuts, quinoa and vegetable salad with olive oil, homemade lentil soup)
- Unhealthy/ultra-processed (e.g., fast food, French fries, soda, packaged desserts, refined snacks)
- Unhealthy/minimally-processed (e.g., white rice, fruit juice, potatoes, homemade desserts with refined flour and sugar)

About half of study participants eventually died or developed heart disease or type 2 diabetes. Risk varied significantly by overall diet quality — healthy diets were associated with lower risk, unhealthy diets with higher risk — but within each quality category, risks were almost identical regardless of processing level.

The findings call into question using processing level as a key indicator of healthy eating, since the Nova classification does not account for nutritional value: healthy foods can be classified as ultra-processed while minimally-processed foods can still be unhealthy. "Advising people to universally 'avoid ultra-processed foods' can lead to lower diet quality since healthy ultra-processed foods are beneficial and unhealthy minimally-processed foods are detrimental," said Wang. "People should prioritize the quality of foods rather than focusing solely on the level of processing."

Wang suggested dietary guidelines should continue promoting established high-quality diets like the Mediterranean or healthy plant-based diets rather than focusing on processing level, and that encouraging food manufacturers to improve nutritional content of packaged foods could make healthy plant-based eating more convenient and accessible. The abstract was evaluated by a NUTRITION 2026 expert committee but has not undergone full peer review, so findings should be considered preliminary until a peer-reviewed publication is available.
