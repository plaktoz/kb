# Real-World Study Provides New Insights into Safety of New Alzheimer's Drug

Researchers at Duke Health studied more than 230 patients treated with lecanemab, an FDA-approved anti-amyloid therapy designed to remove amyloid protein linked to Alzheimer's disease from the brain. The study, published in Neurology Open Access on August 7, found the drug's real-world safety, treatment persistence, and effects on disease progression aligned with clinical trial results — including the most closely watched side effect, Amyloid Related Imaging Abnormalities (ARIA), which involves brain swelling or bleeding.

Corresponding author Dr. Andrew Liu said that with careful patient selection and close monitoring, approximately 80% of patients were able to remain on lecanemab for at least one year, and most treatment-related side effects resolved over time without additional medications or hospitalization.

The study followed patients with early Alzheimer's disease or mild memory impairment treated at Duke between 2023 and 2025, tracking side effects, MRI scans, cognitive testing, and treatment persistence for up to 396 days. 79% of patients who started treatment continued it; the 21% who discontinued did so mostly due to adverse events.

About one in four patients (24.3%) developed ARIA. Key findings for this group:
- Patients with a high-risk Alzheimer's gene variant (APOE ε4 homozygotes) were about four times more likely to develop ARIA, with no difference by sex.
- ARIA events could occur up to 30 weeks after starting therapy, with two peaks around 10 and 25 weeks, underscoring the need for continued monitoring.
- 31% of patients had serious adverse events during treatment, most commonly infusion reactions, falls, or strokes — though some were not necessarily attributable to the drug itself.
- ARIA status was not associated with differences in cognitive performance at 12 months.

Seven commonly available clinical, blood, and imaging tests — alone or combined — could not yet reliably predict who would develop ARIA, though one method combining an MRI vascular-change measure with blood biomarker levels (pTau/AB42) showed the most promise for identifying low-risk patients. Duke researchers also piloted an AI-supported imaging tool to detect subtle ARIA changes earlier and more consistently, and plan to expand its use.

Co-author Dr. P. Murali Doraiswamy noted that cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA) — silent amyloid buildup in brain blood vessels, present in over 50% of Alzheimer's patients — is thought to underlie ARIA, and said better tests are urgently needed to predict who will develop serious ARIA. Duke Health has now treated more than 400 patients with lecanemab, one of the most experienced centers nationally, and is building a biorepository to develop better CAA/ARIA biomarkers.

Patient Sally Osmer, diagnosed with early-stage Alzheimer's at 74, began lecanemab infusions within a month of diagnosis and has completed more than a year on the therapy without serious side effects, saying it has given her "a sense of hope" while allowing her to remain engaged with her family. Researchers emphasized that lecanemab does not cure or reverse Alzheimer's, but slowing progression can help patients maintain independence longer.
