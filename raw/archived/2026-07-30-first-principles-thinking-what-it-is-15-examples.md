# First Principles Thinking: What It Is & 15 Examples

First-principles thinking — reasoning from first principles — is used to reverse-engineer complex problems and encourage creativity. It breaks a problem down into its most basic, fundamental elements and rebuilds solutions from the ground up, rather than reasoning by analogy or convention. Popularized in modern times by Elon Musk, it has historical roots in Aristotle and military strategist John Boyd.

Musk's canonical example: sourcing a rocket cost $65 million per unit. Reasoning from first principles, he asked what a rocket is actually made of (aerospace-grade aluminum alloys, titanium, copper, carbon fiber) and found the raw materials cost about 2% of the typical price — leading him to found SpaceX to build rockets from cheaper raw materials rather than buying them at market price.

Three steps to apply it: (1) identify and challenge your assumptions about a problem, (2) separate the problem into its fundamental, non-reducible truths, and (3) recombine those truths into new solutions. Two supporting techniques: Socratic questioning (systematically clarifying your thinking, challenging assumptions, seeking evidence, considering alternative views, and examining consequences) and the Five Whys (repeatedly asking "why" to reach root causes, mirroring how children naturally reason before adulthood teaches them to accept "because I said so").

First principles thinking is contrasted with reasoning by analogy, where you improve on existing solutions rather than starting from a blank slate. Analogy works well for incremental improvement (making a competitor's product 2x better) or for horizontal transfer (the "Uber for X" pattern), while first-principles thinking is more suited to breaking out of a crowded "red ocean" market and creating a new "blue ocean" category.

Real-world examples cited: SpaceX and Tesla applying first principles to reduce launch and battery costs; Dutch bicycle maker VanMoof printing a flatscreen-TV image on bike shipping boxes (rather than just improving packaging) after realizing shippers handle "electronics" more carefully than "bikes," cutting shipping damage 70–80%; and Airbnb, Amazon, and Google applying the same approach to trust-building, customer-centric operations, and product culture respectively.

Limitations: first-principles thinking is time-consuming, requires strong foundational knowledge, and carries a real risk of "paralysis by analysis" if the deconstruction phase never ends in action.
