# Menopause: Plant-forward diet linked to better heart health

source_url: https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/plant-based-planetary-health-diet-could-help-protect-heart-health

---

By Katharine Lang, Medical News Today. July 30, 2026. Fact checked by Jill Seladi-Schulman, Ph.D.

A study presented at NUTRITION 2026, the annual meeting of the American Society for Nutrition, found that postmenopausal women whose diet most closely followed the Planetary Health Diet had a 28% lower risk of cardiovascular disease than those with lower adherence. The findings have not yet appeared in a peer-reviewed journal.

## What is the Planetary Health Diet?

A science-based dietary pattern designed to promote human health and protect the planet. It shares much with the Mediterranean and DASH diets — rich in whole grains, fruits, vegetables, nuts, and legumes, with only moderate or small amounts of fish, dairy, and meat — while also reducing the environmental impact of food production.

## The study

Researchers assessed dietary intake of almost 67,000 postmenopausal women in the Women's Health Initiative observational study (mean age 63.2 at enrollment), following them for a median of 8.2 years and recording 5,033 cardiovascular events. Participants completed a food-frequency questionnaire scored 0–140 on Planetary Health Diet adherence, then were divided into five groups. After adjusting for sociodemographic factors, lifestyle behaviors, energy intake, and BMI, those in the highest-adherence quintile had a 28% lower risk of cardiovascular disease, 34% lower risk of coronary heart disease, 22% lower stroke risk, and 31% lower risk of heart failure than those in the lowest quintile. Each 10-point increase in adherence was associated with reduced cardiovascular and coronary heart disease risk — meaningful reductions were seen even at moderate adherence levels.

Thomas M. Holland, MD, MS (RUSH Institute for Healthy Aging, not involved in the study), said cardiovascular risk increases after menopause as cholesterol, blood pressure, and metabolism shift, making dietary modification "a powerful tool for improving heart health, even later in life." He noted the findings align with other plant-forward patterns like the Mediterranean and MIND diets: fiber, antioxidants, and unsaturated fats reduce inflammation, improve blood vessel function, lower LDL cholesterol, and support blood sugar regulation.

Eamon Laird, PhD (Atlantic Technological University Sligo / Trinity College Dublin, not involved) called the results encouraging but cautioned this is only an abstract, not yet a full peer-reviewed analysis, and that as an observational study it cannot establish that the diet itself caused the lower risk.

## Small changes that could help

Laird suggested: fill half the plate with vegetables at every meal; choose oily fish or legumes more often and reduce processed meats; choose whole grains over refined grains; swap one or two meat-based meals a week for beans, lentils, or chickpeas; and reduce food waste through meal planning. Holland added that consistent small changes tend to produce greater long-term benefit than short-lived, dramatic diet changes.
