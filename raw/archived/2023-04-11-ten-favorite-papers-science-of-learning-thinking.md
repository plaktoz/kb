# Ten of My Favorite Papers on the Science of Learning and Thinking

Scott Young curates ten research papers that shaped his thinking on learning, drawn from roughly 500 scientific papers he read while researching a book.

1. **Why Minimal Guidance During Instruction Does Not Work** (Kirschner, Sweller, Clark): Constructivist/discovery-based teaching has intuitive appeal but the research favors strongly-guided direct instruction, where skills are carefully explained and taught, over methods relying on students discovering solutions themselves.

2. **Conditions for Intuitive Expertise: A Failure to Disagree** (Kahneman & Klein): Despite opposing research traditions (Kahneman on biased reasoning, Klein on expert intuition), both agree intuitive expertise only develops in environments with highly valid cues and rapid feedback for learning them.

3. **When and Where Do We Apply What We Learn? A Taxonomy for Far Transfer** (Barnett & Ceci): Transfer of learning to new settings is the central issue in education; most studies report transfer failures rather than successes, and the debate hinges on unresolved questions about how the mind represents knowledge.

4. **Self-Efficacy: Toward a Unifying Theory of Behavioral Change** (Bandura): Beyond believing an outcome is valuable, people need to believe they can execute the action required — low self-efficacy undermines motivation even when the goal is desired.

5. **The 2 Sigma Problem** (Bloom): One-on-one tutoring can produce two standard deviations of improvement over group instruction; mastery learning (interim tests plus targeted re-teaching) approaches but falls short of that gain, typically around 0.5 standard deviations.

6. **Blind Variation and Selective Retention in Creative Thought** (Campbell): Creative thinking mirrors biological evolution — generating variations and selectively retaining what works — suggesting inventive creativity is largely acquired knowledge plus trial and retention.

7. **Antagonism Between Achievement and Enjoyment in ATI Studies** (Clark): Low-ability students learn better with structured instruction and high-ability students benefit from open-ended challenge, yet students tend to prefer the method that works *less* well for them, likely because it feels less effortful.

8. **Long-Term Working Memory** (Ericsson & Kintsch): Chunking alone doesn't explain expert performance; as skill grows, experts learn to use long-term memory as a form of working memory, expanding effective capacity for familiar tasks.

9. **Does Learning to Read Improve Intelligence?** (Ritchie, Bates, Plomin): A twin study finds that twins with higher reading ability show greater gains in intelligence over time than their siblings, supporting the idea that reading ability can bootstrap general intelligence.

10. **Eliciting Self-Explanations Improves Understanding** (Chi et al.): Prompting students to explain material to themselves (akin to the Feynman Technique) increases understanding, likely because generating an explanation reveals gaps in knowledge that passive reading does not.
