# Scientists discover why poor sleep may harm some brains more

New research from Edith Cowan University's Centre for Precision Health suggests sleep habits and genetics interact to influence subtle brain and cognitive changes associated with Alzheimer's disease years before symptoms appear. The study examined the aquaporin-4 (AQP4) gene, which helps control fluid movement through the brain and supports its waste-clearing system — a process especially active during sleep that helps clear proteins associated with Alzheimer's.

Researchers analyzed 13 common AQP4 gene variants alongside participants' self-reported sleep patterns, brain scans, and cognitive test results. "Individuals carrying certain AQP4 variants showed faster grey matter loss when they reported shorter sleep," said researcher Dr. Ayeisha Milligan Armstrong. Among some participants, fewer sleep hours predicted faster grey-matter decline; in others, longer time to fall asleep was linked to lower brain volume. Cognitive performance changes also varied — appearing beneficial or harmful — depending on the specific AQP4 variant carried, meaning two people with similar overall Alzheimer's risk may not respond to poor sleep the same way.

Researcher Dr. Tenielle Porter cautioned the findings aren't yet a basis for genetic testing and need replication in larger, more diverse cohorts, but recommended clinical trials incorporating genetic information to test whether improving sleep can reduce inherited vulnerability. Study director Professor Simon Laws framed the work as a step toward personalized Alzheimer's prevention — identifying who is most vulnerable and most likely to benefit from a specific lifestyle intervention, rather than treating all at-risk individuals the same way. The study, "Evidence for Direct and Sleep-Moderated Relationships between Aquaporin-4 Genetic Variants and Alzheimer's Disease Phenotypes," is published in Alzheimer's & Dementia.
