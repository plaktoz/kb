# ‘How to not know’

Posted on [Chetan Parikh](https://capitalideasonline.com/wordpress/author/chetanparikh/ "View all posts by Chetan Parikh")  Posted in [Articles](https://capitalideasonline.com/wordpress/category/articles/)

[Download Article in PDF](/wordpress/how-to-not-know/?pdf=171605)

**“Question:** Can you give the big ideas from the book ‘How to not know’ by Simone Stolzoff?

**Response:** This is very much in the spirit of Annie Duke’s *Thinking in Bets*, Philip Tetlock’s *Superforecasting*, and Ezra Klein & Derek Thompson’s *Abundance*, but with a more psychological and existential focus. Simone Stolzoff’s central argument is that **the defining skill of the twenty-first century is not acquiring more certainty, but becoming more comfortable with uncertainty.** Rather than treating ambiguity as a problem to eliminate, he argues we should learn to navigate it with humility, curiosity, and values.

Here are what I see as the book’s **big ideas**.

---

**1. Uncertainty is not a bug—it is the permanent condition of human life**

Modern technology creates the illusion that every question has an answer.

Google.  
 AI.  
 Experts.  
 Algorithms.

Yet the decisions that matter most remain fundamentally unknowable:

* whom to marry
* where to live
* what career to pursue
* when to invest
* whether to have children
* how nations evolve

The mistake is believing more information eventually eliminates uncertainty.

Often it merely changes its form.

The skill is learning to act without complete certainty.

---

**2. We confuse information with knowledge**

We live in the richest information environment in history.

But information rarely resolves genuine uncertainty.

Instead it often creates:

* analysis paralysis
* contradictory evidence
* overconfidence
* endless optimization

The issue isn’t lack of data.

It is knowing which uncertainty is irreducible.

This echoes Herbert Simon’s observation that **a wealth of information creates a poverty of attention**, and the book argues that wisdom lies partly in recognizing when more data will not produce better decisions.

---

**3. The greatest mistakes come from intolerance of ambiguity**

People hate uncertainty.

Psychologists call this “uncertainty intolerance.”

It drives:

* conspiracy theories
* dogmatism
* premature decisions
* ideological extremism
* false confidence

Certainty often feels emotionally safer than doubt.

Unfortunately reality rarely cooperates.

---

**4. We mistake confidence for competence**

Society rewards people who sound certain.

Politicians.  
 Consultants.  
 CEOs.  
 TV experts.

Yet many domains simply cannot be predicted accurately.

The best thinkers often say

“I don’t know.”

This is intellectual strength rather than weakness.

---

**5. There are no perfect decisions—only better bets**

Many important decisions cannot be optimized.

Instead they resemble poker.

You make decisions with:

* incomplete information
* changing environments
* unknown probabilities

A good process can still produce bad outcomes.

A poor process can produce lucky outcomes.

Judgment therefore matters more than prediction.

---

**6. Curiosity is stronger than certainty**

Curiosity changes uncertainty from a threat into an opportunity.

Instead of asking

“How can I eliminate uncertainty?”

“What can uncertainty teach me?”

This shifts attention from control toward exploration.

The mindset resembles scientific inquiry rather than courtroom certainty.

---

**7. Identity should not depend upon being right**

Many conflicts persist because people fuse beliefs with identity.

Changing one’s mind then feels like losing oneself.

The healthier approach is

“I hold beliefs.”

rather than

“I am my beliefs.”

This makes updating psychologically possible.

---

**8. Values matter more than forecasts**

Since forecasts are unreliable,

good decisions should increasingly be guided by values.

Instead of asking

“What outcome is guaranteed?”

ask

“What action best reflects the kind of person I wish to become?”

Values remain stable even when predictions fail.

---

**9. Anxiety often comes from demanding certainty that reality cannot provide**

Many people postpone action until they “feel sure.”

That day rarely arrives.

The author argues that uncertainty is often the cost of meaningful action.

You do not overcome uncertainty first.

You act despite it.

---

**10. Good institutions preserve productive disagreement**

Organizations frequently suppress uncertainty.

Everyone wants alignment.

Everyone wants confidence.

But healthy organizations deliberately preserve dissent.

Disagreement exposes unknown unknowns.

The absence of disagreement often signals conformity rather than truth.

---

**11. Technology both reduces and amplifies uncertainty**

AI answers questions instantly.

Search engines provide endless explanations.

Prediction markets improve forecasts.

Yet these tools can also create:

* false precision
* algorithmic overconfidence
* dependence on external authority

Technology should support judgment—not replace it.

---

**12. Wisdom is becoming comfortable saying “I don’t know”**

Children naturally tolerate uncertainty.

Experts often lose this ability.

The wisest people recover it.

Not because they know less.

Because they understand better where knowledge ends.

---

**13. The “Three Horsemen of Delusion”**

A recurring framework Stolzoff discusses is that our relationship with uncertainty is distorted by three recurring illusions:

* **Comfort** — mistaking the feeling of certainty for truth.
* **Hubris** — believing we know more than we actually do.
* **Control** — assuming certainty gives us mastery over the future.

These illusions encourage overconfidence precisely where humility is most needed.

---

**14. The future rewards adaptability more than prediction**

The old model was

Predict → Plan → Execute

The emerging model is

Observe → Learn → Adapt

Flexibility increasingly dominates foresight.

---

**15. Living well means learning to “row through the fog”**

One of Stolzoff’s recurring metaphors is that life is less like following a GPS and more like rowing through fog. You cannot see the entire route; you advance by taking the next sound stroke, guided by values rather than perfect visibility.

---

**Why this matters for investors**

For investors, Stolzoff’s message is especially powerful:

* Markets are permanently uncertain, not temporarily uncertain.
* Forecasts are inputs, not foundations.
* Good process beats confident prediction.
* Position sizing acknowledges ignorance.
* Optionality is more valuable than precision.
* Humility compounds because it keeps you learning.

His philosophy complements rather than replaces probabilistic investing: the goal is not to know the future, but to make robust decisions when the future cannot be known.

Viewed through that lens, the book is less about ignorance than about **epistemic maturity**—recognizing the limits of knowledge while still acting decisively and thoughtfully.

---

**Question:** The Munger cut?

**Response:** Charlie Munger would have liked *How to Not Know* far more than its title suggests. He spent decades warning against overconfidence, emphasizing probabilistic thinking, inversion, and the importance of recognizing the boundaries of one’s competence. But he also disliked philosophies that drifted into celebrating uncertainty for its own sake. He wanted uncertainty to be **disciplined, quantified where possible, and reduced where it could be**.

Here’s what I think the **Munger cut** would look like.

---

**What Munger would have strongly endorsed**

**1. “Knowing what you don’t know” is the beginning of wisdom**

This was almost a Munger mantra.

He repeatedly quoted:

“The first rule is that you can’t know everything.”

The real competitive advantage is not intelligence.

It is accurately mapping

* what you know
* what you probably know
* what you definitely don’t know.

He would connect Stolzoff to

* Socrates
* Richard Feynman
* Warren Buffett’s “circle of competence.”

The investor who survives longest usually has the best map of ignorance.

---

**2. Humility is a practical tool—not a moral virtue**

Munger admired humility because it improves decisions.

Humility means

“I may be wrong.”

That produces

* checklists
* second opinions
* larger margins of safety
* diversification where ignorance exists
* delayed judgment

Humility becomes an operating system.

---

**3. Probabilistic thinking is the proper response to uncertainty**

This is where Munger would enrich Stolzoff.

Instead of

“I don’t know”

he would say

“I know roughly.”

Reality rarely gives certainty.

It often gives

60%

80%

95%

10%

Thinking in odds is vastly superior to binary thinking.

He’d bring in:

* Thomas Bayes
* Pierre-Simon Laplace
* Annie Duke
* Richard Zeckhauser

Ignorance is rarely total.

Usually it is probabilistic.

---

**4. The objective is not certainty—but asymmetry**

Investors do not need certainty.

They need

positive expected value.

Even if you’re right only

55%

of the time,

good position sizing creates wealth.

This is why casinos make money.

The house does not know every hand.

It knows the odds.

---

**5. Circle of competence matters more than curiosity**

Stolzoff celebrates curiosity.

Munger would agree—

but with limits.

He often warned:

Curiosity without discipline becomes intellectual tourism.

Instead:

Expand competence slowly.

Stay inside it when capital matters.

Curiosity should deepen competence—not replace it.

---

**6. Most ignorance is removable**

This is perhaps Munger’s biggest addition.

Many people surrender too early.

Often uncertainty exists because

* they haven’t read enough
* haven’t talked to practitioners
* haven’t done enough work
* haven’t decomposed the problem

His advice:

Read another hundred pages.

Then decide.

---

**7. Great judgment comes from multiple mental models**

Uncertainty shrinks when viewed through several disciplines simultaneously.

Economics says one thing.

Biology adds another.

Psychology changes the answer.

Engineering introduces constraints.

History provides analogies.

No single discipline removes uncertainty.

Together they dramatically reduce it.

This is classic latticework thinking.

---

**8. Incentives create much of the apparent uncertainty**

Many forecasts fail because people intentionally distort information.

Ask:

Who benefits?

Who is paid?

Who gets promoted?

Who bears downside?

Much “uncertainty” is actually hidden incentives.

Here Munger would invoke:

* Upton Sinclair
* Garrett Hardin
* Robert Cialdini

---

**9. Optionality is insurance against ignorance**

If uncertainty cannot be removed,

structure decisions so mistakes are survivable.

Examples

* avoid leverage
* maintain liquidity
* preserve reputation
* preserve learning capacity

Taleb later called this optionality.

Munger practiced it decades earlier.

---

**10. Good habits compensate for limited knowledge**

Munger distrusted brilliance unsupported by routine.

When uncertainty rises,

habits become more valuable than cleverness.

Checklists.

Waiting.

Reading.

Patience.

These reduce avoidable mistakes.

---

**Where Munger would push back**

**1. Don’t romanticize uncertainty**

This would be his biggest criticism.

Sometimes writers treat uncertainty as intrinsically beautiful.

Munger would say:

No.

Many uncertainties exist because someone hasn’t done the work.

The objective is always:

Reduce uncertainty where possible.

Accept only the irreducible remainder.

He might summarize it this way:

“Ignorance is not a lifestyle; it’s a starting point.”

---

**2. “I don’t know” is only step one**

The second step is

How could I know?

Can I estimate?

Can I bound the possibilities?

Can I compare base rates?

Can I consult experts?

Can I invert the problem?

Munger always converted ignorance into investigation.

---

**3. Expected value beats emotional comfort**

Psychology matters.

But markets reward mathematics.

People hate uncertainty because it feels uncomfortable.

Munger would insist that feelings are a poor guide.

The key questions are:

* What’s the expected value?
* What’s the downside?
* What’s the probability distribution?
* What’s the opportunity cost?

---

**4. Build systems that make uncertainty manageable**

Munger loved institutional design.

Rather than becoming emotionally comfortable with ambiguity,

design environments where ambiguity matters less.

Examples:

* decentralized decisions
* redundancy
* strong incentives
* checklists
* peer review
* cash reserves
* simple business models

Instead of changing psychology,

change architecture.

---

**5. Action without edge is gambling**

The book encourages acting despite uncertainty.

Munger would add an important qualifier.

Sometimes the correct decision is

**doing nothing.**

Waiting is an action.

One of his most repeated lessons was that investing is unusual because there is **no penalty for not swinging**.

When the odds are unclear,

sit on your hands.
