# Global Equity Views 3Q 2026

J.P. Morgan Asset Management sees strong global earnings growth in 2026, driven by a boom in AI investment spending that has "comprehensively trumped" both geopolitics and central bank policy as a driver of equity returns. Data center investment spending is projected at $1.6 trillion this year — more than triple the level of two years ago — with some optimists projecting $8 trillion within five years and total spend potentially topping $3 trillion by 2028. U.S. corporate profits are now expected to jump 27% this year and 22% in 2027, up sharply from a 14% forecast made in January; emerging-market earnings are expected to rise 65% in 2026.

About 75% of expected U.S. profit growth this year comes from AI spending plus higher oil prices; growth excluding those factors is a more modest ~8%. Risks flagged: growing reliance on debt rather than cash flow to fund AI buildouts, physical constraints like power supply, rising popular opposition to data centers, and uncertainty over future AI token pricing.

On momentum: "we have been experiencing one of the strongest momentum markets we have ever seen" — by size, among the top four momentum booms of the last 40 years, and by speed, second only to the 1999 internet boom. Warning signs include falling correlation between momentum and other quality/valuation factors (meaning gains are increasingly just chasing higher prices) and sharply rising volatility in the most fashionable stocks. J.P. Morgan recommends diversifying by style (value) and geography to guard against the rapid capital loss that typically follows the end of a momentum boom — a consideration directly relevant to momentum-factor ETF exposure.

At a July internal meeting, investors with three to four decades of market experience discussed parallels with past booms and busts. Most don't believe markets are in a bubble yet, since the current boom is in earnings rather than valuations, but flagged that valuation "matters, eventually," that market timing is nearly impossible (the NASDAQ was seen as dangerously overpriced by mid-1999, then doubled again over the next nine months), and that disciplined risk management — staying objective rather than swinging between overconfidence and excessive caution — is what wins across full market cycles. Team preferences currently lean toward industrials, quality stocks, and select "AI losers," while avoiding consumer staples and speculative growth names.
