# The Federal Reserve Just Did Something That No One's Witnessed in 56 Years

On July 29, the day Fed Chair Kevin Warsh and the Federal Open Market Committee (FOMC) announced they were leaving interest rates steady, the Dow Jones Industrial Average, S&P 500, and Nasdaq Composite all tumbled — the Dow's worst single-session performance in more than a year. It was also a history-making FOMC meeting: three members (Beth Hammack, Neel Kashkari, and Lorie Logan) dissented in favor of a quarter-point rate hike, the first time three dissents in the same direction have occurred since September 2016, and the first time a new Fed chair has overseen three dissents this early in a tenure since 1970.

Warsh inherited a historically divided FOMC. Trump's tariffs modestly lifting goods prices, combined with the Trump-led Iran war pushing up fuel prices at the fastest pace in three decades, sent trailing 12-month inflation to a three-year high of 4.2% in May, the month Warsh was sworn in. Powell's final FOMC meeting in April was already marked by four dissents, breaking his tenure's low-dissension record dating back to 1978.

A lack of monetary policy cohesion is viewed as potentially dangerous for Wall Street, since the Fed is seen as a foundational pillar of the market's long-term success. The division, combined with a notable move higher in long-term Treasury bond yields, suggests an increased likelihood the Fed will raise rates to stabilize prices — which could slow the AI infrastructure build-out that has been foundational to recent stock market rallies. In other words: if the Fed holds rates amid historic division, the market loses; if it acts to stabilize prices, the market also loses.
