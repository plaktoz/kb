# Is paying artists enough to convince them to embrace AI?

Illustrators have spent years sounding the alarm about generative AI startups training their models on artists' work without permission, calling the practice tantamount to theft. This has led to contentious legal battles, but it has also given rise to a new wave of AI startups like Pippa that market their services as being more ethical than the competition.

Like most companies selling access to text-to-video models, Pippa's main product is short bursts of user-generated footage that can be cobbled together to tell visual stories. What sets Pippa apart is that its business model is designed to pay artists directly: every time a subscriber generates an image or video clip whose style is derived from a human artist's work, that artist is compensated by the platform.

Pippa's cofounders, Hogan Shrum and Sean Wright, believe their focus on working with artists will attract customers and prove that AI firms can generate content ethically. Wright compared the moment to when Napster was shut down and the music industry moved to the 99-cent song model, paying musical artists differently.

Pippa's payments to artists are small on an individual basis compared to what the service charges users (subscriptions range from $14.99 to $99.99 a month). Artists receive $0.005 per image and $0.003 per second of video, plus access to a cut of a 5 percent royalty pool funded by overall subscription revenue — a structure the company likens to Spotify's, despite many artists criticizing Spotify's own payouts.

Pippa launched in May and currently has around 800 paying subscribers, but has only signed licensing/model-training agreements with four human artists, with four more in talks. Artists must go through a multi-step vetting process to verify their work before being approved, after which models trained on their art become available to subscribers. Artists can also submit work under pseudonyms if they're concerned about being seen as "crossing the picket line."

Despite its ethical framing, Pippa's technology still involves using art scraped from the internet without creators' express consent, since its models still have "initial training on the broader set of content out there." The company plans to incorporate ByteDance's Seedance 2.5 model to expand its capabilities, but to stand out from competitors offering the same model, it will need to sign significantly more in-house artists.
