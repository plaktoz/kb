# How to Improve Your Memory

source_url: https://www.helpguide.org/aging/healthy-aging/how-to-improve-your-memory

---

A strong memory depends on the health and vitality of the brain. The human brain has an ability to adapt and change even into old age — neuroplasticity — and this ability holds true for learning and memory specifically. Nine tips can help improve memory at any age.

**Tip 1: Give your brain a workout.** Memory, like muscular strength, requires "use it or lose it." The best brain exercises break routine and challenge new brain pathways — learning an instrument, a language, chess, or a new physical skill. Evidence increasingly suggests commercial brain-training apps produce only short-term improvements on the specific task practiced, not overall intelligence or memory gains.

**Tip 2: Don't skip physical exercise.** Physical exercise increases oxygen to the brain, reduces risk for disorders that lead to memory loss (diabetes, cardiovascular disease), enhances helpful brain chemicals, reduces stress hormones, and boosts growth factors that stimulate new neuronal connections.

**Tip 3: Get your Zs.** Over 95% of adults need 7.5 to 9 hours of sleep nightly to avoid sleep deprivation; even skimping a few hours compromises memory, creativity, and critical thinking. Sleep is necessary for memory consolidation, with key memory-enhancing activity occurring during the deepest sleep stages. Tips: keep a regular sleep schedule, avoid screens for an hour before bed (blue light suppresses melatonin), and cut back on caffeine.

**Tip 4: Make time for friends.** Research shows meaningful friendships and a strong support system are vital to brain health — interacting with others may provide some of the best brain exercise available. A Harvard School of Public Health study found people with the most active social lives had the slowest rate of memory decline.

**Tip 5: Keep stress in check.** Chronic stress destroys brain cells and damages the hippocampus, the region involved in forming new memories. Meditation improves focus, concentration, creativity, memory, and reasoning skills, and increases the thickness of the cerebral cortex.

**Tip 6: Have a laugh.** Laughter engages multiple brain regions and activates areas vital to learning and creativity, helping people "think more broadly and associate more freely" (per psychologist Daniel Goleman).

**Tip 7: Eat a brain-boosting diet.** Get omega-3s (fatty fish like salmon, tuna, mackerel, sardines, or non-fish sources like walnuts, flaxseed, and soybeans). Limit calories and saturated fat, which increase dementia risk and impair concentration. Eat antioxidant-rich fruits and vegetables. Green tea's polyphenols may enhance memory and slow brain aging. Moderate alcohol (around 1 glass/day for women, 2 for men), especially resveratrol-rich red wine, may improve memory and cognition, while heavier drinking kills brain cells.

**Tip 8: Identify and treat health problems.** Cardiovascular disease, high cholesterol, high blood pressure, and diabetes are linked to cognitive decline. Hormone imbalances (menopause-related estrogen dips, low testosterone, thyroid imbalances) can cause forgetfulness. Many common medications (cold/allergy medications, sleep aids, antidepressants) interfere with memory. A 2024 update to the Lancet Commission's global dementia research added untreated vision loss and high LDL cholesterol to its list of dementia risk factors. Depression is a common but under-recognized cause of memory and concentration problems, especially in older adults, and memory typically returns to normal once depression is treated.

**Tip 9: Take practical steps to support learning and memory.** Pay attention — it takes about eight seconds of intense focus to encode a piece of information; pick a quiet place if easily distracted. Involve multiple senses (colors, textures, the physical act of writing, reading aloud). Relate new information to what's already known. For complex material, focus on understanding basic ideas rather than memorizing isolated details, and practice explaining ideas to someone else. Rehearse information the same day it's learned and at intervals thereafter ("spaced rehearsal" beats cramming). Use mnemonic devices — visual images, sentences, or words that help associate new information with something memorable.
