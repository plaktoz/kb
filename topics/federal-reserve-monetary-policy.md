# Federal Reserve Monetary Policy

This topic follows the Federal Reserve's 2026 return to rate hikes: the September decision and Warsh's hawkish guidance, the late-September split among Fed speakers, sticky PCE inflation, AI's new place in the inflation outlook, and the weak September jobs report that pushed the next hike toward December.

## The September 2026 Hike and Its Setup

On September 16, 2026, the FOMC voted 12-0 to raise the federal funds rate a quarter point to 3.75%–4.00%, its first increase since 2023, with Chair Kevin Warsh pointing to inflation that had not meaningfully improved over the summer [[fed-12-0-rate-hike-3-75-percent-housing-stocks-sep2026]]. Governor Cook later put trailing-year inflation at the time of the vote at an estimated 3.8% headline and 3.4% core [[fed-governor-cook-ai-economy-speech-sep2026]]. Markets had seen the move coming: the day before, Brent crude above $109 had pushed the 10-year Treasury yield briefly past 5%, and Goldman's David Mericle argued that with hike odds near 90% the committee would want to avoid the market reaction to standing pat [[10-year-treasury-yield-tops-5-percent-sep2026]]. In that loop, market pricing itself became the argument for acting, while Ed Yardeni offered the counterintuitive view that a hike might ease long-term yields by restoring Fed credibility.

## Warsh's Guidance: A Longer, Steeper Path

On decision day the guidance moved markets more than the hike did: Warsh called for a "timelier return" to 2%, echoing his Jackson Hole demand that inflation fall at "sufficient speed," and the dot plot showed 50 more basis points before year-end, no cuts in 2027, and core inflation not back at 2% until 2029, sending the Dow down 631 points [[warsh-timelier-return-fomc-dot-plot-ai-sep2026]]. Warsh also said he was "hard-pressed" to call financial conditions restrictive, implying room for more tightening, and cited strong hiring, business investment and private-sector earnings as evidence that the economy could absorb it [[pce-inflation-preview-fed-rate-hike-outlook-sep2026]]. The rest of the committee broadly agreed: 16 of 18 forecasters expected at least one more 2026 move, and most saw no cuts until 2028 or later [[fed-12-0-rate-hike-3-75-percent-housing-stocks-sep2026]]. That left businesses and investors facing several years of elevated borrowing costs.

## Fed Speakers Split on Urgency

Within two weeks of the hike, Fed officials were split on timing, though not on direction: NY Fed President John Williams said there was "no need for urgency" and that the Fed had time to gather more information, while still calling inflation too high and leaving a hike "late this year" on the table [[ny-fed-williams-rate-increase-can-wait-sep2026]]. Governors Michael Barr and Lisa Cook leaned the other way the same week. Barr said further adjustments were likely needed, noting that core PCE had been at target in only 2 of the last 20 months and that tariffs and the prolonged Iran war had knocked disinflation off course, while Cook flagged continued pressure from the AI build-out and from higher oil prices passing through [[october-rate-hike-odds-below-50-williams-barr-cook-sep2026]] [[pce-inflation-preview-fed-rate-hike-outlook-sep2026]]. Even so, one sentence from Williams cut CME FedWatch odds of an October hike from 70% to below 50%, because his permanent FOMC vote and the New York Fed's market-operations role give his words more weight than the governors' more hawkish quotes, reversing the mid-September pattern in which market odds had pushed the Fed rather than the Fed moving the odds.

## Sticky PCE Inflation and a Measurement Wrinkle

The inflation data behind this debate offered little relief: the PCE preview expected headline and core prices each to rise 0.3% in August, holding the annual rates at 3.7% and 3.3%, while consumer spending stayed strong (a projected 0.8% August gain, and Bank of America card spending up 6.9% year over year) even as sentiment weakened [[pce-inflation-preview-fed-rate-hike-outlook-sep2026]]. Williams added nuance, pointing to disinflation in housing services and no inflation pressure from the labor market, but named AI-related goods demand as a third driver of inflation. A retroactive BEA methodology revision covering legal services, software and portfolio management could trim July's annual PCE by two to three tenths to around 3%, which would lower the backward-looking level without changing the outlook. By the time of the jobs report, core PCE was being cited at 3%, and inflation was still described as the bigger threat in policymakers' eyes [[september-jobs-report-29000-payrolls-unemployment-4-2-percent]].

## The September Jobs Report Pushes the Next Hike to December

The October 2 jobs report settled the October question: payrolls rose just 29,000 against an 84,000 forecast, July and August were revised down by a combined 60,000, annual wage growth slowed to 3% (the lowest since May 2021), and unemployment rose to 4.2%, mostly because 485,000 people joined the labor force [[september-jobs-report-29000-payrolls-unemployment-4-2-percent]]. The weakness had already shown in late September, when August job openings fell to 7.079 million and consumer confidence hit its lowest level since 2014 [[october-rate-hike-odds-below-50-williams-barr-cook-sep2026]]; after the report, Jefferies' Thomas Simons called it "the nail in the coffin for an October hike," CME FedWatch odds of an October hold jumped to 82.8%, and markets moved the next expected hike to December. In about a week, October went from a roughly 70% hike probability to a likely pause, and, assuming quarter-point steps, a December-only path would deliver half of the 50 basis points of further 2026 hikes the dot plot projected [[warsh-timelier-return-fomc-dot-plot-ai-sep2026]]. The argument for pausing rests on hiring and wages, not output, since the Atlanta Fed was tracking third-quarter GDP at 3.7%, which leaves the Fed weighing a low-hire, low-fire labor market against inflation still well above target.

## AI as a New Monetary Policy Variable

Governor Cook's September 28 speech made the clearest case for treating AI as a macroeconomic variable: she framed it as a general-purpose technology on a par with electricity whose investment boom is adding to inflation now, through chips and, more broadly, through widely used inputs such as construction labor and energy (electricity and water costs are each up about 5%), while any productivity gains would bring only modest and delayed disinflation [[fed-governor-cook-ai-economy-speech-sep2026]]. Companies have spent only a small fraction of $2 trillion in announced AI investment, and both Cook and Williams listed the AI build-out among the forces keeping inflation high [[pce-inflation-preview-fed-rate-hike-outlook-sep2026]]. On jobs, Cook found unemployment and layoffs still low but saw early signs of weaker demand in coding, translation and entry-level roles, and the September report's 10,000 information-sector job losses came amid similar worries about AI [[september-jobs-report-29000-payrolls-unemployment-4-2-percent]]. Her warning that the Fed's tools are too blunt to target narrow sectors, and that cutting rates to offset AI-driven skill-mismatch unemployment would risk fueling inflation, suggests AI could confront policymakers with price pressure and labor disruption at the same time.

## Sectoral Transmission: Housing and AI Infrastructure

Tightening hits debt-dependent sectors first: in housing, mortgage applications were down 19% year over year with rates nearing 7%, squeezing home purchases and refinancing at once as the lagged effects of earlier hikes add to the new one, and the housing analysis favored higher-quality names such as Rocket Companies and Home Depot over more rate-sensitive peers [[fed-12-0-rate-hike-3-75-percent-housing-stocks-sep2026]]. AI infrastructure is the second exposure, in a cycle Goldman called "particularly capital intensive" [[goldman-sachs-fed-tightening-sp500-historical-returns]]. SpaceX's $25 billion bond offering and the IPO plans at OpenAI and Anthropic show how much AI scaling depends on outside capital, and Morgan Stanley argued the sector is shifting its focus from growth stories to profitability [[fed-rate-hike-sep2026-ai-stocks-self-funding-edge]]. The resulting advantage is financial rather than technological: firms that fund their own capital spending are insulated from rising rates, and their edge over leveraged rivals grows the longer the cycle lasts, which the Fed's guidance suggests will be a long time.

## Equity Markets Through the Cycle

Goldman's history sets the baseline: the S&P 500 has averaged a 2% decline in the three months after a tightening cycle begins but a 9% gain over twelve months, driven mainly by earnings growth rather than rate levels, and about 75 basis points of tightening through mid-2027 was already priced in before the first hike [[goldman-sachs-fed-tightening-sp500-historical-returns]]. The risk is that hiking cycles have tended to mark the peaks of "high-valuation, high-concentration" bull markets, and the sell-off after Warsh's remarks showed how directly rate guidance threatens the AI-led rally [[warsh-timelier-return-fomc-dot-plot-ai-sep2026]]. Views on the cycle's length ranged from Mackenzie's forecast of "more rather than less" hikes into 2027 to Goldman's view that the Fed does not envision an aggressive cycle [[fed-rate-hike-sep2026-ai-stocks-self-funding-edge]]. When the weak jobs report came out, stocks rose and yields fell because it lowered the odds of an October hike, so in this environment soft economic data can be good news for stocks [[september-jobs-report-29000-payrolls-unemployment-4-2-percent]].
