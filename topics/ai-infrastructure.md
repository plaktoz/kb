# AI Infrastructure

AI infrastructure — data centers, power grids, networking, and specialized compute — has emerged as the physical foundation constraining AI progress and concentrating market power, with hyperscalers collectively committing trillions to buildout before AI revenues fully materialize.

## The Infrastructure Arms Race

U.S. large-scale data centers could nearly triple by 2030 driven by AI demand from Alphabet, Microsoft, Amazon, and Meta [[ai-data-center-footprint-triple-2030]], and Microsoft alone is targeting expansion from roughly 12 gigawatts to over 38 GW by 2032 — a more-than-threefold increase driven directly by the cascading disruptions its 2025 capacity pause caused [[microsoft-data-center-38gw-2032-expansion]]. Hyperscalers collectively have earmarked roughly $2.4 trillion for computing infrastructure, a capital commitment that concentrates risk in incumbents capable of absorbing the spend and creates winner-takes-most dynamics where falling behind on capacity translates directly to lost enterprise deals. Jensen Huang's framing at the G20 Summit — a 1-gigawatt AI data center facility costs $50B–$60B to construct — offers a replacement-cost lens for sizing these assets beyond ARR multiples, with Nebius Group's 5 GW contracted capacity implying up to $300B in facility replacement value [[jensen-huang-gigawatt-data-center-valuation-neocloud]]. Nscale's fall 2026 IPO plans (seeking up to $3.5B pre-IPO) and marquee board assembly (Sandberg, Clegg, Simo) are a leading indicator that infrastructure capital is attracting public-market interest at scale [[nscale-fidji-simo-board-ipo]].

The CPU-centric nature of AI agent workloads — orchestration, inference routing, memory — is reshaping infrastructure procurement beyond the GPU-dominated training buildout that defined earlier AI spend. Anthropic's $11.6 billion commitment to Akamai (the largest deal in Akamai's history, potentially growing to ~$20B with additional spending) centers on CPU capacity, with revenue recognition not beginning until 2H 2027 as Akamai invests ~$5.5B in new capacity [[anthropic-akamai-11-6-billion-cloud-deal]]. The deal's equity structure inverts the typical "circular AI investment" pattern: rather than the hyperscaler investing in the AI lab, Anthropic received a warrant for ~7.7 million Akamai shares vesting with spending milestones — a customer-equity mechanism that blurs the procurement-investment boundary and mirrors the AMD-OpenAI 2025 precedent, creating mutual lock-in rather than pure vendor procurement.

## Power as the Binding Constraint

Power availability — not chip supply — is emerging as the primary constraint on AI expansion, with "infrastructure starting to matter almost as much as the chips themselves" [[ai-data-center-footprint-triple-2030]]. The bottleneck principle applies: as chip supply constraints ease, the next binding resource becomes reliable electricity, grid connections, and cooling systems — each requiring multi-year procurement and construction timelines that dwarf hardware lead times. Regulatory headwinds compound the supply constraint: governors in Texas and New York have moved to halt new data center construction over energy and land-use concerns, introducing political risk into capacity planning that technical roadmaps alone cannot model [[microsoft-data-center-38gw-2032-expansion]]. The strategic implication is that securing energy — not algorithmic edge — may determine which companies can sustain frontier AI development in the late 2020s.

The nuclear energy route is gaining capital but facing reality constraints. Google has committed to sourcing power from Kairos Power's fluoride salt-cooled HTGR reactors — a technology type that received its first-ever NRC construction permit in 2023 for a demonstration unit and targets commercial operation by 2030, with Samsung C&T providing up to $100M in EPC investment [[kairos-power-samsung-google-nuclear-reactor]]. SpaceX is bridging with natural gas and the EIB has made its first planned SMR investment (€40M to Steady Energy). But the practical SMR timeline is still "a decade or more" away for meaningful commercial deployment — with NRC approvals still pending for Oklo and NuScale not yet commercialized [[oklo-nuscale-smr-data-center-backlash]]. The 80+ global SMR projects in development and the AI-infrastructure-to-energy-supply-chain integration pattern (compute demand vertically integrating straight to power source) are real structural signals, but current AI buildout must still rely on conventional grid power supplemented by natural gas and wind/solar in the interim.

The energy flexibility-versus-lock-in tradeoff is crystallizing as a real strategic decision. Crusoe Energy's termination of its $1.25 billion turbine agreement with Boom Supersonic — walked away to preserve site-by-site energy source flexibility rather than committing to a single power format — illustrates operators' growing preference for optionality over scale-optimized procurement [[crusoe-abandons-boom-turbine-deal]]. Boom used the freed capital ($300M raised separately) to cross-subsidize its Overture commercial jet program, revealing the dual-use financing logic behind large industrial energy deals. Oracle's invocation of force majeure on its 2.45 GW Project Jupiter Stargate campus in New Mexico — after Energy Transfer's natural gas pipeline experienced a 6-month delay and Bloom Energy fuel cell permits remained pending — demonstrates the permitting fragility that the social license critique makes visible at the individual project level [[oracle-force-majeure-stargate-new-mexico]]. The force majeure clause functions as a contractual hedge, not a withdrawal: Oracle's intent is to continue the project, not exit, while managing cost exposure during the delay. The structural lesson across both cases is that energy supply chains are now as operationally binding as GPU procurement for hyperscale AI development, and the organizations with diversified energy sourcing strategies carry the same strategic option value as those with diversified chip sourcing.

## Social License and Community Opposition

Community opposition to data center siting has emerged as a structural constraint the industry underestimated. Environmental justice groups are opposing data centers in already-burdened neighborhoods — the Grays Ferry/Philadelphia case documents coordinated opposition to centers sited in communities with existing pollution loads — and moratoriums have been enacted in Denver, Indianapolis, Asheville, Charlotte, Reno, and New York [[ai-data-center-community-opposition-philadelphia]]. BloombergNEF projects that U.S. data centers may use more gas than Germany and Japan combined by 2035, and diesel backup generator pollution during grid outages has become a specific flashpoint beyond the operational power draw alone. Governor Hochul's "consent-based siting" framework signals that political accommodation — not just NIMBY resistance — is being institutionalized: future data center expansion will require demonstrable community benefit agreements, not merely FAA and zoning approval. The 70% American opposition to local data centers documented in the SMR context ([[oklo-nuscale-smr-data-center-backlash]]) reflects the same dynamic: the infrastructure that AI requires is sited in someone's neighborhood, and that someone is increasingly organized, litigious, and politically connected. Taken together, the social license problem is distinct from and compounds the regulatory risk: permitting can be technically obtained while political opposition makes timelines and costs unpredictable.

## Infrastructure Investment Ecosystem: Picks and Shovels

The AI infrastructure buildout has created a durable "picks-and-shovels" investment thesis in the companies supplying power, switching, and construction rather than the hyperscalers themselves. GE Vernova reported $5B+ in U.S. data center orders year-to-date through mid-2026 — more than double full-year 2025 — with data center orders representing 25% of total orders as of Q2 2026 [[anthropic-ipo-ai-infrastructure-beneficiaries-gev-pwr-etn]]. Quanta Services reported a $53.4B backlog of electrical infrastructure projects, and Eaton posted Q2 2026 sales of $8.5B (+21% year-over-year). These three names — grid-scale power (GE Vernova), electrical construction (Quanta), and power management hardware (Eaton) — represent the layer between the electrical grid and data center walls: every GPU cluster must be wired, cooled, and power-managed, creating recurring infrastructure demand that does not require any single AI application to succeed. This layer is more diversified than hyperscaler bets and is already generating cash flows, making it attractive under the Morgan Stanley "mature AI phase" framing ([[fed-rate-hike-sep2026-ai-stocks-self-funding-edge]]) that favors infrastructure players with positive cash flow over AI firms dependent on capital markets.

## Compute Hardware Competition and GPU Alternatives

Nvidia's dominance of AI compute hardware faces emerging architectural competition. Cornelis Networks raised $205M (September 2026) and is commercializing its Active Compute Fabric — a network architecture that eliminates GPU idle time by enabling simultaneous processing and transmission, attacking the 30–50% idle-time inefficiency in conventional GPU cluster networking [[cornelis-ai-infrastructure-205m-funding-nvidia-alternative]]. As an Intel spin-out (2020) building open architecture versus Nvidia's proprietary interconnect lock-in, Cornelis represents the "AI infrastructure unbundling" pattern: specialized components from non-Nvidia vendors outperforming Nvidia's bundled stack on specific dimensions (interconnect efficiency, power per flop, open interoperability). The competitive dynamic mirrors the history of CPU architecture competition: Nvidia's CUDA ecosystem moat is deep, but it is sustained primarily by switching costs and toolchain inertia rather than fundamental performance advantages on all workloads. At data-center scale — where interconnect efficiency at 30% of workload cost translates directly to CapEx and OpEx — the economics of open alternatives improve as cluster sizes grow, which is exactly the direction the infrastructure arms race is heading.

Memory bandwidth is emerging as a second hardware bottleneck alongside interconnect: Samsung is targeting 2x HBM4/4E output by 2027, growing total HBM wafer capacity from ~180K to ~250K wafers/month, with HBM4 share of Samsung's mix growing from ~40% to ~80% of HBM output [[samsung-hbm4-output-doubling-2027]]. Samsung shipped HBM4E 12-layer samples to Nvidia in May 2026 — the most advanced HBM generation at higher stack density — ahead of broad qualification. The structural significance is that HBM supply now has dedicated roadmap commitment from the largest memory manufacturer, reducing the risk of the supply shortages that plagued HBM3E procurement in 2024–2025. Micron's simultaneous DRAM revenue projections (quadrupling as data centers shift to LPDDR5X and HBM) and Sandisk's entry signal that memory hardware is following the same multi-supplier commodity trajectory as GPU alternatives — competitive pressure ultimately benefits hyperscalers on procurement, but reduces memory-vendor margin over the medium term.

## Neocloud Economics and Valuation

The neocloud sector has developed its own valuation frameworks distinct from traditional SaaS metrics, driven by the physical asset intensity of AI data centers. Per-megawatt annual contract values surged from $12M at the start of 2026 to over $20M in Q2, with short-term Q3 deals reaching "above $40M per megawatt" — a tripling in under a year — enabling customer prepayments to self-finance 50–60% of Nebius' capital expenditures and build an $8B cash position [[jensen-huang-gigawatt-data-center-valuation-neocloud]]. The flywheel dynamic is notable: rising contract values generate larger prepayments, which self-finance more capacity, which attracts larger contracts — a compounding loop that explains accelerating cash positions at leading neoclouds without traditional debt financing. Unencumbered data center portfolios provide a secondary financing safety net, as both Iren and Nebius hold physical assets that could support asset-backed borrowing if GPU-collateralized debt becomes insufficient.

## The Cost of Getting Capacity Wrong

Microsoft's 2025 capacity pause illustrates how infrastructure shortfalls propagate unpredictably through a product portfolio. A single pause triggered: Temu defecting to Oracle, a GitHub eight-hour outage rerouting traffic through AWS, and Xbox capping cloud game streaming — all from a shared infrastructure constraint [[microsoft-data-center-38gw-2032-expansion]]. The 38 GW expansion target and $145B capital expenditure commitment are directly driven by that lesson: insufficient capacity converts to immediate competitive damage, not just foregone growth, and recovery from capacity shortfalls takes years longer than the shortfalls take to develop. The bottleneck principle applies at the portfolio level: shared infrastructure constraints propagate laterally across unrelated products in ways that silo-based planning cannot anticipate.

## Quantum as Adjacent Infrastructure

IBM's quantum innovation hub at ETH Zurich represents a qualitatively different infrastructure investment — not data center capacity but foundational compute architecture being seeded for the post-classical era [[ibm-quantum-innovation-hub-eth-zurich]]. The hub combines IBM's Quantum Nighthawk processor with a 10-year ETH Zurich academic partnership, defense applications with Lockheed Martin (quantum sensing for navigation, metallic alloy additive manufacturing), and an education flywheel of certifications and hackathons — the classic ecosystem-building pattern applied to pre-commercial technology. The competitor landscape (Microsoft targeting chemistry and materials science, Google Quantum AI pursuing performance benchmarks) reflects a bet that the first lab to entrench its hardware as the academic default will inherit the installed base when quantum computing crosses commercial viability thresholds — directly analogous to NVIDIA's CUDA ecosystem moat in classical AI compute.

## Space-Based and Novel Compute Architectures

Google's Project Suncatcher is exploring LEO satellite deployment as an AI compute platform, with a core efficiency hypothesis: TPUs in low Earth orbit receive approximately 8x more solar energy than ground installations, potentially reducing power cost-per-flop substantially for inference workloads [[google-project-suncatcher-tpu-in-orbit]]. The technical qualification milestone — Google's Trillium TPU (v6) passing radiation hardening tests — clears the path to a planned 2027 two-satellite demonstrator using inter-satellite laser communications, scheduled to launch on SpaceX Transporter-18. The immediate operational questions (latency for ground-request round trips, thermal management without atmospheric cooling, on-orbit maintenance and upgrade constraints) are not yet resolved, but the power economics are compelling enough to justify a multi-year development program. Space-based compute is adjacent to, not a replacement for, terrestrial AI infrastructure at current timescales — but its development alongside nuclear and grid-scale power diversification reflects a structural bet that the binding constraint on AI compute in the late 2020s and beyond will be power availability, not silicon manufacturing capacity.
