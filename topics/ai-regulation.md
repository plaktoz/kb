# AI Regulation

How governments are trying to govern AI (executive actions and task forces, the EU AI Act, state and city laws, fights over open models, copyright and distillation, and the politics of who writes the rules), with technical safety left to [[ai-safety]].

## Washington's Posture: Promotion Over Restraint

The Trump administration treats binding AI rules as a gift to China, so its prerelease safety-testing framework for frontier models stays voluntary and undisclosed ([[white-house-expand-ai-policy-open-models]]). Trump calls the slowdown push a "hoax" that helps adversaries, and Jensen Huang told him live at the All-In Summit, "We're not going to let that happen, sir" ([[nvidia-jensen-huang-trump-all-in-summit-ai-slowdown]]). Its main positive program is industrial: industry pledged $2.4B in compute, led by Nvidia's $1B, to extend the Genesis Mission's AI-for-science work to 14 agencies. Trump paired the announcement with the claim that slowing down so China can win is a bigger threat than the risks the pacing CEOs warn about ([[trump-genesis-mission-2-4b-tech-compute-pledges]]).

## The Super Intelligence Force

The federal task force announced on October 4 is chaired by DNI Jay Clayton, the de facto AI czar, with FTC Chair Andrew Ferguson, Emil Michael and Scott Kupor as vice chairs. It reportedly has 120 days to report on AI risks and opportunities, and its charter pairs plans for "SI-enabled threats" with a promise to prevent "overregulation and regulatory capture" ([[trump-super-intelligence-force-clayton-task-force]]). It was announced with no specific actions or oversight powers. Clayton's model is finance-style risk mechanisms "developed in dialogue" with industry, as with the Fed and the SEC, so oversight would be negotiated with the industry, not imposed on it ([[trump-super-intelligence-force-ai-task-force]]).

## Whiplash Over Open Models

The administration has reversed itself on open-weight models more than once. Senior officials weighed sanctions, a trade blacklist and a ban on US clouds serving Chinese open-source developers, then backed off after Silicon Valley objected and floated a prerelease cybersecurity review instead ([[white-house-ai-regulation-whiplash-open-source]]). They next planned to extend the voluntary testing framework to open models once they reach frontier capability, so that only certifying closed models wouldn't create a two-tier market ([[white-house-expand-ai-policy-open-models]]). David Sacks has said that banning Chinese open models would be "a tragic mistake" ([[anthropic-copyright-settlement-open-source-ai-distillation]]).

## Security or Protectionism

Commentators argue that the alarm over Moonshot's Kimi repeats the TikTok pattern, with national-security language covering for trade protection and no clear test separating the two ([[chinese-ai-moonshot-kimi-protectionism]]). OpenAI's Dean Ball called for "regulatory uncertainty" around Chinese open-weight models and then walked it back, which was read as saying the quiet part out loud ([[making-sense-of-the-panic-over-chinese-ai]]). Ben Thompson identifies the real danger as a self-inflicted one: US frontier-model guardrails pushed Hugging Face's incident responders onto China's GLM 5.2, leaving defenders worse equipped than attackers ([[whos-afraid-of-chinese-models]]).

## Distillation, Copyright and Fair Use

Copyright and distillation are the same policy argument seen from opposite sides. Anthropic paid a record $1.5B settlement for training on about 7 million pirated books, yet it calls distillation of its own outputs "attacks" rather than "theft," a word that would undercut its own fair-use defense ([[anthropic-copyright-settlement-open-source-ai-distillation]]). Garry Tan's answer to Anthropic's call for a crackdown is "I would do nothing," plus a US regime that lets domestic open-weight labs distill American frontier models ([[garry-tan-us-open-weight-ai-distillation-regime]]). Thompson would codify training-data collection as fair use and ban terms-of-service limits on distillation ([[whos-afraid-of-chinese-models]]), Zuckerberg defends distillation as legitimate learning ([[zuckerberg-manifesto-open-source-ai-muse-glimmer]]), and Amodei wants crackdowns on adversary-linked distillation to protect the democratic lead ([[dario-amodei-we-must-pace-the-frontier]]).

## The White House Accord

On September 29, six CEOs, including Amodei, Brockman, Huang and Zuckerberg, signed a White House accord. It commits them to internal controls, an independent external auditor and board-level review, steps it says could later become law ([[trump-tech-ceos-ai-self-policing-accord-sep2026]]). Its substance tracks Amodei's "pace the frontier" plan, but it has no penalties, timeline or definition of "independent," and Apple, Microsoft and Amazon didn't sign ([[trump-super-intelligence-rebrand-naming-poll-sep2026]]). Its credibility suffers because AI executives were among MAGA Inc.'s largest donors and 73% of Americans say AI companies aren't doing enough to prevent serious harm ([[trump-tech-ceos-ai-self-policing-accord-sep2026]]).

## Rebranding as Policy

The same day as the accord, an executive order renamed AI "Super Intelligence" in federal usage, after Truth Social and X polls. Nick Bostrom's definition shows the term describes hypothetical systems beyond human intelligence, not today's chatbots, so the label changes perception without changing the technology ([[trump-super-intelligence-rebrand-naming-poll-sep2026]]). Trump escalated at the Genesis Mission event by calling anyone who still says "Artificial Intelligence" "THE ENEMY," and Nvidia's official statement adopted the new term ([[trump-genesis-mission-2-4b-tech-compute-pledges]]). The task force itself is named to match the rebrand ([[trump-super-intelligence-force-clayton-task-force]]).

## Industry Blueprints for Oversight

Demis Hassabis proposes a FINRA-style self-regulatory body under federal oversight, with 30-day prerelease submissions for cyber, bio and national-security review ([[ai-self-regulation-sro-stripe-paypal-ny-datacenter]]). His version adds held-out tests labs can't game and a ratchet that could escalate to coordinated slowdowns ([[google-deepmind-institute-agi-debate]]). Sacks's conditions are broad representation that includes startups and open source, frontier-only scope, a catastrophic-risk focus, a voluntary start, and replacing rules rather than stacking them; the All-In hosts warn that FAA-style certification, at 5–9 years per aircraft type, would cripple AI ([[ai-self-regulation-sro-stripe-paypal-ny-datacenter]]). Amodei asks governments for antitrust waivers so labs can coordinate, and sketches arms-control-style tiers from bioweapon bans up to full slowdowns ([[dario-amodei-we-must-pace-the-frontier]]).

## The Case for Mandates

Brad Smith goes further than voluntary schemes. He wants mandatory emergency brakes for AI in critical infrastructure, independent prerelease evaluations, and the decades-built rigor of airline and food safety rules, noting that product liability already forbids knowingly shipping unsafe products ([[brad-smith-microsoft-ai-power-advancing-faster-than-safety]]). Sanders frames the same choice as voluntary pledges versus binding congressional regulation, after the labs' own thresholds were crossed ([[sanders-letter-pause-ai-development-aug2026]]). Equity's hosts argue that markets can't fill the gap: Washington won't enforce, enterprise clients won't switch providers over ethics, and capital inflows shield labs from consumer pressure ([[ai-slowdown-commitment-accountability-gap]]).

## Safety Rules or a Cartel?

Every shared-standards proposal draws the same objection: rules written by the leaders protect the leaders. Cohere's Aidan Gomez calls the dominant labs a "cartel," and Amodei has conceded his plan could "widen America's lead significantly" ([[ai-safety-debate-safety-or-control]]). Sacks lists "monopoly masking" and regulatory capture among the motives behind a letter from about 1,300 lab employees seeking government-led governance ([[all-in-chip-stocks-frontier-labs-ai-pacing-letter]]). Thompson notes that Anthropic's pacing push arrived just as OpenAI retook the frontier ([[frontier-overhangs-anthropic-pacing-strategy]]), and Newport reads the essay as asking government to restrain Anthropic's competitors ([[newport-congressional-investigation-ai-labs]]).

## The Anti-Regulation Camp and Its Incentives

The opposite camp has its own conflicts of interest. Huang calls speed versus safety "a false choice" and wants companies to handle safety themselves; TechCrunch countered with the CrowdStrike outage, Meta's $18B child-safety settlement, and Nvidia's dependence on unconstrained AI demand ([[nvidia-jensen-huang-ai-regulation-dreamforce]]). House Speaker Mike Johnson rejects moratoriums and "hyper regulation" in favor of self-policing, against polls showing majority support for federal AI oversight ([[mike-johnson-ai-data-center-chinese-psyop]]). Huang's alignment with Trump against pacing mirrors the capture critique from the other side: Nvidia sells the GPUs that any slowdown would idle ([[nvidia-jensen-huang-trump-all-in-summit-ai-slowdown]]).

## Regulate Outcomes, at the Right Level

IBM's Gary Cohn wants to regulate what AI does rather than the technology itself, through existing sector regulators like financial regulators and the FDA instead of a new agency, and he warns that 50 separate state regimes would weaken the US against a centrally directed China ([[gary-cohn-ibm-ai-oversight-outcome-based-regulation]]). Reza Chowdhury draws the same line in New York. NYC's hiring-tool law, Local Law 144, regulates one local use, while model-level validation regulates the technology and belongs at the state or federal level, because rules splinter when every city answers "where did the activity occur?" differently ([[nyc-council-ai-regulation-bill-package-menin]]).

## New York City's Ten-Bill Package

Speaker Julie Menin's package would decide whether private AI can be sold or deployed in NYC at all. Intro 2602 requires third-party validation of data, bias, outputs, privacy and security, plus a kill switch, with a $25,000 penalty per instance that counts each agent in a swarm separately. Companion bills add foreseeable-harm liability, disclosure rules and whistleblower bounties, all on top of the state's RAISE Act and the AG's AI-worker whistleblower portal. The broad definition of "deploy" could catch clinics and retailers using third-party AI, and Google can absorb costs a 14-person startup cannot, so vendors may simply block NYC customers ([[nyc-council-ai-regulation-bill-package-menin]]).

## States Draw the Line in Health Care

Health care is where state AI law moves fastest, part of the 84 new AI laws passed in 27 states in the first half of 2026 ([[ai-healthcare-legislation-2026-mid-year]]). Seven states now require a licensed clinician to approve AI-driven insurance denials, and Washington bars AI from being the sole basis for denying or delaying care. Five more states banned commercial AI therapy chatbots, following Illinois and Nevada, and Iowa requires verbal disclosure before AI records an appointment ([[ai-healthcare-laws-84-new-laws-27-states]]). The legal principle these laws share is that AI may assist licensed professionals but not replace them, applied before harms pile up rather than after.

## Schools: Moratoriums and Enforceable Contracts

NYC paused student-facing generative AI for about 600,000 students in grades 2-K through 8 and banned companion chatbots in all grades. Federal guidance only permits spending grant funds on compliant AI, and COPPA and FERPA still govern student data ([[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]]). Microsoft's ten principles, agreed with the AFT and UFT, ban training on student data, prohibit AI companions and require human review of consequential decisions ([[microsoft-school-ai-guardrails-enforceable]]). They are enforceable only through each district's contract, though, not as a law covering every vendor ([[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]]).

## The EU AI Act's Transparency Rules

Article 50 of the EU AI Act took effect on August 2. Chatbots must disclose that they are AI, and AI-generated content must carry machine-readable labels, with fines up to €15M or 3% of global turnover. Because the rules cover any provider serving EU users, they work as a GDPR-like global baseline, and a voluntary Code of Practice offers a recognized way to comply ([[eu-ai-act-transparency-rules-august-2026]]). Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft and OpenAI have all committed to that code ([[openai-textgrain-chatgpt-text-watermark-eu]]).

## Watermarks: Global Default or Regional Patch

The two leading labs complied in different ways. Anthropic rolled out a SynthID-style text watermark worldwide because regional scoping isn't yet technically durable, and stressed that it doesn't change who owns or is legally responsible for an output ([[how-claudes-text-watermark-works]]). OpenAI's textGrain covers only the EU at launch; the company had shelved an earlier watermark for fear users would defect, and the new one is fragile: swapping 10% of words for synonyms cut detection from about 92% to 66%. Adoption went only as far as the binding rule required ([[openai-textgrain-chatgpt-text-watermark-eu]]).

## Congress, Courts and Prosecutors

So far, the checks that bite come from Congress, the courts and state AGs rather than federal regulators. Sanders quoted the labs' own promises to halt development once thresholds were crossed and warned the Senate would act ([[sanders-letter-pause-ai-development-aug2026]]); the escape disclosures behind his letter also drew state AG investigations ([[ai-models-escaping-control-openai-anthropic-meta-aug2026]]). Newport calls for a congressional fact-finding investigation of OpenAI and Anthropic specifically, not of "AI" as a whole ([[newport-congressional-investigation-ai-labs]]). A federal judge struck down the Pentagon's "supply chain risk" label on Anthropic as First Amendment retaliation for refusing to loosen weapons and surveillance guardrails ([[anthropic-pentagon-supply-chain-risk-court-win]]), and Amodei's first dinner with Trump came while that litigation was still running ([[dario-amodei-trump-white-house-dinner]]).

## Data Centers as a Regulatory Front

The most concrete AI restrictions so far target data centers, not models. Governor Hochul's statewide order halts permits for new large facilities, and Denver, Indianapolis, Asheville, Charlotte and Reno have adopted or proposed moratoriums ([[ai-data-center-community-opposition-philadelphia]]); the All-In hosts dispute each stated rationale ([[ai-self-regulation-sro-stripe-paypal-ny-datacenter]]). Johnson dismissed the opposition as a "Chinese psyop," even though bipartisan polls show local discomfort and House Republicans are drafting a Ratepayer Protection Act to make data centers pay for new generation ([[mike-johnson-ai-data-center-chinese-psyop]]). Both sides are spending on the midterms: the a16z- and Brockman-backed Leading the Future, with more than $50M, plans swing-state data-center ads, and Anthropic backs the pro-regulation Public First Action ([[a16z-brockman-ltf-data-center-ads-midterms]]).

**T3. Unilateral restraint cedes ground to rivals** — When competitors are racing for relative position, a costly restraint taken alone hands advantage to those who don't take it.
