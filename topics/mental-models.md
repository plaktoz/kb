# Mental Models

Mental models are deliberate simplifications for navigating a complex world, and the vault now holds a growing lattice of them (forward and backward reasoning tools, bias diagnostics, decision protocols and systems lenses) that reinforce and check one another.

## What Mental Models Are

Every mental model is a map: it compresses messy territory into something navigable, but it throws away detail and carries its maker's blind spots, so the core discipline is knowing where each map stops working, the way physicists know exactly where Newton gives way to Einstein [[map-is-not-the-territory]]. Jane Jacobs's critique of planners who redesigned cities to fit their models shows what happens when you forget this: the abstraction starts reshaping, and damaging, the reality it was meant to describe. [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] invokes George Box's view that all models are false yet some are useful, and treats them as tools in a toolbox, where judgment about which one suits the situation matters as much as owning it.

## Why Models Compound

Durable frameworks are worth building because they compound: most consumed information expires within months while slow-changing knowledge builds on itself, and internalized knowledge can be combined on the spot when a decision requires weighing dozens of factors at once, which searchable knowledge cannot [[compounding-knowledge]]. Buffett's 70-plus years of accumulated reading is the vault's canonical case, and [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] sharpens it: his and Munger's edge came from applying existing models from psychology, competitive strategy and incentives more consistently than the researchers who described them, not from inventing new ideas. The same logic favors depth over volume, which is why Parrish cut back from 140-plus books a year once he saw that finishing books was crowding out synthesis. [[mental-models-9-lenses-strategic-leaders-c3-cycle]] turns compounding into a schedule by adding one new model per quarter, building toward a calibrated decision system over roughly a year.

## First Principles: Rebuilding from Bedrock

First principles thinking strips a problem down to its confirmed truths and rebuilds from there rather than reasoning by analogy, and the effort it demands is exactly what keeps it rare and valuable [[first-principles-thinking]]. Its best-known cases share a pattern of convention passing itself off as constraint: rocket materials costing about 2% of a rocket's retail price, BuzzFeed recognizing that sharing behavior rather than content quality drove reach, and VanMoof cutting shipping damage by 70–80% by printing a TV on its bike boxes instead of buying sturdier packaging. [[marketing-first-principles-durable-advantage]] applies the same split to marketing: platform tactics from SEO to AI are rented and periodically disrupted, while the questions of who the customer is and what the product uniquely delivers remain owned. Because the method is costly, the Impact/Time matrix in [[first-principles-thinking]] saves it for high-stakes problems where time is available and sends urgent high-impact calls to faster, proven models.

## Second-Order Thinking: "And Then What?"

Second-order thinking keeps asking what follows the obvious first consequence, and Howard Marks sees that extra step as the gap between ordinary and exceptional results, because everyone reaches first-order conclusions [[second-order-thinking-farnam-street]]. Its practical supports (projecting outcomes at 10 minutes, 10 months and 10 years, and asking how employees, competitors, suppliers and regulators will react) target the failure [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] sees most often: stopping at the first visible effect, as with a cost-saving purchase whose savings rivals eventually compete away. The payoff is asymmetric, since paths that look bad at first but pay off later scare most people away and leave less competition. [[second-order-thinking-is-the-new-systems-thinking]] argues the skill is becoming the premium differentiator now that AI maps system architecture in seconds, and proposes asking before every decision: if this goes to plan, what becomes more likely to break that nobody is watching?

## Efficiency's Rebound: The Jevons Paradox

Jevons's 1865 observation, that more efficient coal use raised total coal consumption, is second-order thinking applied to efficiency: the saving per unit is real, but cheaper use invites more use until the growth in total consumption outweighs the saving [[jevons-paradox-dark-side-ai-labor-market]]. Newport's hopeful reading for AI bets that this rebound works in labor's favor, with cheaper AI output expanding demand for services like software development, but his darker cases run the other way: steam-engine efficiency fed industrial pollution, and frictionless email and Slack left workers interrupted roughly every two minutes. The same shape, a gain absorbed by how people respond to it, appears as risk compensation in [[mental-model-redundancy]], where trusted safety backups encourage bolder risks, and in [[second-order-thinking-is-the-new-systems-thinking]], where an AI coding assistant's 40% productivity boost quietly erodes early-career engineers' debugging instincts. In systems terms the messaging case resembles a Fix That Fails, and [[systems-thinking-twenty-tools-complete-course]] supplies the test: after years of applying the fix, is the original problem actually smaller?

## Thinking Backward: Inversion and Chesterton's Fence

Inversion attacks a problem from the failure side, naming what would guarantee a bad outcome and eliminating it, on Munger's premise that steadily avoiding stupidity is more achievable than chasing brilliance [[inversion-avoiding-stupidity]]. Chesterton's Fence points that backward look at existing structures: a gate whose purpose you cannot see should stay until you learn why it was built, because habits, norms and hierarchies often solve problems that are no longer visible to newcomers [[chestertons-fence-a-lesson-in-thinking]]. Both Chesterton notes stress that this asks for understanding rather than defending the status quo, and they lean on Will Durant's estimate that ninety-nine of a hundred new ideas are worse than the traditions they would replace [[chestertons-fence-understand-before-removing]]. That creates a productive tension with [[first-principles-thinking]], which questions every default, and sequencing resolves it: first learn why the fence exists, then reason from first principles about whether it should stay.

## Subtraction and the Vasa Effect

Inversion's preference for removal counters a measurable bias: in Leidy Klotz's experiments only 41% of people chose a subtractive fix by default, and only 61% did even when reminded that removal cost nothing [[vasa-effect-subtract-bias]]. The 1628 warship *Vasa* shows how that bias compounds into structural failure, since it capsized after a string of individually reasonable additions (decks, guns, carvings) left it top-heavy. [[inversion-avoiding-stupidity]] supplies the rationale for resisting the bias, because removing obstacles carries less risk of unintended harm than adding interventions. The Vasa note also turns Chesterton's Fence around: if you should know why a fence exists before removing it, you should equally ask why something deserves to be added before building it, a check that the habit of adding skips entirely.

## Knowing Your Limits: Circle of Competence and Anchoring

The circle of competence holds that the boundary of your earned knowledge matters more than its size, because outside it your edge passes to better-informed rivals while your confidence falls more slowly than your competence [[circle-of-competence]]. Buffett and Munger guard that boundary through their information diet, ignoring fast-changing sectors they cannot forecast, and the Feynman Technique of explaining an idea plainly on paper as if to a novice offers a quick test of where the boundary really lies [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]]. Staying inside the circle does not make you immune to bias, though: anchors work by selective accessibility, priming evidence that agrees with the anchor so counter-evidence is harder to recall, and although domain knowledge weakens irrelevant anchors, experts remain susceptible [[mental-model-anchoring]]. Self-awareness therefore needs a structural backstop, such as the rule in [[decide-framework-decision-making-template]] that criteria be fixed before any options are generated.

## Ignoring the Denominator: Base Rates and Survivorship

Kahneman and Tversky, who first documented anchoring, also devised the classic base-rate trap: given a personality sketch of "Steve" that fits the librarian stereotype, most people guess librarian over salesperson, even though the US has roughly 140,000 librarians and more than 13 million salespeople [[everywhere-millionaire-illusion-base-rate-neglect]]. Hernandez argues that the "Everywhere Millionaire" story makes the mirror-image error by celebrating survivors like Dick Portillo, whose business lasted fifty years when only about a third of businesses reach ten, without counting everyone who tried the same path and failed. In both errors, as with the anchors in [[mental-model-anchoring]], whatever comes readily to mind (a stereotype, a vivid success story) stands in for the real population, and the fix pairs the probabilistic-thinking lens of [[mental-models-9-lenses-strategic-leaders-c3-cycle]] (explicit best, base and worst cases with expected values) with a decision journal that judges reasoning rather than results [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]]. This also qualifies Bezos's rule in [[map-is-not-the-territory]] that anecdotes usually win when they contradict the data: his hold-time call checked a metric against one system he could observe directly, whereas a survivor story gets used to estimate odds across a population it never sampled.

## Strategic Interdependence: Game Theory

Game theory covers decisions that one-player analysis cannot: two or more players, each with several strategies, whose choices jointly set everyone's payoff, so each must anticipate rivals who are anticipating them [[mental-model-game-theory]]. It formalizes the business lens of [[second-order-thinking-farnam-street]], which asks how competitors and suppliers will respond, and explains why the cost-saving purchase in [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] loses its advantage once rivals react. Its home ground is oligopoly, where firms would collectively do best acting like a single monopolist yet each is tempted to defect, the classic prisoners' dilemma. Pure competition and monopoly need no game theory, because in one no single rival matters and in the other none exists.

## Leverage Points: Redundancy and Activation Energy

Redundancy and activation energy tackle the question of where to apply force from opposite ends: redundancy multiplies safety by requiring every path to fail (two independent circuits with a 1-in-10 failure rate each fail together only 1 time in 100), but it backfires through common-mode failures, shirking among human backups, and risk compensation [[mental-model-redundancy]]. The Challenger launch combined common-mode failure with risk compensation, as confidence in the secondary O-ring led officials to discount cold temperatures that could degrade both rings at once. Activation energy works the other end: the hard part of a behavior is the starting threshold rather than the sustained effort, so a single catalyst, such as an incentive or a change to the environment, can unlock lasting change more cheaply than repeated motivation [[activation-energy-mental-model]]. This matches the advice in [[systems-thinking-twenty-tools-complete-course]] that balancing loops respond to a changed set point, not to willpower.

## Structuring the Decision: DECIDE and the Decision Journal

The DECIDE framework opens with a reversibility test: irreversible "one-way door" choices earn a heavyweight sequence of defining the problem, fixing criteria before generating options (including doing nothing), naming one accountable decider via RAPID and documenting the reasoning, while reversible "two-way door" choices should move faster [[decide-framework-decision-making-template]]. The written record also enforces commitment, because dissenters who never explicitly "disagree and commit" tend to resurface later as half-hearted execution. Parrish's decision journal extends documentation to learning by recording the variables and expected range of outcomes before committing, so the reasoning, not just the result, can later be checked against what happened [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]].

## Running Models as a System: The C³ Cycle

[[mental-models-9-lenses-strategic-leaders-c3-cycle]] diagnoses stalled high performers as having a leverage problem rather than an effort problem: they default to one lens (momentum, intuition or industry convention) for every decision. It packages nine models into the Clarity phase of its Clarity–Command–Compound cycle, each with a three-minute protocol to run before deciding, and pairs them by bottleneck: inversion with margin of safety for scaling risk, Pareto with opportunity cost for strategic influence, and second-order with probabilistic thinking for execution under uncertainty. The pairing logic echoes the toolbox framing of [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] and the Impact/Time matrix in [[first-principles-thinking]], which both insist on choosing a model deliberately rather than by habit. The guide also includes Hanlon's Razor, which means blaming poor systems or miscommunication before malice and helps prevent cycles of retaliation.

## Systems Thinking and Structural Leverage

[[systems-thinking-twenty-tools-complete-course]] treats every recurring problem as the product of a structure (a draining stock, a loop sustaining the drain, a delay hiding the connection, and a lever that was never the strong one), and its Valve Rule makes intervention concrete: "get in shape" is an order shouted at a stock, while "walk Tuesday and Thursday lunchtimes" is a hand on a flow. Donella Meadows's Leverage Ladder exposes the usual mistake, since new apps and numeric targets sit on the lowest rung while changes to information flows, rules or the underlying paradigm multiply the effect with each rung, and named traps such as Shifting the Burden, Fixes That Fail and Tragedy of the Commons let you recognize the one you are in before choosing a countermeasure. The Vasa's loss reads as a systems story too, because every addition widened the ship's failure surface until complexity built up non-linearly and one trigger was enough [[vasa-effect-subtract-bias]]. Jacobs's planners in [[map-is-not-the-territory]] fit the course's premise that people act rationally on what they can see locally, which means well-meaning actors working from their model can still cause harm the model never shows.
