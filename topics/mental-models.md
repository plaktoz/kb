# Mental Models

Mental models are deliberate simplifications that let you navigate a complex world — and the vault now holds a growing lattice of named frameworks, each one a node in a shared reasoning infrastructure.

## What Mental Models Are (and Why They Compound)

Every mental model is a map: it compresses a messy territory into something navigable, but it is never the territory itself [[map-is-not-the-territory]]. The danger is mistaking the abstraction for reality — as city planners who redesign neighborhoods to fit their models discover when their interventions harm the actual street life they were modeling. What makes this infrastructure worth building is that durable frameworks compound over time: unlike most consumed information, which expires within months, deeply retained models build on prior understanding, enabling the simultaneous integration of dozens of signals that no search query can replicate [[compounding-knowledge]]. Warren Buffett's edge was not access to information others lacked — it was 70+ years of retained, slowly-changing knowledge that made each new fact add to a coherent whole rather than compete with it. [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] reinforces this: Munger and Buffett's real competitive advantage was applying *existing* mental models — psychology, competitive strategy, incentive design — more rigorously and consistently than most people, including the original researchers who described those models. Depth of reading matters more than volume by the same logic: reading 140 books a year for the sake of finishing means shallow synthesis, while deeply retaining 20 changes how you think.

## Thinking Forward: First Principles and Second-Order Consequences

Two models govern the forward direction of reasoning. First principles thinking dismantles a problem to its irreducible, confirmed truths and rebuilds from scratch rather than by analogy [[first-principles-thinking]]; Elon Musk's insight that rocket materials cost 2% of retail price was invisible to anyone reasoning from "rockets are expensive" as a baseline assumption. The same logic extends beyond engineering — marketers who reason from first principles identify that distribution behavior, not content quality, drives reach, and that platforms are just rented tactics layered on top of durable customer-and-value fundamentals [[marketing-first-principles-durable-advantage]]. Second-order thinking asks "and then what?" after a decision, tracing consequence chains past the obvious first outcome [[second-order-thinking-farnam-street]]; Howard Marks frames it as the difference between ordinary performance and extraordinary, because first-order outcomes are visible to everyone. [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] treats this as a distinct, underused discipline: most decision failures stem not from missing information but from stopping analysis at the first visible effect — what looks like a cost-saving purchase whose savings eventually get competed away. As AI commoditizes the ability to map system architecture in seconds, second-order thinking is emerging as the scarcer, more differentiated skill, because reasoning about behavioral consequences of a decision chain remains computationally hard to delegate [[second-order-thinking-is-the-new-systems-thinking]].

## Thinking Backward: Inversion and Chesterton's Fence

Inversion reframes problems by working backward from failure: rather than asking how to achieve a goal, ask what would cause failure and eliminate those conditions [[inversion-avoiding-stupidity]]. Charlie Munger's formulation — "avoiding stupidity is easier than achieving brilliance" — captures why the subtractive approach is more reliable than the additive one; removing known obstacles creates less risk of unintended harm than engineering novel solutions. Chesterton's Fence is inversion applied to existing structures: before removing a gate, understand why it was built, because its function may be invisible to anyone who arrived after the problem it solved [[chestertons-fence-a-lesson-in-thinking]]. Will Durant's reinforcement — that roughly 99% of new ideas are inferior to the traditions they replace — is the empirical shadow of this principle. Both notes in the vault on Chesterton's Fence converge on the same rule: the reformer who cannot see the use of a fence should not be permitted to remove it [[chestertons-fence-understand-before-removing]]. This creates a productive tension with first principles thinking: first principles demands that you question every default assumption, while Chesterton's Fence demands that you understand why defaults were adopted before dismantling them. The resolution is sequencing — understand the fence before applying first principles to decide whether it should be torn down.

## Knowing Your Limits: Circle of Competence and Anchoring

The circle of competence model asserts that the boundary of your earned knowledge matters more than its size — operating outside it transfers your edge to better-informed competitors, while confidence does not shrink as quickly as actual competence when you cross the perimeter [[circle-of-competence]]. [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] connects this to information diet: Buffett and Munger deliberately ignore fast-moving sectors where they cannot reliably forecast outcomes, staying inside a circle of slow-changing industries where 70 years of compounded knowledge gives them genuine advantage. The Feynman Technique is a rigorous self-test for circle boundaries: explaining a concept plainly on paper as if teaching a novice — an inability to do so simply flags a gap in real understanding rather than an indexing gap. Anchoring complicates the picture: even holding a relevant mental model does not fully protect against cognitive biases, because anchors work through selective accessibility — they prime anchor-consistent information to flood working memory, making counter-evidence less retrievable, and domain expertise only reduces susceptibility to *irrelevant* anchors, not domain-relevant ones [[mental-model-anchoring]]. The implication is that knowing you have a circle of competence does not make you immune to anchoring *within* that circle; structural safeguards (written pre-commitments, reference-class data) are required in addition to self-awareness.

## Strategic Interdependence: Game Theory

Game theory governs a class of decisions that first-principles and second-order thinking cannot fully handle: situations where multiple parties are simultaneously trying to anticipate each other [[mental-model-game-theory]]. The formal definition requires at least two players, each with multiple possible strategies, where chosen strategies *jointly* determine outcomes — meaning any individual optimization is incomplete without modeling how rivals respond to your move. This is the core error in one-player analysis: treating a competitor's behavior as a fixed environmental input rather than as a strategic response shaped by what they observe you doing. The prisoners'-dilemma structure pervades oligopoly: firms are collectively best off cooperating like a monopolist, yet individually incentivized to defect — the tension game theory makes explicit and any durable competitive strategy must account for. Game theory is unnecessary in pure competition (no single firm matters enough to elicit response) or monopoly (no rivals to strategize against); it is essential precisely where a few identifiable actors can all see and respond to each other.

## Leverage Points: Redundancy and Activation Energy

Redundancy and activation energy address the same question from opposite sides: where should you apply force in a system? Redundancy multiplies safety by requiring all paths to fail simultaneously, but carries three backfire mechanisms — common-mode failures where added complexity causes all components to collapse together, social shirking where human redundancy diffuses responsibility, and risk compensation where actors take bolder risks precisely because they trust the backup [[mental-model-redundancy]]. NASA's Challenger decision illustrates all three: the secondary O-ring was trusted as a backup, which allowed the launch to proceed in conditions that would degrade both O-rings simultaneously. Activation energy is the reciprocal leverage: rather than adding redundant safeguards, identify the minimum energy threshold for a desired behavior and lower it through catalysts [[activation-energy-mental-model]]. The barrier exists only at the starting threshold, not throughout the behavior — so a single targeted friction-reducer (one incentive, one environmental tweak) can unlock a sustained behavior change at much lower total cost than repeated motivational effort.

## Decision Process and Discipline

A distinct cluster of models governs *how* to structure a decision rather than *what* to decide. The DECIDE framework [[decide-framework-decision-making-template]] starts from a gate question: is this decision reversible? Irreversible "one-way door" choices justify a heavyweight structured process — define the problem, establish criteria *before* generating options (to preserve objectivity against anchoring), assign a single named decider via the RAPID framework, and document reasoning so that dissenters can "disagree and commit" rather than silently under-execute. Reversible "two-way door" choices warrant a lighter, faster process because the cost of a wrong decision is recoupable. Setting criteria before generating options is the critical discipline — evaluating an option first anchors the criteria to that option rather than the actual problem. [[shane-parrish-mental-models-decision-making-interview]] reinforces the documentation principle with the Decision Journal: recording the variables considered and the expected range of outcomes *before* committing creates an audit trail that exposes reasoning quality rather than just outcome quality — essential because good decisions can have bad outcomes, and outcome-only feedback causes regression toward survival-biased reasoning.
