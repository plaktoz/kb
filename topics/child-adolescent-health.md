# Child and Adolescent Health

This topic follows what the vault knows about the physical and mental health of children and teenagers, from how adversity and early-life exposures shape lifelong biology, to which everyday habits matter most, to why diseases once reserved for adults now need pediatric attention, and this week's Australian cohort extends the habits thread to what teenagers eat and drink.

## Adversity Leaves Measurable Biological Marks

A UCLA-led ENIGMA mega-analysis of 3,711 people found that more severe childhood abuse and neglect track with deviations from normal brain structure, a "neurobiological scar" in regions such as the thalamus, pallidum and cingulate cortex [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Chronic stress also shows up in aging markers: among roughly 1,800 15-year-olds, living near a deadly shooting in the prior year was tied to about two and a half months of extra biological age acceleration, independent of whether a teen had been directly victimized [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]]. The common thread is that a child's surroundings can leave physical wear whether or not the child is directly harmed, although the brain-scan authors caution that such markers say nothing about any individual's abilities.

## Adversity Is Not One Thing

The sources in this topic treat adversity as a family of distinct exposures rather than a single dose. Household unpredictability, such as irregular routines, parental mood swings and unstable relationships, is a separate type from abuse or neglect, and in a study of roughly 30,000 children, a screen for it predicted a 12-fold depression risk against 8-fold for a traditional adversity screen, with the two screens together detecting risk best [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]. Within maltreatment, abuse and neglect left different structural patterns that varied by age and sex and were strongest in young women [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Neighborhood gun violence adds another dimension: it fell disproportionately on Black and Hispanic teens, yet its biological effect held across races [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]].

## Early-Life Windows Cast Long Shadows

A natural experiment built on the UK's abrupt end of sugar rationing in 1953 linked less sugar in the first 1,000 days of life to lower adult dementia, Alzheimer's, depression and anxiety risk, along with brain-imaging differences and a lasting lower preference for sweets [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]]. Later reports of the same research put the effect sizes differently, and outside experts cautioned that the Alzheimer's figure rests on relatively few cases while the mood findings look more robust. Famine studies found that exposure in the womb sped up epigenetic aging decades later, and the maltreatment mega-analysis makes a parallel argument about childhood, when major neural networks are still forming [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]] [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Researchers stress that this does not make health fixed at birth, since dietary changes at later stages still produce measurable improvements.

## Screen Time: Smaller and More Specific Than It Looks

A Karolinska study following more than 21,000 Swedish twins found that shared genes and upbringing explain much of the link between heavy screen use and later depression or anxiety [[twin-study-screen-time-mental-health-family-factors]]. Interactive use such as gaming and chatting still carried some risk, while passive TV or video watching showed little. Separately, the ABCD-based UC Davis analysis found that screen time explained only 5–6.3% of the path from mental-health symptoms to worse school and social outcomes, far less than sleep [[adolescent-sleep-academic-social-outcomes]]. The twin authors argue that guidance should focus on what young people do on screens and why, not only on how many hours they spend.

## Sleep, Routine and Meals as Everyday Levers

The most actionable findings in the vault involve ordinary daily structure. Sleep quality was the strongest of four lifestyle factors linking adolescent anxiety, depression and psychotic-like symptoms to poorer grades and social functioning, explaining 36.3% of the anxiety association, and it is something families can change directly [[adolescent-sleep-academic-social-outcomes]]. The unpredictability research frames its goal as more consistent routines, like a regular bedtime, rather than a "perfect home" [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]. On meals, a six-month trial found that habitual breakfast-skippers who started eating a higher-protein breakfast took in more fiber, iron, zinc and choline and less added sugar without eating more total calories [[breakfast-boosts-diet-quality-teens]].

## Fruit, Sugary Drinks and Teenage Mood

A three-year Australian study of 6,537 adolescents, one of this week's additions, linked each extra daily serving of fruit (150 g) to 13% lower depression and 7% lower anxiety risk on screening scales, while higher sugar-sweetened beverage intake tracked with greater risk in both sexes [[fruit-sugary-drinks-adolescent-anxiety-depression-australia-study]]. Vegetables showed no significant association in adjusted models, and the sugary-drink links were more consistent than the fruit links, which partly dropped out in complete-case analyses. The pathways proposed to explain the sugary-drink link, inflammation and cortisol, were never tested. Because diet was self-reported and the design observational, the study can support school and policy efforts that target eating habits but cannot show that dietary change prevents depression or anxiety.

## Pediatric Obesity and the GLP-1 Shift

Pharmacological treatment of childhood obesity is growing fast. A *Pediatrics* study of more than 3.5 million children found that GLP-1 use among non-diabetic 8-to-11-year-olds rose from 0.3% to 9.3% between 2019 and 2026, although FDA approval for weight management starts at age 12 [[glp1-use-children-8-11-pediatrics-study]]. Clinicians increasingly treat pediatric obesity as a chronic multi-organ disease and are moving away from "wait and watch," but they make medication conditional on intensive lifestyle treatment and monitor growth, puberty, muscle mass, nutrition and mental health, because BMI is only one measure and "calories don't equate nutrients." That nutrient-versus-calorie distinction echoes the breakfast trial, where food quality rather than quantity drove the benefit [[breakfast-boosts-diet-quality-teens]].

## Adult Diseases Arriving Early

Conditions once thought of as adult problems now appear in pediatric clinics. A review of 84 colorectal cancer patients aged 21 or younger found that most were diagnosed at Stage III or IV and only 20% had a family history; more than a third relapsed and nearly half died, partly because symptoms such as rectal bleeding and weight loss were overlooked at that age [[early-onset-colorectal-cancer-adolescents-late-diagnosis]]. Children with obesity can similarly present with prediabetes, fatty liver disease, high blood pressure and sleep apnea [[glp1-use-children-8-11-pediatrics-study]]. Both notes argue against waiting: the cancer authors want young patients' symptoms taken seriously and coordinated pediatric-adult care, while obesity specialists reject "wait and watch" once complications appear.
