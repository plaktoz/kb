# Child and Adolescent Health

This topic follows what the vault knows about the physical and mental health of children and teenagers: how adversity and early-life exposures shape lifelong biology, which everyday habits matter most, and why diseases once reserved for adults now need pediatric attention.

## Adversity Leaves Measurable Biological Marks

A UCLA-led ENIGMA mega-analysis of 3,711 people found that more severe abuse and neglect track with deviations from normative brain charts, a "neurobiological scar" in regions such as the thalamus, pallidum and cingulate cortex [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Community-level trauma registers too: 15-year-olds living near a deadly shooting in the prior year showed about two and a half months of extra epigenetic age acceleration and a 5.3% faster pace of aging, even without being victims themselves [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]]. The common thread is that chronic stress around a child builds up physical wear whether or not the child is directly harmed, though the brain-scan authors caution that these markers say nothing about any one person's abilities.

## Adversity Is Not One Thing

The vault's sources agree that "adversity" breaks into distinct exposures with distinct effects. Household unpredictability, meaning irregular routines, parental mood swings and unstable relationships, is a separate adversity type from abuse or neglect, and screening for it predicted a 12-fold depression risk against 8-fold for traditional adversity screens, with the two screens combined working best [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]. Within maltreatment itself, abuse and neglect left different structural patterns that varied by age and sex and were strongest in young women [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. The gun-violence study adds neighborhood exposure as yet another dimension, and it fell disproportionately on Black and Hispanic teens even though the biological effect held across races [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]].

## Early-Life Windows Cast Long Shadows

In a natural experiment built on the UK's 1953 end of sugar rationing, less sugar in the first 1,000 days of life was linked to lower adult dementia, Alzheimer's, depression and anxiety risk, along with brain-imaging differences and a lasting lower preference for sweets. Related famine studies found that womb exposure specifically sped up epigenetic aging decades later [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]]. The maltreatment mega-analysis makes the same argument about childhood plasticity, since major neural networks are still forming when trauma hits [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Researchers stress that health is not fixed at birth, but timing changes how much a given exposure counts.

## Screen Time: Smaller and More Specific Than It Looks

A Karolinska study following more than 21,000 Swedish twins found that shared genes and upbringing explain much of the link between heavy screen use and later depression or anxiety; interactive use such as gaming and chatting still carried some risk, while passive TV or video watching showed little [[twin-study-screen-time-mental-health-family-factors]]. Separately, the ABCD-based UC Davis analysis found that screen time explained only 5–6.3% of the path from mental-health symptoms to worse school and social outcomes, far less than sleep [[adolescent-sleep-academic-social-outcomes]]. Together these notes argue for asking what young people do on screens and what that use pushes aside, not just counting hours.

## Sleep, Routine and Meals as Everyday Levers

The most actionable findings in the vault involve ordinary daily structure. Sleep quality was the strongest of four lifestyle factors linking adolescent anxiety, depression and psychotic-like symptoms to functional decline, explaining 36.3% of the anxiety association, and it is something families can change directly [[adolescent-sleep-academic-social-outcomes]]. The unpredictability research frames its goal as more consistent routines, like a regular bedtime, rather than a "perfect home" [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]. On diet, a six-month trial found that habitual breakfast-skippers who started eating a higher-protein breakfast took in more fiber, iron, zinc and choline and less added sugar without eating more total calories [[breakfast-boosts-diet-quality-teens]].

## Pediatric Obesity and the GLP-1 Shift

Pharmacological treatment of childhood obesity is growing fast. A *Pediatrics* study of more than 3.5 million children found that GLP-1 use among non-diabetic 8-to-11-year-olds rose from 0.3% to 9.3% between 2019 and 2026, although FDA approval for weight management starts at age 12 [[glp1-use-children-8-11-pediatrics-study]]. Clinicians increasingly treat pediatric obesity as a chronic multi-organ disease and are moving away from "wait and watch," but they make medication conditional on intensive lifestyle treatment and monitor growth, puberty, muscle mass, nutrition and mental health, because BMI is only one measure and "calories don't equate nutrients." That nutrient-versus-calorie distinction echoes the breakfast trial, where food quality rather than quantity drove the benefit [[breakfast-boosts-diet-quality-teens]].

## Adult Diseases Arriving Early

Conditions once thought of as adult problems now appear in pediatric clinics. A review of 84 colorectal cancer patients aged 21 or younger found that most were diagnosed at Stage III or IV and only 20% had a family history; more than a third relapsed and nearly half died, partly because symptoms like rectal bleeding and weight loss were dismissed as unlikely at that age [[early-onset-colorectal-cancer-adolescents-late-diagnosis]]. Children with obesity similarly present with prediabetes, fatty liver disease, high blood pressure and sleep apnea [[glp1-use-children-8-11-pediatrics-study]]. Both notes argue against waiting: the cancer authors want young patients' symptoms taken seriously and coordinated pediatric-adult care, while obesity specialists reject "wait and watch" once complications appear.

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Developmental timing amplifies exposure** — Inputs that arrive while biological systems are still forming (in utero, infancy, childhood) leave larger and more lasting effects than the same inputs later in life. · *model:* [[Critical Period Hypothesis]]
→ explains: [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]], [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]], [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]]

**T2. Chronic stress accumulates as physiological wear** — Ongoing adversity in a child's environment imposes measurable biological cost even without direct injury, and different kinds of adversity cause different kinds of damage. · *model:* [[Allostatic Load]]
→ explains: [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]], [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]], [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]

**T3. Shared causes inflate raw associations** — When family, genetic or social factors drive both an exposure and an outcome, the observed link overstates the exposure's own effect until those factors are held constant. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]
→ explains: [[twin-study-screen-time-mental-health-family-factors]], [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]], [[gun-violence-exposure-accelerates-biological-aging]]

**T4. A low prior delays action on real cases** — Treating youth as presumptively healthy makes inaction feel safe, so progressing disease advances before it is acted on, and the cost grows as the true rate rises. · *model:* [[Base Rate Neglect]]
→ explains: [[early-onset-colorectal-cancer-adolescents-late-diagnosis]], [[glp1-use-children-8-11-pediatrics-study]]

**T5. Gross quantity metrics misrepresent health** — Totals such as screen hours, calories or BMI hide the composition and context that determine effect, so the same quantity can be harmless or harmful. · *model:* [[Map Is Not the Territory]]
