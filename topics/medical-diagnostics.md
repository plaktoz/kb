# Medical Diagnostics: Tests, Devices, and Diagnostic Delay

How the vault understands diagnostic tests and medical devices: how accurate they are, where bias creeps in, how testing protocols shape results, and why so much disease goes undetected. Cancer screening specifically lives in [[cancer-early-detection]].

## Device Accuracy and Measurement Bias

A device can be wrong in ways that no fixed correction can repair. A five-year Johns Hopkins study found that one make of pulse oximeter slowly moved from overestimating to underestimating blood oxygen against arterial blood gas results, with larger errors in patients of color and no known cause, while Emory findings suggest anemia, cardiovascular disease, and liver disease also distort readings, so calibrating for skin tone alone may not remove the bias ([[pulse-oximeter-accuracy-drift-hopkins-study]]). Newer tools have the same problem of unproven reliability: smartphone memory tests showed test-retest reliability ranging from 0.53 to 0.91 across measures and reported no individual sensitivity or specificity ([[smartphone-memory-tests-alzheimers-brain-changes]]). An FDA-cleared aneurysm-detection AI had higher sensitivity than radiologists but lower positive predictive value, so each one's errors were different in kind ([[ai-complements-radiologists-brain-aneurysm-detection]]).

## Testing Protocols and Their Hidden Costs

Rules for how a test is done can outlast the evidence behind them. The 8–12 hour fasting rule, originally meant for glucose and lipid testing, became the default for nearly all blood panels, yet a study of about 10 million results found that only a handful of analytes, such as glucose, triglycerides, and lipase, changed meaningfully with short fasts, and that fasting past 12 hours added nothing except discomfort, extra visits, and morning lab congestion ([[fasting-before-blood-tests-mostly-unnecessary-study]]). Diagnostic protocols can also slow detection. Oxford researchers argue that NICE's requirement of several readings to confirm hypertension, and of lifestyle changes before medication in some cases, delays control, and that NHS Health Check age limits leave out younger adults even though more than 15% of them are hypertensive ([[undiagnosed-hypertension-england-our-future-health]]).

## Less Invasive Tests Replacing Gold Standards

Several notes describe gentler tests taking over work that used to need invasive procedures. Alzheimer's amyloid pathology was once confirmed by PET scans or spinal taps, but there are now four FDA-cleared blood tests: Elecsys pTau217 can both rule amyloid pathology in and rule it out from one blood draw ([[fda-clears-first-single-biomarker-blood-test-alzheimers]]), and PrecivityAD2 reached 97.6% positive and 93.1% negative predictive value against PET and CSF ([[fda-clears-precivityad2-alzheimers-blood-test-adults-40]]). Likewise, a blood test for the pathogen-secreted protein ESAT-6 could serve patients who cannot produce sputum for tuberculosis testing ([[tuberculosis-blood-test-esat6-detection]]), and fibroblasts grown from a small skin biopsy can often diagnose mitochondrial disease without a muscle or liver biopsy ([[skin-biopsy-fibroblast-testing-mitochondrial-disease-diagnosis]]). In every case the new test is checked against the old reference standard, and several still need prospective validation before routine use.

## Measuring the Disease Instead of Estimating Risk

Tests that measure disease directly keep exposing the gaps in indirect estimates. The REACT study imaged more than 16,800 adults with no known heart disease and found plaque in 57.1% of them, including about 1 in 13 under 30, while standard risk scores flagged only a small share of those with plaque ([[silent-atherosclerosis-young-adults-react-study]]). The ESAT-6 test measures a protein made by the bacterium itself rather than the body's immune response, and its levels rose step by step from uninfected to latent to active TB ([[tuberculosis-blood-test-esat6-detection]]), and functional fibroblast testing helps resolve the variants of uncertain significance that whole-genome sequencing often returns ([[skin-biopsy-fibroblast-testing-mitochondrial-disease-diagnosis]]). Screening questionnaires show the same pattern: adding household-unpredictability measures to a standard childhood adversity screen identified depression and sleep-disorder risk that the traditional screen missed ([[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]).

## Context Determines What a Result Means

The same number can mean different things for different people or settings. Among APOE-ε4 carriers, each standard-deviation rise in p-tau217 meant a 24% shorter time to cognitive impairment, compared with 13% in non-carriers, and its predictive strength also varied by racial and ethnic group ([[ptau217-apoe-cognitive-decline-prediction]]). Aging, kidney disease, atrial fibrillation, obesity, and medications can all shift NT-proBNP levels ([[nt-probnp-blood-marker-heart-disease-risk]]), and the aneurysm AI helped most in inpatient and emergency settings but much less in outpatient care ([[ai-complements-radiologists-brain-aneurysm-detection]]). This is why the new Alzheimer's blood tests are cleared only for symptomatic adults, must be ordered by a physician, and are described as aids to a full clinical evaluation, not stand-alone diagnoses ([[fda-clears-first-single-biomarker-blood-test-alzheimers]], [[fda-clears-precivityad2-alzheimers-blood-test-adults-40]]).

## The Undiagnosed Majority and Diagnostic Delay

Much common disease is never diagnosed. In England, 59% of people with hypertension did not know they had it, and half of those on medication were still not controlled ([[undiagnosed-hypertension-england-our-future-health]]). About 62% of AFib patients are unaware of their condition before diagnosis, though retinal-layer thinning on routine eye scans could flag AFib risk up to four years early ([[eye-scan-retinal-imaging-afib-risk-prediction]]), and about 75% of dementia cases remain undiagnosed ([[fda-clears-first-single-biomarker-blood-test-alzheimers]]). Delays also come from people: one patient's PCOS took five years to diagnose because clinicians kept attributing her symptoms to lifestyle ([[pcos-pmos-name-change-diagnosis-hope]]), and Stephen Curry lived with keratoconus for years, having come to treat his distorted vision as normal, until a non-clinician noticed him squinting on TV ([[steph-curry-keratoconus-eye-disease-awareness]]).

## Detection Is Not the Same as Better Outcomes

Finding more disease does not by itself show that patients do better. The aneurysm AI found 55 aneurysms that radiologists had missed, but the study could not show that this improved outcomes ([[ai-complements-radiologists-brain-aneurysm-detection]]). NT-proBNP predicts cardiovascular events, but lowering it has not been shown to reduce risk, and there is no evidence that screening for it helps ([[nt-probnp-blood-marker-heart-disease-risk]]). The REACT authors say their results call for attention to heart health rather than screening everyone, and the study's next phase will test whether earlier detection actually improves prevention ([[silent-atherosclerosis-young-adults-react-study]]).

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. A result is only as reliable as its check against a reference standard** — Every test or device estimates a truth that has to be confirmed by a gold standard in the people it is used on. **T5. Silent conditions stay undiagnosed until something prompts a test** — Disease with no symptoms, or with symptoms that get normalized or dismissed, goes undetected by default.
