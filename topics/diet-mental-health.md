# Diet and Mental Health

The relationship between diet and mental health has moved from peripheral observation to a recognized research field, nutritional psychiatry and metabolic psychiatry, with mounting evidence that what people eat shapes mood, stress tolerance and the course of serious psychiatric conditions. The vault's notes now reach from adults with depression and schizophrenia to a three-year cohort of teenagers, but the evidence is still mostly associational, with a few trials and proposed mechanisms beginning to test it.

## Mechanisms: How Food Reaches the Brain

Three biological pathways are proposed. Inflammation comes first: [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] links ultra-processed foods, which make up roughly half of the average American diet, to chronic inflammation, and Eric Verdin cites that inflammation as the mechanism by which poor diet impairs brain function. The gut-brain axis is the second, since [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] notes that gut bacteria produce about 90% of the body's serotonin, so microbiome composition may feed directly into mood. Nutrient availability is the third, as [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]] ties dieting-related shortfalls in B vitamins, iron, omega-3s and complex carbohydrates to the neurochemistry that regulates mood.

## Dietary Patterns and Mood Outcomes

The Mediterranean pattern has the strongest evidence base. [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] cites a 32% reduced depression risk associated with Mediterranean eating across studies, and the SMILES trial found that dietary counseling toward that pattern produced 32% depression remission versus 8% with social support. [[mediterranean-diet-reduces-perceived-stress]] extends the picture to stress: in a Binghamton study of more than 1,500 people, Mediterranean components were associated with lower perceived stress and mental distress, while Western components such as red meat, refined grains and processed foods went the other way. That study also reversed the usual direction of inquiry, asking how diet shapes stress perception rather than how stress shapes food choices.

## Adolescent Mood and Sugary Drinks

[[fruit-sugary-drinks-adolescent-anxiety-depression-australia-study]] extends the diet-mood link to young people. Following 6,537 Australian adolescents for three years, the Health4Life cohort found that each extra daily fruit serving was linked to 13% lower depression risk and 7% lower anxiety risk, while more sugar-sweetened beverages were linked to greater risk in both sexes, and vegetables showed no significant association. The sugary-drink signal was more consistent than the fruit signal, which lost some associations in complete-case analyses, and the inflammation and cortisol pathways the authors propose remain untested. Self-reported diet and unmeasured confounding mean the findings cannot show that changing diet prevents these disorders, though the note suggests school programs could target diet if the link is confirmed.

## Metabolic Psychiatry: Diet as Clinical Intervention

[[metabolism-mental-health-keto-schizophrenia]] describes metabolic psychiatry as a clinical field that studies measured ketogenic metabolic therapy for serious psychiatric conditions, distinct from keto as a weight-loss fad. The field traces to a 2009 case observed by Stanford physician Shebani Sethi, in which a patient's schizophrenia symptoms improved on a ketogenic intervention after years of failed medication. [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] widens the frame: speakers at Food Tank's Capitol Hill Luncheon cited ketogenic approaches for bipolar disorder and argued for a food-is-medicine model that would redirect the 90% of $4.5 trillion in US healthcare spending that goes to chronic disease toward prevention. The emerging view treats the brain as dependent on systemic metabolism, shaped by inflammation, nutrient supply and energy substrates, not only by neurotransmitter chemistry.

## Restriction Risks: When Dieting Harms Mood

Dieting can work against mood when it degrades nutrient adequacy. In a cross-sectional analysis of 28,525 NHANES adults, calorie-restricted dieters scored 0.29 points higher on the PHQ-9 depression scale than comparison groups, 0.46 points higher among overweight participants, and nutrient-restricted overweight dieters scored 0.61 points higher [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]]. The proposed cause is the nutrient pathway, since cutting calories or whole food groups produces deficits in B vitamins, iron and omega-3s. Read with [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]], the implication is that diet changes made for physical health can harm mood when nutrient quality is sacrificed, so nutrient adequacy and not only caloric deficit belongs in the design of any diet.

## Limits of the Evidence

Most of this literature is associational. [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] says so directly and advises that treatment decisions be guided by a healthcare provider, the adolescent cohort and the NHANES analysis cannot establish cause, and the metabolic-psychiatry field began with a single clinical observation. The randomized evidence cited across these notes is the early SMILES trial, so the field pairs an intervention signal with observational data rather than settling the question.
