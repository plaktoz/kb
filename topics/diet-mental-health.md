# Diet and Mental Health

The relationship between diet and mental health has moved from peripheral observation to a recognized research field — nutritional psychiatry and metabolic psychiatry — with mounting evidence that what people eat shapes mood, stress tolerance, and even the trajectory of serious psychiatric conditions.

## Mechanisms: How Food Reaches the Brain

Three biological pathways connect diet to mental health. The first is inflammation: [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] documents that ultra-processed foods, which now account for roughly 50% of the average American diet, are linked to chronic inflammation, and Eric Verdin's Buck Institute research identifies inflammation as a direct mechanism by which poor diet impairs brain function. The second is the gut-brain axis: [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] notes that gut bacteria produce approximately 90% of the body's serotonin, making microbiome composition a direct input into mood regulation — not merely a downstream effect of it. The third pathway is nutrient availability: [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]] documents that calorie restriction can create shortfalls in B vitamins, iron, omega-3 fatty acids, and complex carbohydrates — all implicated in the neurochemical processes that regulate mood — explaining why the 28,525-person NHANES study found calorie-restricted dieters scoring significantly higher on the PHQ-9 depression scale than those following other patterns.

## Dietary Patterns and Mood Outcomes

The Mediterranean dietary pattern has accumulated the strongest evidence base for mental health benefits. [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] cites a 32% reduced depression risk associated with adherence to Mediterranean eating across multiple studies, and the SMILES trial — one of the first randomized controlled trials to test diet as a depression treatment — found that dietary counseling toward Mediterranean-style eating produced 32% depression remission versus just 8% in a social-support control group. [[mediterranean-diet-reduces-perceived-stress]] adds the stress dimension from a Binghamton University study of 1,500+ participants: Mediterranean dietary components were associated with lower perceived stress tolerance thresholds and lower mental distress, while Western dietary components (red meat, refined grains, processed foods) correlated with higher perceived stress and mental distress. Critically, this study reversed the usual research direction — investigating how diet shapes stress perception rather than how stress shapes diet choices — suggesting a bidirectional relationship where poor diet and heightened stress reinforce each other.

## Metabolic Psychiatry: Diet as Clinical Intervention

[[metabolism-mental-health-keto-schizophrenia]] introduces metabolic psychiatry as a distinct clinical field — not keto diets for weight loss, but measured ketogenic metabolic therapy targeted at serious psychiatric conditions. The field traces to a 2009 case observed by Stanford physician Shebani Sethi, in which a patient's schizophrenia symptoms improved through a dietary intervention after years of failed medication, providing enough anomaly to justify systematic investigation. [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] expands the scope: at Food Tank's Capitol Hill Luncheon, speakers including Tufts' Dariush Mozaffarian cited ketogenic approaches as an example of a metabolic intervention for bipolar disorder, and framed the broader "food is medicine" case as redirecting the 90% of $4.5 trillion in US healthcare spending that currently goes to treating chronic disease rather than preventing it. The emerging clinical consensus treats the brain not as immune to metabolic processes but as deeply dependent on them — an organ whose function is shaped by systemic inflammation, nutrient availability, and energy substrate choices just as much as by neurotransmitter chemistry.

## Restriction Risks: When Dieting Harms Mood

The link between diet quality and mental health implies a corresponding risk when dieting degrades nutrient adequacy. [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]] found that calorie-restricted dieters showed PHQ-9 depression scores 0.29 points higher than comparison groups, with overweight participants scoring 0.46 points higher — and nutrient-restricted dieters showed even larger increases (0.61 points for overweight participants). The mechanism is the same as the nutrient pathway: cutting total calories often produces deficits in B vitamins, iron, and omega-3s that directly affect neurochemical function. The practical implication from [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]] and [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]] together is that dietary changes intended to improve physical health can inadvertently harm mental health when nutrient quality is sacrificed for caloric reduction — framing nutrient adequacy, not only caloric deficit, as the relevant design goal.
