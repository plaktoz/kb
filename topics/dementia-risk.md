# Dementia Risk and Brain Aging

This topic gathers the vault's evidence on what raises or lowers dementia and Alzheimer's risk across the whole lifespan, from infant diet and menopause timing to midlife blood pressure, sleep, and hearing, along with how to separate ordinary forgetfulness from neurodegeneration and the new tools for catching Alzheimer's pathology early.

## A Largely Modifiable Disease With a Long Lead Time

WHO guidance cited in two notes places up to 45% of dementia risk within reach of lifestyle change [[maria-shriver-womens-alzheimers-movement-brain-health]] [[dementia-risk-diet-timing-midlife-factors]], and neuroscientist Tommy Wood puts the preventable share at half or more, adding that people who expect decline as inevitable tend to skip the very behaviors that would prevent it [[reversible-causes-of-memory-lapses-vs-dementia]]. The prevention case rests on timing: Alzheimer's pathology is described as starting anywhere from 15 to more than 20 years before memory symptoms [[long-sleep-alzheimers-biomarker-study]] [[maria-shriver-womens-alzheimers-movement-brain-health]], which is why Shriver treats the 30s and 40s, rather than old age, as the decisive window. The most concrete midlife evidence is a 26-year study of 12,409 people in which carrying diabetes, hypertension, and smoking together meant 2.69 times the dementia risk, while avoiding all three was tied to as many as 13 extra dementia-free years [[dementia-risk-diet-timing-midlife-factors]]. The U.S. POINTER trial supplies the interventional counterpart, with adults over 60 who combined more movement, learning, socializing, and Mediterranean-style eating showing sharper cognition after two years [[brain-healthy-habits-barriers-aarp]].

## Vascular and Metabolic Roads to Dementia

Dementia risk repeatedly travels through the same vascular and metabolic pathways that drive heart disease (see [[cardiovascular-health]]). A Neurology study of more than 110,000 people found a transient ischemic attack carried 34% higher long-term dementia risk, rising to 75% in the first year, with dementia following roughly three times as often as a second stroke, which casts the "mini-stroke" as a visible sign of wider cerebrovascular disease rather than a closed episode [[mini-strokes-tia-dementia-risk-34-percent]]. Metabolism shows up both as mechanism and as mediator: Houston Methodist researchers traced obesity-raised phosphatidylethanolamine lipids into the brain, where they appear to weaken immune defenses and promote amyloid, and rebalancing them improved memory in Alzheimer's models [[obesity-alzheimers-fat-molecules-link]], while in the UK sugar-rationing cohort later type 2 diabetes and hypertension together explained about a quarter of the early-life sugar effect on dementia [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]].

## Early-Life Programming: The First 1,000 Days

The strongest sign that dementia risk is shaped long before midlife comes from the natural experiment created when UK sugar rationing ended in 1953: UK Biobank participants exposed to less sugar from conception to about age two had 46% lower Alzheimer's and 27% lower all-cause dementia risk in the npj Aging analysis, plus brain-age estimates about 0.39 years younger and larger hippocampal volume [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]] [[dementia-risk-diet-timing-midlife-factors]]. A Neurology analysis of the same cohort reported more modest hazard ratios (around 0.79 for dementia), a 2.55-year delay in onset, and no significant vascular-dementia link, and outside experts warned that the Alzheimer's figure rests on few cases in people not yet at peak-risk age [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]]. One proposed carry-forward mechanism is durable food preference, since rationed infants were still eating less added sugar and more fiber five decades later, and the researchers stress the results argue for cutting added sugars in infancy, not restricting calories or nutrients [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]]. Read alongside the midlife data, this fits a critical-window pattern in which the same protective investment pays off differently depending on life stage [[dementia-risk-diet-timing-midlife-factors]].

## Hormones, Menopause, and Women's Higher Risk

Women face nearly double men's Alzheimer's risk [[maria-shriver-womens-alzheimers-movement-brain-health]] and account for about two-thirds of cases, with falling estrogen after menopause stripping away a neuroprotective buffer [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]]. The timing of that loss matters: in two aging cohorts followed for up to 18 years, each decade-earlier menopause meant roughly 1.8 fewer Alzheimer's-free years, each five years earlier meant about 15% more white matter damage, and abrupt surgical menopause produced faster memory decline than the natural transition [[early-menopause-alzheimers-cognitive-decline]]. Hormone therapy data approach the same pattern from the other side, with a 183,000-woman UK Biobank analysis linking HRT to about 10% lower dementia and 16% lower Alzheimer's risk, strongest when begun between ages 46 and 56 and among women with surgical menopause or APOE4 [[menopause-hrt-timing-dementia-risk]], and a September 2026 Neurology study finding estrogen-only users, who by definition lack a uterus, 35% less likely to show Alzheimer's brain changes at autopsy and 39% less likely to receive a dementia diagnosis [[estrogen-only-hormone-therapy-lower-dementia-risk]]. Both studies are observational and the estrogen-only data had no timing or duration information, so neither supports starting hormone therapy purely to prevent dementia; the wider menopause picture lives in [[womens-hormonal-health]].

## Sleep as Early Marker and Possible Lever

Sleep appears in the vault both as an early warning sign and as a channel of modifiable risk. In people aged 50 to 69, though not in younger adults, more frequent micro-awakenings tracked with higher polygenic Alzheimer's risk, implicating the locus coeruleus, a brainstem sleep-wake region where Alzheimer's-linked deposits can appear as early as adolescence [[sleep-micro-awakenings-alzheimers-risk]], while in the Framingham cohort both short sleep and sleep beyond 8.5 hours went with higher p-tau181 on a J-shaped curve, leaving open whether long sleep causes harm or is an early symptom [[long-sleep-alzheimers-biomarker-study]]. Genes shape how much poor sleep costs: across 13 variants of AQP4, which supports the brain's overnight glymphatic waste clearance, short sleep predicted faster grey-matter loss in some carriers and slow sleep onset predicted lower brain volume in others [[poor-sleep-aqp4-gene-alzheimers-risk]]. Sleep also frames the mild cognitive impairment window, where an Emory study of 54 older adults with sleep disturbances found those taking at least 5,000 IU of vitamin D daily scored more than 13% higher on the MoCA, a small observational signal still awaiting a randomized trial [[vitamin-d-cognitive-scores-mild-cognitive-impairment]].

## Diet and Mental Engagement

No single eating pattern wins outright: a Frontiers in Nutrition review found Mediterranean, DASH, and MIND diets all tied to lower Alzheimer's risk but argued their effect hinges on mediators such as insulin resistance and neuroinflammation and on moderators such as APOE4 status, age, and sex [[diet-alzheimers-risk-personalized-approach]], while simpler swaps still count, with replacing just 10% of ultra-processed food linked to 19% lower dementia risk over a decade [[brain-healthy-habits-barriers-aarp]]. Meal timing may be a separate lever, as postmenopausal women eating within about an 8-hour window gained modest cognitive improvements independent of weight loss, though experts rank exercise, sleep, and blood pressure control as better supported [[time-restricted-eating-brain-health-older-adults]]. Mental engagement completes the set, with regular reading for pleasure associated with up to 33% lower Alzheimer's risk via cognitive reserve, though reviewers position it as one component of a brain-healthy lifestyle rather than a standalone fix [[reading-habit-cognitive-health-alzheimers-risk]].

## Movement: Starting Small, and How Hard Counts

The largest movement study in the vault used wrist devices to follow nearly 100,000 adults for about seven years and found that people with the highest share of vigorous, slightly breathless activity had 63% lower dementia risk than those with none, with benefits appearing at as little as 15–20 minutes a week and with unplanned bursts such as hurrying up stairs counting toward the total [[exercise-intensity-beats-duration-disease-risk-study]]. Its proposed mechanism, lower systemic inflammation plus more neuron-supporting compounds, overlaps with the anti-inflammatory, vascular, and plasticity pathways cited for a trial in which 45 minutes of moderate aerobic exercise four times weekly improved executive function in sedentary postmenopausal women within three months [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]]. Walking speed points the same way, with "super movers" over 80 showing about half the risk of cognitive impairment and better-preserved memory regions, though Leana Wen warns they may simply have been healthier or wealthier all along [[super-movers-walking-brain-health]]. Both lines of evidence favor small starts over large time commitments, since Wen locates the biggest gain in going from no activity to a little and AARP's experts suggest beginning with 10 minutes and adding 5–10 minutes each week [[brain-healthy-habits-barriers-aarp]].

## Hearing, Infection, and Inflammation

Hearing loss is described as the largest single modifiable dementia risk factor, and Tommy Wood lists hearing aids and cataract surgery among direct ways to lower cognitive-decline risk [[hearing-aids-dementia-risk-epilepsy]] [[reversible-causes-of-memory-lapses-vs-dementia]], with the ACHIEVE trial showing hearing aids delayed decline in at-risk people [[brain-healthy-habits-barriers-aarp]]. Yet a 250-million-record analysis found hearing-aid use tied to 23% lower dementia risk only among people who also had epilepsy, with no significant association in the general hearing-loss population, a gap researchers attribute to differences in cognitive reserve [[hearing-aids-dementia-risk-epilepsy]]. Infection forms another thread, as nursing-home entrants who received Shingrix developed dementia less often over four years (18.8% versus 24.6%, a 24% lower risk), though vaccinated residents were somewhat healthier and GSK funded the work [[shingles-vaccine-linked-to-lower-dementia-risk]]. Inflammation may itself run on separate tracks, with a UC Irvine study finding YKL-40 tracking small-vessel damage and GFAP tracking amyloid, two routes that converge on the same tau rise, hippocampal shrinkage, and memory loss and that may explain why single-target therapies help only some patients [[alzheimers-inflammatory-blood-markers-brain-shrinkage]].

## Genetic Risk Is Not Destiny

APOE4 is the most studied genetic risk factor among more than 100 variants tied to late-onset Alzheimer's [[maria-shriver-womens-alzheimers-movement-brain-health]], but across the vault genetics behaves more like an effect modifier than a verdict. SuperAgers in their 80s and 90s showed no advantage on APOE or polygenic risk scores over average peers, implying exceptional memory depends on protective factors beyond low inherited risk [[superagers-genetics-alzheimers-risk]], and the AQP4 work shows a single genotype looking protective or harmful depending on how someone sleeps [[poor-sleep-aqp4-gene-alzheimers-risk]]. APOE4 status also changes how other factors play out, strengthening the HRT association [[menopause-hrt-timing-dementia-risk]], altering dietary response [[diet-alzheimers-risk-personalized-approach]], and amplifying what a p-tau217 level predicts about time to impairment [[ptau217-apoe-cognitive-decline-prediction]].

## Telling Reversible Lapses From Dementia

Tommy Wood separates stress- and fatigue-driven forgetting, a retrieval failure for memories that were stored, from dementia, which breaks encoding itself, and notes that noticing your own lapses is mildly reassuring because people with true decline often miss it [[reversible-causes-of-memory-lapses-vs-dementia]]. Many apparent declines have treatable causes, including B12 or B1 deficiency, hypothyroidism, sedatives, anticholinergics and polypharmacy, depression-driven pseudodementia, and kidney, liver, or electrolyte problems, which makes early evaluation worthwhile [[reversible-causes-of-memory-lapses-vs-dementia]], and the same logic applies to a rapid drop in walking speed, whose causes range from medication effects and muscle loss to neurological disease [[super-movers-walking-brain-health]]. The boundary between worry and early disease remains blurry, however, since people reporting subjective cognitive decline were exactly the group in which smartphone memory tests separated amyloid-positive from amyloid-negative participants [[smartphone-memory-tests-alzheimers-brain-changes]], and mild cognitive impairment is itself framed as a critical intervention window [[vitamin-d-cognitive-scores-mild-cognitive-impairment]].

## Detection: Blood Tests and Digital Tools

Alzheimer's detection has moved quickly, from no FDA-cleared blood tests to four in little more than a year, against a backdrop in which about 75% of dementia cases go undiagnosed [[fda-clears-first-single-biomarker-blood-test-alzheimers]]. Roche and Eli Lilly's Elecsys pTau217 became the first single-biomarker test able to both rule in and rule out amyloid pathology for symptomatic adults 55 and over, and C2N's PrecivityAD2 became the first cleared for symptomatic adults as young as 40, with 97.6% positive and 93.1% negative predictive value against PET and spinal-fluid benchmarks [[fda-clears-first-single-biomarker-blood-test-alzheimers]] [[fda-clears-precivityad2-alzheimers-blood-test-adults-40]]. None of these tests stands alone as a diagnosis, guidance for intermediate results is still missing, and context matters for reading them, since p-tau217 predicted time to impairment more strongly in APOE-ε4 carriers (24% versus 13% shorter per standard deviation) and its predictive strength varied by racial and ethnic group [[ptau217-apoe-cognitive-decline-prediction]]. Digital tools lag further behind, with a Roche-funded 28-day smartphone study detecting group-level memory differences tied to amyloid status but unable to classify individuals [[smartphone-memory-tests-alzheimers-brain-changes]], while blood panels that sort patients by vascular versus amyloid inflammatory driver remain a research aim [[alzheimers-inflammatory-blood-markers-brain-shrinkage]].
