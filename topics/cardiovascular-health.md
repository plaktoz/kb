# Cardiovascular Health

The vault treats cardiovascular health as a lifelong process rather than an event of old age: it covers silent plaque, undetected hypertension and rising heart failure in young adults, the modifiable and inflammatory drivers of arterial damage, the exercise, diet and drug interventions that change the course of disease, and newly recognized anatomical routes to stroke, so this topic maps the points where intervention can still help.

## Plaque Vulnerability and Early-Onset Risk

Coronary imaging separates two processes that are often lumped together: the five modifiable factors (dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, obesity and smoking) make plaques rupture-prone, while age, sex and family history mostly track with stable plaque buildup, and once four or more modifiable factors are present half of all plaques are thin-cap fibroatheromas [[modifiable-risk-factors-coronary-plaque-heart-attack]]. Population imaging shows the process starts far earlier than the clinic usually looks: the REACT study found plaque in 57.1% of more than 16,800 adults aged 18–70 with no known heart disease, including about 1 in 13 under 30, with men developing it 5–10 years before women [[silent-atherosclerosis-young-adults-react-study]], and about 79% of adults averaging 23 years old already show cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, with higher stages carrying carotid wall thickening normally seen in much older people [[ckm-syndrome-young-adults-cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic]]. REACT also found that standard risk scores built from age, blood pressure and cholesterol flagged only a small share of the people who already had silent plaque, so estimated risk is a weak stand-in for disease that is already present. Its authors stop short of calling for universal screening, which leaves the CKM authors' prescription, integrated management of total risk-factor burden from early adulthood, as the practical response.

## Hypertension and the Leaky Care Cascade

Hypertension, one of the five plaque-vulnerability drivers, shows how much modifiable risk goes unmanaged: among 1.4 million English adults in Our Future Health, 37% had hypertension, 59% of those did not know it, and half of those on medication still read at or above 140/90 mmHg, so losses pile up at each step from diagnosis to treatment to control [[undiagnosed-hypertension-england-our-future-health]]. The problem is silent and starts young, since more than 15% of 18–39-year-olds have hypertension but are not eligible for NHS Health Checks, while the menopause evidence stresses that blood pressure rises unnoticed [[early-menopause-linked-to-high-blood-pressure]] and roughly 62% of AFib patients are likewise unaware before diagnosis [[eye-scan-retinal-imaging-afib-risk-prediction]]. The Oxford authors want proactive, population-wide detection and treatment based on predicted cardiovascular risk instead of NICE's multi-reading, lifestyle-first route, which matters because high blood pressure is tied to about half of UK heart attacks and strokes.

## Heart Failure's Rising Toll and the Therapy Gap

HF Stats 2026 reports that US age-adjusted heart failure mortality rose about 37% from 2010 to 2023, reversing more than a decade of progress, with heart failure contributing to 452,573 deaths in 2023 and the burden moving younger: hospitalizations rose among adults under 65 while falling among older adults, and crude mortality more than doubled among 25–44-year-olds [[hf-stats-2026-heart-failure-mortality-rising-younger-adults]]. The report ties the projected rise from 7.7 to 11.4 million patients by 2050 to growing obesity, hypertension and other cardiometabolic risk factors, and notes that about a third of US adults without symptoms already meet "at-risk" stage A criteria, which fits the early-onset picture from the CKM and REACT data. As with hypertension control, the bottleneck is delivery rather than knowledge: only 18% of patients with reduced ejection fraction receive quadruple guideline-directed therapy, the same pattern of proven but underused treatment that the cardiac rehab review describes [[at-home-cardiac-rehab-as-effective-as-in-person-programs]].

## Exercise Dose, Volume and Intensity

Meeting the 150-minute weekly guideline lowers cardiovascular risk by only 8–9%, while cutting it by more than 30% takes roughly 560–610 minutes a week, a level only 12% of the UK Biobank cohort reached, and less-fit people need more minutes for the same gain [[exercise-volume-heart-protection-study]]. Two wearable studies show that intensity can stand in for volume: brisk walkers (80+ steps per minute at peak 30-minute cadence) taking fewer than 5,000 daily steps had mortality risk similar to slower walkers taking 5,000–7,500 [[walk-pace-step-count-mortality-risk]], and among nearly 100,000 adults tracked for about seven years, those with the highest share of vigorous, slightly breathless activity, sometimes only 15–20 minutes a week, had 46% lower all-cause mortality, 60% lower type 2 diabetes risk and 63% lower dementia risk than those with none [[exercise-intensity-beats-duration-disease-risk-study]]. That study counted unplanned bursts such as hurrying up stairs, found intensity mattered most for inflammation-linked conditions while duration and intensity both mattered for metabolic health, and proposed better VO2 max and lower systemic inflammation as the routes. Taken together, the notes treat the guideline as a minimum, note that the biggest gains still come from moving from sedentary to active, and point to effort and pace as the practical levers for people who cannot add hours.

## Structured Exercise as Treatment: AFib and Cardiac Rehabilitation

Exercise also works as therapy for people who already have heart disease: in the NEXAF trial, a year-long individualized program cut time spent in AFib by 45% and AFib-related hospitalizations by 46%, and experts suggested it could count as a primary treatment used alongside medication, not instead of it [[tailored-exercise-intervention-reduces-afib-recurrence]]. An updated Cochrane review of 107 trials extends this to coronary heart disease, where exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation lowered heart attack risk by 28% and all-cause admissions by about 35%, preventing one heart attack for every 71 participants within 6–12 months [[at-home-cardiac-rehab-as-effective-as-in-person-programs]]. Both bodies of evidence point toward remote delivery, since NEXAF moved patients from eight supervised sessions to home exercise tracked by smartwatch and implanted monitor and a 27-study companion analysis found home and digitally supported rehab matched facility programs, which suggests access, not effectiveness, has limited the therapy's reach. Two gaps remain: rehab is cheap and cost-effective yet underused, and only 17% of trial participants were women, a blind spot that resembles how exercise–brain-health trials routinely ignore menopause status [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]].

## Aerobic Exercise Beyond the Heart

The same aerobic exercise also improves brain and cancer outcomes. In sedentary postmenopausal women, 45 minutes of moderate activity four times a week improved executive function within three months through vascular, metabolic, anti-inflammatory and neuroplasticity pathways [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]], and the wearable intensity data links vigorous effort to a 63% lower dementia risk [[exercise-intensity-beats-duration-disease-risk-study]]. After cancer treatment, a meta-analysis of 21 RCTs linked aerobic exercise alone to a 29% lower relative risk of death and structured exercise to a 17% higher chance of recurrence-free survival, while resistance training on its own was not significant [[aerobic-exercise-cancer-recurrence-survival-meta-analysis]]. The cardiac rehab review likewise calls strength training an underused complement for longevity and metabolic health in older adults [[at-home-cardiac-rehab-as-effective-as-in-person-programs]], so the evidence treats resistance work as an addition to aerobic training rather than a substitute for it.

## Beverages and Sweetener Substitutions

Coffee's benefits depend on the outcome being measured: three to five cups a day is considered safe for most adults, one daily cup cut recurrent atrial fibrillation or flutter by 39% in a 2025 JAMA trial, and five or more cups is associated with lower cirrhosis and liver cancer risk [[coffee-research-daily-intake-heart-liver-health]]. Xylitol points the other way: in conference data from more than 17,000 people, not yet peer-reviewed, the highest blood levels carried 57% (Canada) and 18% (UK) higher major-event risk even after adjusting for BMI, hypertension, smoking and lipids [[xylitol-cardiovascular-risk-study-2026]]. The lesson from both is that swapping in a substitute to fix one variable, such as blood sugar, can create its own cardiovascular cost, which is why experts rank overall eating patterns such as DASH above cutting any single ingredient.

## Dietary Patterns: Whole Grains, Plant-Based Groceries and Ultra-Processed Food

The largest RCT meta-analysis of whole grains (87 trials) found that 4–6 daily servings lower body weight, waist size, total cholesterol and IL-6 with high certainty, with moderate-certainty improvements in LDL, triglycerides, systolic pressure and fasting glucose and benefits starting at around three servings [[whole-grains-daily-servings-cardiovascular-risk-reduction]], while a meta-analysis of 51 cohorts and about 8.8 million people found 14% higher cardiovascular-event risk for each extra 100g a day of ultra-processed food and no safe threshold [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. A Mass General Brigham Food Is Medicine program adds the long-duration view that the short whole-grain trials lack: 68 adults who received an average of 97 weekly plant-based grocery packages (produce, beans, nut butter and whole grains) over 2.7 years lost 3.21 mmHg of diastolic pressure, 2.6 kg and 1.04 BMI points per 100 packages, though their A1c did not improve [[food-is-medicine-long-term-plant-based-groceries-cardiometabolic]]. None of these results fits single-nutrient thinking, since whole grains act through fiber, minerals, polyphenols and phytosterols at once, the UPF "cocktail effect" frames harm as coming from combinations of additives and processing byproducts, and the grocery program changed whole meals rather than one ingredient. The practical message across all three is sustained, gradual substitution of refined and processed foods, though 8-week trials, self-reported diets and a small uncontrolled before-and-after design limit how strongly cause can be claimed.

## Inflammation as the Connecting Mechanism

Systemic inflammation is the mechanism that comes up most often across the vault: about 34% of U.S. adults have it, and in 488,079 UK Biobank participants the top quartile of the marker GlycA carried 43% higher major-event risk along with smaller ventricles, thicker walls and weaker diastolic function [[systemic-inflammation-glyca-cardiovascular-risk]]. Several of GlycA's drivers, including abdominal fat and smoking, also appear among the modifiable plaque-vulnerability factors whose shared pathway is vascular inflammation [[modifiable-risk-factors-coronary-plaque-heart-attack]], and GlycA is meant to be read alongside ApoB, LDL, blood pressure and glucose rather than on its own. Inflammation is also the proposed explanation for whole grains' IL-6 reduction, for the heart benefits of tirzepatide and the shingles vaccine, and for vigorous exercise's link to lower dementia risk [[exercise-intensity-beats-duration-disease-risk-study]], which suggests that lowering inflammation, more than any particular food, drug or workout, is what many of the strongest findings have in common.

## Pharmacological and Vaccine-Based Interventions

The FDA's August 2026 approval of Mounjaro to reduce major cardiovascular events in high-risk type 2 diabetes rests on two lines of evidence: in the SURPASS-CVOT trial (13,000+ patients) it had 8% lower relative risk than dulaglutide, and BMJ observational data showed 33% lower heart-attack risk than sitagliptin, with clinicians crediting part of the benefit to reduced inflammation and more stable plaque [[mounjaro-heart-health-type-2-diabetes]]. Shingrix recipients aged 60 and over had 9% lower overall cardiovascular burden than Zostavax recipients across seven years, including 12% less heart failure, 10% less ischemic heart disease and 7% less AFib, possibly through the AS01 adjuvant's effect on IL-6-linked pathways [[shingles-vaccine-linked-to-lower-cardiovascular-burden]]. Both results have limits, since SURPASS-CVOT enrolled very high-risk patients and the Shingrix finding is observational while two RCTs are under way, so they add options for specific groups rather than replacing the lifestyle measures above.

## Hormonal Risk and the HRT Timing Window

Menopause before 45, and especially before 40, raises later hypertension risk: estrogen supports nitric oxide production and arterial flexibility, and losing it early removes that protection, with sleep disruption, weight gain and worse insulin sensitivity adding to the effect [[early-menopause-linked-to-high-blood-pressure]]. Twenty years of SWAN data on more than 2,700 women shows the other side, with HRT started in perimenopause or early postmenopause associated with about 22% lower CVD risk, 27% when started within 10 years of menopause, and roughly 49% among Black women [[menopausal-hormone-therapy-early-timing-heart-disease-risk]]. The proposed estradiol mechanism (lower LDL, including plaque-linked subparticles, plus lower blood sugar and inflammation) matches the plaque and inflammation findings above, and the REACT finding that women's plaque rises most steeply between 40 and 60 [[silent-atherosclerosis-young-adults-react-study]] places that window in the same decades. Hot flashes and night sweats that occur alongside high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol may mark a moment for an individual HRT discussion that age-based screening would miss.

## Circadian Disruption and Cardiac Remodeling

Even 3 lux of nighttime light, about the level of a dim nightlight, was linked in more than 11,000 UK Biobank participants to 2.4% greater left ventricular mass, 1.5% thicker heart walls and 1.9% lower contraction fraction, and a separate 2025 study tied nighttime light to 32–56% higher risk of various cardiovascular conditions [[nighttime-light-exposure-cardiovascular-disease-risk]]. Shorter sleep explains 24–49% of the association, which points to a disrupted body clock affecting blood pressure and metabolism rather than direct damage to the heart. The thicker-wall pattern resembles the remodeling seen with high GlycA [[systemic-inflammation-glyca-cardiovascular-risk]], so two quite different exposures leave similar structural traces, and because the fixes are cheap (blackout curtains, face-down phones, warm dim nightlights, morning sunlight), this is one of the easiest risks in the topic to act on.

## Stroke Pathways: Arrhythmia, Anatomy, and the Brain

The vault describes two separate ways clots can reach the brain: through the left atrial appendage, where clots usually need AFib to form, and through the left atrial septal pouch (LASP), an 8–11mm pocket in the wall between the upper chambers found in about one in three adults, where blood can pool and clot even in normal rhythm and which 16 years of observational work link to strokes with no other identified cause [[left-atrial-septal-pouch-heart-discovery]]. A 2026 Fuwai Hospital trial, the first randomized test of closing the pouch with a catheter-delivered occluder, found the procedure feasible and saw markedly fewer recurrent MRI-detected ischemic brain lesions than with medication alone, while on the AFib route retinal thinning visible on routine eye scans appears about four years before diagnosis, possibly because of mini-strokes caused by AFib clots [[eye-scan-retinal-imaging-afib-risk-prediction]]. Catching either route early matters because TIAs carry a 34% higher dementia risk that lasts 20 years and dementia follows about three times as often as a second stroke [[mini-strokes-tia-dementia-risk-34-percent]], while the LASP case shows that some stroke risk is anatomical and falls outside the modifiable-factor model behind most of this topic.
