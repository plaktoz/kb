# Cardiovascular Health

The vault treats cardiovascular health as a lifelong process, not an event of old age: it covers arterial damage and undetected hypertension in young adults, the modifiable and inflammatory drivers of plaque, treatments and rehabilitation that change the course of established disease, and newly recognized anatomical routes to stroke, so this topic maps the points where intervention can still help.

## Plaque Vulnerability and Early-Onset Risk

Coronary imaging separates two processes that are often lumped together: the five modifiable factors (dyslipidemia, hypertension, diabetes, obesity and smoking) make plaques rupture-prone, while age, sex and family history mostly track with stable plaque buildup [[modifiable-risk-factors-coronary-plaque-heart-attack]]. The effect scales with the number of factors, with half of all plaques being thin-cap fibroatheromas once four or more are present and vascular inflammation proposed as the shared mechanism, so the target is the total risk-factor burden rather than any single reading. The CKM data shows this burden builds up decades earlier than assumed: about 79% of adults averaging 23 years old already show cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic syndrome, and higher stages come with the carotid wall thickening normally seen in much older people [[ckm-syndrome-young-adults-cardiovascular-kidney-metabolic]]. That makes the case for integrated care starting in early adulthood rather than separate treatment of each condition later.

## Hypertension and the Leaky Care Cascade

Hypertension, one of the five plaque-vulnerability drivers, shows how much modifiable risk goes unmanaged: among 1.4 million English adults in Our Future Health, 37% had hypertension, 59% of those did not know it, and half of those on medication still read at or above 140/90 mmHg, so losses pile up at each step from diagnosis to treatment to control [[undiagnosed-hypertension-england-our-future-health]]. The problem is silent and starts young, since more than 15% of 18–39-year-olds have hypertension but are not eligible for NHS Health Checks, matching the early-onset CKM picture, while the menopause evidence stresses that blood pressure rises unnoticed [[early-menopause-linked-to-high-blood-pressure]] and roughly 62% of AFib patients are likewise unaware before diagnosis [[eye-scan-retinal-imaging-afib-risk-prediction]]. The Oxford authors want proactive, population-wide detection and treatment based on predicted cardiovascular risk instead of NICE's multi-reading, lifestyle-first route, which echoes the plaque research's focus on total risk and matters because high blood pressure is tied to about half of UK heart attacks and strokes.

## Exercise Dose-Response and Behavioral Flexibility

Meeting the 150-minute weekly guideline lowers cardiovascular risk by only 8–9%, while cutting it by more than 30% takes roughly 560–610 minutes a week, a level only 12% of the UK Biobank cohort reached, and less-fit people need more minutes for the same gain [[exercise-volume-heart-protection-study]]. The walking data makes that target less daunting by showing that intensity can stand in for volume: brisk walkers (80+ steps per minute, measured objectively as peak 30-minute cadence) taking fewer than 5,000 daily steps had mortality risk similar to slower walkers taking 5,000–7,500 [[walk-pace-step-count-mortality-risk]]. Together these notes treat the guideline as a minimum rather than a goal while noting that the biggest gains still come from moving from sedentary to active, so for people who cannot add hours, raising fitness and walking pace are the practical levers.

## Structured Exercise as Treatment: AFib and Cardiac Rehabilitation

Exercise also works as therapy for people who already have heart disease: in the NEXAF trial, a year-long individualized program cut time spent in AFib by 45% and AFib-related hospitalizations by 46%, and experts suggested it could count as a primary treatment used alongside medication, not instead of it [[tailored-exercise-intervention-reduces-afib-recurrence]]. An updated Cochrane review of 107 trials extends this to coronary heart disease, where exercise-based cardiac rehabilitation lowered heart attack risk by 28% and all-cause admissions by about 35%, preventing one heart attack for every 71 participants within 6–12 months [[at-home-cardiac-rehab-as-effective-as-in-person-programs]]. Both bodies of evidence point toward remote delivery: NEXAF moved patients from eight supervised sessions to home exercise tracked by smartwatch and implanted monitor, and a 27-study companion analysis found home and digitally supported rehab matched facility programs, which suggests access, not effectiveness, has limited the therapy's reach. Two gaps remain, since rehab is cheap and cost-effective yet underused and only 17% of trial participants were women, a blind spot that resembles how exercise–brain-health trials routinely ignore menopause status [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]].

## Aerobic Exercise Beyond the Heart

The same aerobic exercise also improves brain and cancer outcomes. In sedentary postmenopausal women, 45 minutes of moderate activity four times a week improved executive function within three months, through vascular, metabolic, anti-inflammatory and neuroplasticity pathways [[aerobic-exercise-brain-health-postmenopausal-women]]. After cancer treatment, a meta-analysis of 21 RCTs linked aerobic exercise alone to a 29% lower relative risk of death and structured exercise to a 17% higher chance of recurrence-free survival, while resistance training on its own was not significant [[aerobic-exercise-cancer-recurrence-survival-meta-analysis]]. The cardiac rehab review likewise calls strength training an underused complement for longevity and metabolic health in older adults [[at-home-cardiac-rehab-as-effective-as-in-person-programs]], so the evidence treats resistance work as an addition to aerobic training rather than a substitute for it.

## Beverages and Sweetener Substitutions

Coffee's benefits depend on the outcome being measured: three to five cups a day is considered safe for most adults, one daily cup cut recurrent atrial fibrillation or flutter by 39% in a 2025 JAMA trial, and five or more cups is associated with lower cirrhosis and liver cancer risk [[coffee-research-daily-intake-heart-liver-health]]. Xylitol points the other way: in conference data from more than 17,000 people, not yet peer-reviewed, the highest blood levels carried 57% (Canada) and 18% (UK) higher major-event risk even after adjusting for BMI, hypertension, smoking and lipids [[xylitol-cardiovascular-risk-study-2026]]. The lesson from both is that swapping in a substitute to fix one variable, such as blood sugar, can create its own cardiovascular cost, which is why experts rank overall eating patterns such as DASH above cutting any single ingredient.

## Whole Grains Versus Ultra-Processed Food

The largest RCT meta-analysis of whole grains (87 trials) found that 4–6 daily servings lower body weight, waist size, total cholesterol and IL-6 with high certainty, with moderate-certainty improvements in LDL, triglycerides, systolic pressure and fasting glucose, and benefits starting at around three servings [[whole-grains-daily-servings-cardiovascular-risk-reduction]]. Ultra-processed food shows the opposite pattern, with a meta-analysis of 51 cohorts and about 8.8 million people finding 14% higher cardiovascular-event risk for each extra 100g a day and no safe threshold identified [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. Neither result fits single-nutrient thinking: whole grains work through fiber, minerals, polyphenols and phytosterols at once, while the UPF "cocktail effect" frames harm as coming from combinations of additives and processing byproducts. In both cases the practical step is gradual substitution, replacing refined and processed foods rather than eating more or cutting everything out, although short whole-grain trials and self-reported UPF diets limit how strongly cause can be claimed.

## Inflammation as the Connecting Mechanism

Systemic inflammation is the mechanism that comes up most often across the vault: about 34% of U.S. adults have it, and in 488,079 UK Biobank participants the top quartile of the marker GlycA carried 43% higher major-event risk along with smaller ventricles, thicker walls and weaker diastolic function [[systemic-inflammation-glyca-cardiovascular-risk]]. Several of GlycA's drivers, including abdominal fat and smoking, also appear among the modifiable plaque-vulnerability factors whose shared pathway is vascular inflammation [[modifiable-risk-factors-coronary-plaque-heart-attack]], and GlycA is meant to be read alongside ApoB, LDL, blood pressure and glucose rather than on its own. Inflammation is also the proposed explanation for whole grains' IL-6 reduction and for the heart benefits of tirzepatide and the shingles vaccine, which suggests that lowering inflammation, more than any particular food or drug, is what many of the strongest findings have in common.

## Pharmacological and Vaccine-Based Interventions

The FDA's August 2026 approval of Mounjaro to reduce major cardiovascular events in high-risk type 2 diabetes rests on two lines of evidence: in the SURPASS-CVOT trial (13,000+ patients) it had 8% lower relative risk than dulaglutide, and BMJ observational data showed 33% lower heart-attack risk than sitagliptin, with clinicians crediting part of the benefit to reduced inflammation and more stable plaque [[mounjaro-heart-health-type-2-diabetes]]. Shingrix recipients aged 60 and over had 9% lower overall cardiovascular burden than Zostavax recipients across seven years, including 12% less heart failure, 10% less ischemic heart disease and 7% less AFib, possibly through the AS01 adjuvant's effect on IL-6-linked pathways [[shingles-vaccine-linked-to-lower-cardiovascular-burden]]. Both results have limits, since SURPASS-CVOT enrolled very high-risk patients and the Shingrix finding is observational while two RCTs are under way, so they add options for specific groups rather than replacing the lifestyle measures above.

## Hormonal Risk and the HRT Timing Window

Menopause before 45, and especially before 40, raises later hypertension risk: estrogen supports nitric oxide production and arterial flexibility, and losing it early removes that protection, with sleep disruption, weight gain and worse insulin sensitivity adding to the effect [[early-menopause-linked-to-high-blood-pressure]]. Twenty years of SWAN data on more than 2,700 women shows the other side, with HRT started in perimenopause or early postmenopause associated with about 22% lower CVD risk, 27% when started within 10 years of menopause, and roughly 49% among Black women [[menopausal-hormone-therapy-early-timing-heart-disease-risk]]. The proposed estradiol mechanism (lower LDL, including plaque-linked subparticles, plus lower blood sugar and inflammation) matches the plaque and inflammation findings above. Hot flashes and night sweats that occur alongside high blood pressure or abnormal cholesterol may mark a window for an individual HRT discussion that age-based screening would miss.

## Circadian Disruption and Cardiac Remodeling

Even 3 lux of nighttime light, about the level of a dim nightlight, was linked in more than 11,000 UK Biobank participants to 2.4% greater left ventricular mass, 1.5% thicker heart walls and 1.9% lower contraction fraction, and a separate 2025 study tied nighttime light to 32–56% higher risk of various cardiovascular conditions [[nighttime-light-exposure-cardiovascular-disease-risk]]. Shorter sleep explains 24–49% of the association, which points to a disrupted body clock affecting blood pressure and metabolism rather than direct damage to the heart. The thicker-wall pattern resembles the remodeling seen with high GlycA [[systemic-inflammation-glyca-cardiovascular-risk]], so two quite different exposures leave similar structural traces. Because the fixes are cheap (blackout curtains, face-down phones, warm dim nightlights, morning sunlight), this is one of the easiest risks in the topic to act on.

## Stroke Pathways: Arrhythmia, Anatomy, and the Brain

The vault now describes two separate ways clots can reach the brain: through the left atrial appendage, where clots usually need AFib to form, and through the left atrial septal pouch (LASP), an 8–11mm pocket in the wall between the upper chambers found in about one in three adults, where blood can pool and clot even in normal rhythm and which 16 years of observational work link to strokes with no other identified cause [[left-atrial-septal-pouch-heart-discovery]]. A 2026 Fuwai Hospital trial, the first randomized test of closing the pouch with a catheter-delivered occluder, found the procedure feasible and saw markedly fewer recurrent MRI-detected ischemic brain lesions than with medication alone. On the AFib route, retinal thinning visible on routine eye scans appears about four years before diagnosis, possibly because of mini-strokes caused by AFib clots [[eye-scan-retinal-imaging-afib-risk-prediction]]. Catching either route early matters because TIAs carry a 34% higher dementia risk that lasts 20 years and dementia follows about three times as often as a second stroke [[mini-strokes-tia-dementia-risk-34-percent]], while the LASP case shows that some stroke risk is anatomical and falls outside the modifiable-factor model behind most of this topic.
