# Meetings

This topic covers what the vault knows about workplace meetings: how much they cost, when they create value as coordination and organizational capital, how they fragment attention, and how to design them so the time is an investment rather than overhead.

## The Measured Cost of Meetings

The most careful estimate in the vault comes from the Harvard–Norwegian School of Economics survey of roughly 9,000 workers: 4.7 hours a week in meetings, about 12% of working time but around 14% of wage spending because meetings use several people's time at once, and progress meetings take slightly more weekly prep (4.6 hours) than meeting time (4.5 hours) ([[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]], [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]]). Vendor and survey figures run much higher: WorkTime's monitoring data puts meetings at 21.5% or more of the U.S. workweek and "work about work" at up to 60% of all time ([[worktime-meeting-overload-productivity-study]]), and a statistics roundup has meeting share climbing from 6% of the week for an average employee to 72% for a CEO, with 72% of meetings judged unproductive ([[time-management-statistics-2026]]). That spread reflects differences in method as much as in reality. Still, the sources agree the burden is large and growing: one compilation says meeting counts doubled in two years ([[deep-work-statistics-2026-focus-time-crisis]]), and 64% of surveyed workers call half or fewer of their meetings productive ([[only-3-in-10-workers-deep-focus-survey]]).

## Meetings as Organizational Capital

Set against the cost data, [[David Deming]]'s NBER work finds that meeting-heavy firms pay higher wages and earn more revenue per worker, and that workers with more meeting time see faster wage growth and more on-the-job learning. The researchers read this as meetings being investments in [[Organizational Capital]], a coordination technology for specialized knowledge work and a channel for building [[Human Capital]] ([[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]]). [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]] describes that capital as the decisions, relationships, and shared understanding that good recurring meetings build up over time. The firms whose worker time costs the most choose to meet more, a [[Revealed Preference]] at odds with the familiar complaints, and Deming expects human interaction to grow more valuable as AI absorbs routine tasks ([[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]]).

## Correlation, Causation, and the Limits of the Evidence

The "meetings pay off" finding is correlational, and [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]] flags the obvious confound: more capable people, or people in more complex jobs, may simply end up in meeting-heavy roles, and the data can't tell good meetings from bad. The Itoki–Matsuo study takes a different route, applying VAR-LiNGAM causal discovery to 70 objective behavioral indicators from 854 workers instead of surveys, though its authors say the findings reflect only one company ([[itoki-matsuo-ai-productivity-model-meeting-frequency]]). Many of the loudest overload claims come from employee-monitoring vendors or self-reported surveys ([[worktime-meeting-overload-productivity-study]], [[time-management-statistics-2026]]), so the vault's best evidence on value and its loudest evidence on cost were not gathered the same way.

## Fragmentation: Count Matters More Than Duration

The clearest mechanistic finding is that how often meetings happen hurts more than how long they last. At Itoki, meeting count predicted next-day mental decline more strongly than total meeting time across every job type, and days with 60 or more uninterrupted minutes were followed by better condition for 88% of workers ([[itoki-matsuo-ai-productivity-model-meeting-frequency]]). That fits WorkTime's claim that interruptions, not meetings in themselves, drive cognitive overload ([[worktime-meeting-overload-productivity-study]]), and the roughly 23–25-minute recovery cost per interruption, which [[deep-work-as-organizational-architecture]] says spreads through a team's dependencies ([[time-management-statistics-2026]]). Placement compounds the damage, since half of all meetings land in peak-focus hours ([[deep-work-statistics-2026-focus-time-crisis]]).

## Which Meetings Earn Their Cost

The sources converge on interdependence as the test. Roger Schwarz asks whether the group really depends on each other to get the work done, and Matthew Bidwell argues that complex, interdependent work needs real-time coordination because tone and body language carry information faster than email ([[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]]). Deming separates cross-expertise meetings, which matter more as teams spread out, from recurring status meetings that only relay information ([[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]]). [[deep-work-as-organizational-architecture]] goes further, treating meetings as a last resort for when async has failed or stakes demand real-time interaction, while Lencioni's 15% time cap offers a rough budget that Norway's 12% average sits just under ([[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]]).

## Designing the Meeting Itself

When a meeting is justified, its quality depends on deliberate structure. [[how-best-leaders-shape-conversations]] argues that meetings drift by default, with concerns left unsaid and decisions left open or reopened, unless a leader steers toward clarity, inclusion, and resolution, and Nancy Rothbard notes that the best-prepared attendees are the most let down when that steering is missing ([[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]]). At the scale of a single 1:1, [[6-tips-productive-1-1-meetings-manager]] turns the same logic into routines: an agreed format, one shared agenda-and-notes document, prereads, Rose–Thorn–Bud updates, and ten minutes afterward turning notes into scheduled action items. In both cases the leverage sits before and after the meeting, not only inside it.

## Meeting Culture as an Organizational Design Problem

Several sources place meeting overload in organizational systems rather than personal habits. [[deep-work-as-organizational-architecture]] argues that attendance is read as commitment and responsiveness as engagement, so personal focus tactics fail unless async-first norms, decision-authority documents, and planned cuts to recurring meetings change the defaults, and the Norway data agrees that firm effects explain more of the variation in meeting hours than in how productive meetings feel ([[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]]). Survey sources favor cutting meetings over buying tools ([[only-3-in-10-workers-deep-focus-survey]]), and one cites an HBR finding that a 40% cut raised productivity by 71% ([[deep-work-statistics-2026-focus-time-crisis]]). Deming's warning cuts the other way: reduction should not be a goal in itself, and policy should ask which meetings solve problems and share knowledge and which only make up for unclear processes ([[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]]).

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. A meeting costs the combined output its attendees give up** — Meeting cost grows with headcount times duration plus preparation, so the real question is whether a given meeting returns more than all of that forgone work, not how many meetings there are. · *model:* [[Opportunity Cost]]
→ explains: [[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]], [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]], [[time-management-statistics-2026]]

**T2. Fragmentation, not duration, sets the attention cost** — Every switch into a meeting carries a recovery cost, so the number and placement of interruptions damage focus and well-being more than total meeting hours do. · *model:* [[Attention Residue]]
→ explains: [[itoki-matsuo-ai-productivity-model-meeting-frequency]], [[worktime-meeting-overload-productivity-study]], [[deep-work-as-organizational-architecture]], [[deep-work-statistics-2026-focus-time-crisis]], [[time-management-statistics-2026]], [[only-3-in-10-workers-deep-focus-survey]]

**T3. Synchronous time pays off in proportion to interdependence** — A meeting creates value when participants need each other's real-time input to coordinate specialized work. Where they don't, as with one-way status updates, a written channel does the same job more cheaply.
→ explains: [[meetings-organizational-capital-deming-nber-hr]], [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]], [[deep-work-as-organizational-architecture]]

**T4. Unstructured group conversation drifts toward the least useful outcome** — Without deliberate purpose, preparation, steering, and follow-through, discussions slide into meandering, unspoken concerns, and reopened decisions. A meeting's value depends on the structure built around it. · *model:* [[Systems Thinking]]
→ explains: [[how-best-leaders-shape-conversations]], [[work-meetings-productivity-norway-study]], [[6-tips-productive-1-1-meetings-manager]]

**T5. Meeting load is a property of the organizational system** — How much an organization meets is set by its incentives, norms, and role structure, not by individual preference, so durable change comes from redesigning defaults rather than from personal tactics. · *model:* [[Systems vs. Goals]]
