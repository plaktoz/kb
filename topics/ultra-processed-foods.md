# Ultra-Processed Foods

This topic brings together the vault's notes on ultra-processed foods (UPFs): how the category is defined, what large cohort studies and a first depression trial say about harm, which mechanisms are proposed, and whether processing itself or overall diet quality is what matters.

## Defining UPFs: NOVA and Its Limits

Every study in the vault uses the NOVA classification, which defines UPFs as industrial formulations with five or more ingredients rarely used in home cooking [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], such as soft drinks, packaged snacks, cereals, processed meats and fast food made with refined fats, sugars, starches, preservatives, colorings and emulsifiers [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]] [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. Estimates of how much Americans eat vary, from roughly half of average US nutrition according to Food Tank speakers [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] to nearly 60% of adult calories in the prostate cancer study. The definition measures processing, not nutrition: the Harvard team behind [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] argues it can label sound foods like fortified cereal or plant-based dairy as harmful, and the AMA's call to educate the public on healthy versus unhealthy ultraprocessed foods [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]] accepts the same point.

## Dose-Response Evidence Across Chronic Disease

The main evidence is a September 2026 meta-analysis of 51 prospective cohorts and about 8.8 million people, covered by three vault notes [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. The heaviest consumers had higher risk of digestive disease (31%), depression and anxiety (27%), cardiovascular events and metabolic syndrome or diabetes (24% each), obesity (23%), death (18%) and cancer (12%), and each extra 100 g per day added risk (14% cardiovascular, 11% blood pressure, 4% cancer, 3% mortality) with no apparent safe threshold. Because these conditions often occur together, the authors present cutting UPFs as a single lever against multimorbidity [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. A separate NHANES study of 17,024 US men found 24–31% higher prostate cancer risk in higher-intake groups after adjusting for age, race, smoking and poverty [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]].

## Mental Health: From Association to a First Intervention

Mental health is the only outcome in the vault where UPF research has moved from association to intervention, which ties this topic to [[diet-mental-health]]. The meta-analysis's 27% higher depression and anxiety risk is observational [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and Food Tank speakers cited strong evidence linking UPF-heavy diets to mental health conditions [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]]. In what its authors believe is the first before-and-after comparison within the same people, the UCSF pilot in *JAMA Psychiatry* had 20 metabolically unhealthy adults with depression cut UPF intake by more than 85% in a crossover design, and saw moderate to large improvements on three depression measures with smaller anxiety gains [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. The tiny sample shows a larger trial is feasible but proves nothing yet, and phase 2 enrollment is not expected until 2028.

## Proposed Mechanisms

The notes keep pointing to the gut and inflammation: the meta-analysis authors suggest additives disrupt the gut microbiome [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], UCSF's Nyasha Chagwedera describes a "leaky, out-of-balance gut" whose low-grade inflammation may drive depression [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], and Eric Verdin links diet-driven inflammation to impaired brain function [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]]. Lead author David Xiao Liu's "cocktail effect" hypothesis holds that combinations of additives may do more harm than any single one, supported by a French cohort of about 100,000 people whose additive exposures clustered into patterns, while heat-formed compounds (acrylamide, acrolein), nutrient loss and packaging microplastics are also proposed [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. There is also a behavioral mechanism, since manufacturers tune sugar, salt and fat to trigger dopamine-driven craving, which raises consumption and makes cutting back hard [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Processing or Diet Quality? Limits of the Evidence

The main counterweight is [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]], which followed more than 200,000 US adults over four decades and found that healthy diets lowered and unhealthy diets raised the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and death by about the same amount whether foods were ultra-processed or not, although the result has not yet been peer-reviewed. The other studies have gaps too: the meta-analysis relied on self-reported diets and could not fully rule out lifestyle confounders or establish causation [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and the prostate study's highest-intake subgroup was not statistically significant because it had few cases [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]]. The notes therefore leave open whether harm comes from processing itself or from the poorer nutrition that usually comes with it, since the dose-response gradient makes a causal link more plausible [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] but even the UCSF diet replaced UPFs with healthier foods and so cannot separate the two [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Practical Reduction: Gradual Swaps Over Elimination

Dietitians Johannah Katz and Vahista Ussery read the dose-response data as support for harm reduction, not elimination: keep whole foods as the base, choose smaller packaged portions, add a side salad and cook more at home [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], while Liu advises building habits around vegetables, fruit and whole grains rather than strict rules [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. The Harvard results suggest choosing by nutrient density rather than processing label, since a healthy plant-based diet that included some processed foods beat an unhealthy minimally processed one [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]]. Change is still hard because UPFs are cheap, convenient and engineered to be craved, and even the UCSF participants made their 85%-plus cut with research-team guidance on what to buy [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Policy, Labeling and Food Is Medicine

Regulation lags the research: only Colombia requires national labels identifying ultra-processed products [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and the prostate cancer researchers compare UPFs to tobacco, where decades passed between early signs of harm and policy action [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]]. Change is appearing first in medicine, as the AMA's 2026 food-is-medicine policy expanded its UPF position to include public education and multilingual patient materials [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. At Food Tank's Capitol Hill event, speakers called the US system "sickcare," noted that 90% of 2022's $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending went to patients with chronic disease, and pointed to rare bipartisan interest in nutrition policy [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]].
