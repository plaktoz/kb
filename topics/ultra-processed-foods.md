# Ultra-Processed Foods

This topic brings together the vault's notes on ultra-processed foods (UPFs): how the category is defined and where that definition strains, what large cohort studies and a first depression trial say about harm, which mechanisms are proposed, whether processing itself or overall diet quality is what matters, and how the next generation of studies is trying to answer that question.

## Defining UPFs: NOVA and Its Limits

The studies in the vault use the NOVA classification, which defines UPFs as industrial formulations with five or more ingredients rarely used in home cooking [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], such as soft drinks, packaged snacks, cereals, processed meats and fast food made with refined fats, sugars, starches, preservatives, colorings and emulsifiers [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]] [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. These foods make up a large share of Western diets, from roughly half of average US nutrition according to Food Tank speakers [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] to nearly 60% of US adult calories in the prostate cancer study and about 60% of UK calorie intake [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The weakness is that NOVA measures processing, not nutrition: the Harvard team behind [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] argues it can label sound foods like fortified cereal or plant-based dairy as harmful, the AMA now calls for educating the public on healthy versus unhealthy ultraprocessed foods [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]], and PROSPECT-UK's researchers note that processed foods differ widely in ingredients, processing methods and nutritional makeup even within a single category.

## Dose-Response Evidence Across Chronic Disease

The main evidence is a September 2026 meta-analysis of 51 prospective cohorts and about 8.8 million people, covered by three vault notes [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. The heaviest consumers had higher risk of digestive disease (31%), depression and anxiety (27%), cardiovascular events and metabolic syndrome or diabetes (24% each), obesity (23%), death (18%) and cancer (12%), and each extra 100 g per day added risk (14% cardiovascular, 11% blood pressure, 4% cancer, 3% mortality) with no apparent safe threshold. Because these conditions often occur together, the authors present cutting UPFs as a single lever against multimorbidity [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. A separate NHANES study of 17,024 US men found 24–31% higher prostate cancer risk in higher-intake groups after adjusting for age, race, smoking and poverty [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]].

## Early-Onset Bowel Cancer: A Test Case for the Next Generation of Studies

Digestive disease showed the largest relative increase in the meta-analysis [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and bowel cancer in younger adults has become the focus of the most ambitious new study in the vault. Early-onset bowel cancer rose in 27 of 50 countries in a 2024 PROSPECT analysis, England had the fastest rise in Europe from 2007 to 2017, and UK rates in 25- to 49-year-olds are up more than 50% since the early 1990s, yet no single diet or lifestyle factor has been shown to drive the trend [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. PROSPECT-UK, led by King's College London with ZOE, aims to recruit 250,000 adults aged 18 to 50 and is the first study to record diet at the level of specific brands, linked to long-term health records, so researchers can look past the broad "ultra-processed" label that [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] also questions. It is still observational, so like the existing cohorts it can narrow down suspects but cannot prove causes.

## Mental Health: From Association to a First Intervention

Mental health is the only outcome in the vault where UPF research has moved from association to intervention, which ties this topic to [[diet-mental-health]]. The meta-analysis's 27% higher depression and anxiety risk is observational [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and Food Tank speakers cited strong evidence linking UPF-heavy diets to mental health conditions [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]]. In what its authors believe is the first before-and-after comparison within the same people, the UCSF pilot in *JAMA Psychiatry* had 20 metabolically unhealthy adults with depression cut UPF intake by more than 85% in a crossover design, and saw moderate to large improvements on three depression measures with smaller anxiety gains [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. The tiny sample shows a larger trial is feasible but proves nothing yet, and phase 2 enrollment is not expected until 2028.

## Proposed Mechanisms

The notes keep pointing to the gut and inflammation: the meta-analysis authors suggest additives disrupt the gut microbiome [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], UCSF's Nyasha Chagwedera describes a "leaky, out-of-balance gut" whose low-grade inflammation may drive depression [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], Eric Verdin links diet-driven inflammation to impaired brain function [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]], and the PROSPECT team is studying the gut's bacteria, fungi and viruses alongside Harvard findings that cells may keep a "memory" of inflammation that shapes how bowel cancer develops [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. Lead author David Xiao Liu's "cocktail effect" hypothesis holds that combinations of additives may do more harm than any single one, supported by a French cohort of about 100,000 people whose additive exposures clustered into patterns, while heat-formed compounds (acrylamide, acrolein), nutrient loss and packaging microplastics are also proposed [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. There is also a behavioral mechanism, since manufacturers tune sugar, salt and fat to trigger dopamine-driven craving, which raises consumption and makes cutting back hard [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Processing or Diet Quality? Limits of the Evidence

The main counterweight is [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]], which followed more than 200,000 US adults over four decades and found that healthy diets lowered and unhealthy diets raised the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and death by about the same amount whether foods were ultra-processed or not, although the result has not yet been peer-reviewed. The other studies have gaps too: the meta-analysis relied on self-reported diets and could not fully rule out lifestyle confounders or establish causation [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], the prostate study's highest-intake subgroup was not statistically significant because it had few cases [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], and PROSPECT-UK needs a quarter of a million participants precisely because rare outcomes produce too few cases to separate signal from noise [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The notes therefore leave open whether harm comes from processing itself or from the poorer nutrition that usually comes with it, since the dose-response gradient makes a causal link more plausible [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] but even the UCSF diet replaced UPFs with healthier foods and so cannot separate the two [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Practical Reduction: Gradual Swaps Over Elimination

Dietitians Johannah Katz and Vahista Ussery read the dose-response data as support for harm reduction, not elimination: keep whole foods as the base, choose smaller packaged portions, add a side salad and cook more at home [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], while Liu advises building habits around vegetables, fruit and whole grains rather than strict rules [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. The Harvard results suggest choosing by nutrient density rather than processing label, since a healthy plant-based diet that included some processed foods beat an unhealthy minimally processed one [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]], and Cancer Research UK's interim advice for bowel cancer is similarly broad: more fibre and whole foods, less red and processed meat, staying active, keeping a healthy weight, limiting alcohol and not smoking [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. Change is still hard because UPFs are cheap, convenient and engineered to be craved, and even the UCSF participants made their 85%-plus cut with research-team guidance on what to buy [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Policy, Labeling and Food Is Medicine

Regulation lags the research: only Colombia requires national labels identifying ultra-processed products [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and the prostate cancer researchers compare UPFs to tobacco, where decades passed between early signs of harm and policy action [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]]. Change is appearing first in medicine, as the AMA's 2026 food-is-medicine policy expanded its UPF position to include public education and multilingual patient materials [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. At Food Tank's Capitol Hill event, speakers called the US system "sickcare," noted that 90% of 2022's $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending went to patients with chronic disease, and pointed to rare bipartisan interest in nutrition policy [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]].

**T2. Association cannot separate an exposure from what travels with it** — Observational data link an exposure to outcomes but cannot by themselves rule out the habits and nutrients that accompany it, so they narrow down suspects without proving causes. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]

**T3. A category sorts by one attribute while outcomes may depend on another** — When the property that drives harm varies within a category, the category label will misclassify some members, and finer-grained measurement is needed to find the real driver. · *model:* [[Map Is Not the Territory]]

**T4. An environment engineered toward an exposure defeats abstinence but yields to substitution** — When products are built to be craved, cheap and convenient, lasting reduction comes from incremental swaps and habits rather than strict elimination. · *model:* [[Harm Reduction]]
