# Ultra-Processed Foods

This topic brings together the vault's notes on ultra-processed foods (UPFs): how the category is defined and where that definition strains, what large cohort studies, a first depression trial and a growing body of brain-health findings say about harm, which mechanisms are proposed, whether processing itself or overall diet quality is what matters, and why a null colorectal cancer result and its expert critics show how much the answer depends on measurement.

## Defining UPFs: NOVA and Its Limits

The studies in the vault use the NOVA classification, which defines UPFs as industrial formulations with five or more ingredients rarely used in home cooking [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], such as soft drinks, packaged snacks, cereals, processed meats and fast food made with refined fats, sugars, starches, preservatives, colorings and emulsifiers [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]] [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. These foods make up a large share of Western diets, from roughly half of average US nutrition according to Food Tank speakers [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]] to nearly 60% of US adult calories in the prostate cancer study and about 60% of UK calorie intake [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The weakness is that NOVA measures processing, not nutrition: the Harvard team behind [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] argues it can label sound foods like fortified cereal or plant-based dairy as harmful, and the AMA now calls for educating the public on healthy versus unhealthy ultraprocessed foods [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. The sharpest critique in the vault comes from UK scientists who note that NOVA puts wholegrain bread and processed meat in one group, with one of them calling its retrospective use "research waste" [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]], while PROSPECT-UK's researchers observe that processed foods differ widely in ingredients, processing methods and nutritional makeup even within a single category [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]].

## Dose-Response Evidence Across Chronic Disease

The main evidence is a September 2026 meta-analysis of 51 prospective cohorts and about 8.8 million people, covered by three vault notes [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. The heaviest consumers had higher risk of digestive disease (31%), depression and anxiety (27%), cardiovascular events and metabolic syndrome or diabetes (24% each), obesity (23%), death (18%) and cancer (12%), and each extra 100 g per day added risk (14% cardiovascular, 11% blood pressure, 4% cancer, 3% mortality) with no apparent safe threshold. Because these conditions often occur together, the authors present cutting UPFs as a single lever against multimorbidity [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. Smaller studies point the same way: an NHANES analysis of 17,024 US men found 24-31% higher prostate cancer risk in higher-intake groups after adjusting for age, race, smoking and poverty [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], and a UK Biobank analysis of 56,964 adults found higher intake raised type 2 diabetes risk, while meeting the 150-minute weekly activity target offset part of the genetic risk, with low intake plus exercise tied to 49% lower risk in genetically susceptible people [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]].

## Brain Health: Silent Damage, Dementia and Stroke

A study of nearly 9,000 Canadian adults aged 35 to 69 found the heaviest UPF consumers 40% more likely to show white matter hyperintensities and silent brain infarcts on MRI, with the effect nearly twice as strong in women, though the absolute baseline is low (roughly 10% rising to 14%) and the cross-sectional design cannot show causation [[ultra-processed-foods-brain-injury-mri-middle-age]]. A second write-up of the same McMaster/PHRI study adds that top consumers also carried more body and visceral fat, that whole foods made up 41% of their intake versus 68% for the lowest consumers, and that the damage appeared in middle-aged adults without health concerns [[ultra-processed-foods-silent-brain-damage-mcmaster-jama-fox]]; it is the same evidence, not a replication, and it names longer-term studies with repeated MRI scans as the next step. A Healthline news summary of research on dementia and stroke reports that high UPF consumption correlates with higher rates of both, attributes this to persistent vascular and neural inflammation from additives and refined ingredients, and says substituting whole foods mitigates the risk [[ultra-processed-food-intake-dementia-stroke-risk]]; as a secondary summary it supplies the claim without the underlying study details. The Globe and Mail roundup adds that an Annual Review of Nutrition article links UPF-heavy diets to worse cognition and dementia risk through several pathways at once [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]], which together makes the brain a growing front for the topic.

## Early-Onset Bowel Cancer and Colorectal Cancer: A Test Case for Better Studies

Digestive disease showed the largest relative increase in the meta-analysis [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and bowel cancer in younger adults is the focus of the most ambitious new study in the vault. Early-onset bowel cancer rose in 27 of 50 countries in a 2024 PROSPECT analysis, England had the fastest rise in Europe from 2007 to 2017, and UK rates in 25- to 49-year-olds are up more than 50% since the early 1990s, yet no single diet or lifestyle factor has been shown to drive the trend [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. PROSPECT-UK, led by King's College London with ZOE, aims to recruit 250,000 adults aged 18 to 50 and is the first study to record diet at the level of specific brands, linked to long-term health records, so researchers can look past the broad "ultra-processed" label that [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] also questions. The strongest counterpoint is an NCI analysis of 461,682 adults followed for nearly 19 years, which found no association between total intake and colorectal cancer, with ultra-processed whole grains, fibre, dairy and calcium linked to lower incidence and ultra-processed meat to higher [[ultra-processed-foods-colorectal-cancer-nci-plos-medicine]]. Five UK nutrition scientists reacting to it agreed that UPF is too heterogeneous to treat as one group, but stressed that a single mid-1990s food frequency questionnaire cannot reliably capture processing or additives, that the older, mostly white cohort says little about early-onset disease, and that a null result does not show UPFs are harmless [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]].

## Mental Health: From Association to a First Intervention

Mental health is the only outcome in the vault where UPF research has moved from association to intervention, which ties this topic to the wider question of diet and mental health. The meta-analysis's 27% higher depression and anxiety risk is observational [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and Food Tank speakers cited strong evidence linking UPF-heavy diets to mental health conditions [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]]. In what its authors believe is the first before-and-after comparison within the same people, the UCSF pilot in *JAMA Psychiatry* had 20 metabolically unhealthy adults with depression cut UPF intake by more than 85% in a crossover design, and saw moderate to large improvements on three depression measures with smaller anxiety gains [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. The tiny sample shows a larger trial is feasible but proves nothing yet, with phase 2 enrollment not expected until 2028, and the Globe and Mail roundup likewise notes the pilot's small sample and four-week duration while calling it consistent with Mediterranean-diet trials [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]].

## Proposed Mechanisms

The notes keep pointing to the gut and inflammation: the meta-analysis authors suggest additives disrupt the gut microbiome [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], UCSF's Nyasha Chagwedera describes a "leaky, out-of-balance gut" whose low-grade inflammation may drive depression [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], Eric Verdin links diet-driven inflammation to impaired brain function [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]], and the PROSPECT team is studying the gut's bacteria, fungi and viruses alongside Harvard findings that cells may keep a "memory" of inflammation that shapes how bowel cancer develops [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. Lead author David Xiao Liu's "cocktail effect" hypothesis holds that combinations of additives may do more harm than any single one, supported by a French cohort of about 100,000 people whose additive exposures clustered into patterns, while heat-formed compounds (acrylamide, acrolein), nutrient loss and packaging microplastics are also proposed [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. There is also a behavioral mechanism, since manufacturers tune sugar, salt and fat to trigger dopamine-driven craving, which raises consumption and makes cutting back hard [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. For the ageing brain, inflammation, oxidative stress, impaired blood vessel function, insulin resistance and gut-microbiome disruption may act together [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]], which fits the small-vessel injury seen on MRI, and displacement of fruit, vegetables and whole grains is another suggested route, one that the colorectal-study experts also say a food questionnaire cannot capture [[ultra-processed-foods-brain-injury-mri-middle-age]] [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]].

## Processing or Diet Quality? Limits of the Evidence

The main counterweight is [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]], which followed more than 200,000 US adults over four decades and found that healthy diets lowered and unhealthy diets raised the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and death by about the same amount whether foods were ultra-processed or not, although the result has not yet been peer-reviewed. The other studies have gaps too: the meta-analysis relied on self-reported diets and could not fully rule out lifestyle confounders or establish causation [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], the prostate study's highest-intake subgroup was not statistically significant because it had few cases [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], the brain-imaging work evaluated participants only once [[ultra-processed-foods-silent-brain-damage-mcmaster-jama-fox]], and PROSPECT-UK needs a quarter of a million participants precisely because rare outcomes produce too few cases to separate signal from noise [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The notes therefore leave open whether harm comes from processing itself or from the poorer nutrition that usually comes with it: even the UCSF diet replaced UPFs with healthier foods and so cannot separate the two [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], and the roundup says researchers still cannot separate harm from processing, additives and by-products, or the poor nutrient profile, while some packaged whole-grain breads, fortified cereals and flavoured yogurts still supply calcium, protein, fibre and iron [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]]. The NCI colorectal result points the same way, since replacing 10% of ultra-processed weight with minimally processed food was tied to only 2% lower incidence [[ultra-processed-foods-colorectal-cancer-nci-plos-medicine]], and the expert reaction adds that results may reflect general diet quality rather than UPF itself, so the guidance to eat more fibre, whole grains and dairy and limit processed meat stands unchanged [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]].

## Practical Reduction: Gradual Swaps Over Elimination

Dietitians Johannah Katz and Vahista Ussery read the dose-response data as support for harm reduction, not elimination: keep whole foods as the base, choose smaller packaged portions, add a side salad and cook more at home [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]], while Liu advises building habits around vegetables, fruit and whole grains rather than strict rules [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. The Harvard results suggest choosing by nutrient density rather than processing label, since a healthy plant-based diet that included some processed foods beat an unhealthy minimally processed one [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]], and Cancer Research UK's interim advice for bowel cancer is similarly broad: more fibre and whole foods, less red and processed meat, staying active, keeping a healthy weight, limiting alcohol and not smoking [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. Change is still hard because UPFs are cheap, convenient and engineered to be craved, and even the UCSF participants made their 85%-plus cut with research-team guidance on what to buy [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. Beck's advice is reduction rather than perfection, such as fruit, nuts or plain popcorn for granola bars and oatmeal for sugary cereal [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]], the Healthline summary on dementia frames whole-food substitution as the preventive step [[ultra-processed-food-intake-dementia-stroke-risk]], and the NCI-study coverage adds screening from age 45 [[ultra-processed-foods-colorectal-cancer-nci-plos-medicine]].

## Policy, Labeling and Food Is Medicine

Regulation lags the research: only Colombia requires national labels identifying ultra-processed products [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and the prostate cancer researchers compare UPFs to tobacco, where decades passed between early signs of harm and policy action [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]]. Change is appearing first in medicine, as the AMA's 2026 food-is-medicine policy expanded its UPF position to include public education and multilingual patient materials [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. At Food Tank's Capitol Hill event, speakers called the US system "sickcare," noted that 90% of 2022's $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending went to patients with chronic disease, and pointed to rare bipartisan interest in nutrition policy [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]].

## 🧠 Core Principles

**T1. Risk scales continuously with exposure** — When harm rises with every increment of intake and no threshold appears, there is no safe "moderate" zone, and every partial reduction buys some benefit. · *model:* [[Dose-Response Relationship]]

**T2. Association cannot separate an exposure from what travels with it** — Observational data link an exposure to outcomes but cannot by themselves rule out the habits and nutrients that accompany it, so they narrow down suspects without proving causes. · *model:* [[Correlation vs Causation]]

**T3. A coarse category or coarse measurement hides the attribute that drives outcomes** — When harm depends on a property that varies within a category, or on detail the measuring instrument cannot see, the label misclassifies members and a null or positive overall result is weak evidence, so finer-grained measurement is needed to find the real driver. · *model:* [[Map Is Not the Territory]]

**T4. An environment engineered toward an exposure defeats abstinence but yields to substitution** — When products are built to be craved, cheap and convenient, lasting reduction comes from incremental swaps and habits rather than strict elimination. · *model:* [[Harm Reduction]]
