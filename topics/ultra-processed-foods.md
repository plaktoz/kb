# Ultra-Processed Foods

This topic brings together the vault's notes on ultra-processed foods (UPFs): how the category is defined and where that definition strains, what large cohorts, a small depression pilot and a growing set of brain and child studies say about harm, which mechanisms are proposed, whether processing or overall diet quality is what matters, how poorly people perceive their own intake, and why a null colorectal cancer result shows how much the answer depends on measurement.

## Defining UPFs: NOVA and Its Limits

The studies in the vault use the NOVA classification, which defines UPFs as industrial formulations with five or more ingredients rarely used in home cooking [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. The category takes in soft drinks, packaged snacks, cereals and processed meats built from refined fats, sugars, starches and additives [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], along with preservatives, colorings, flavor enhancers and emulsifiers [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. Such foods make up a large share of diets: Food Tank speakers put them at about half of average American nutrition [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]], the prostate study at nearly 60% of US adult calories [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], and PROSPECT-UK at about 60% of UK calorie intake [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The weakness of the label is that NOVA measures processing rather than nutrition. The Harvard team behind [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]] argues that it can flag fortified cereal or plant-based dairy as harmful, and the AMA's 2026 policy likewise distinguishes healthy from unhealthy ultraprocessed foods when it asks for public education [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. The sharpest critique comes from UK scientists who note that NOVA places wholegrain bread in the same group as processed meat and call retrospective use of the classification a poor use of research effort [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]], while PROSPECT-UK's team points out that processed foods differ in ingredients, processing methods and nutritional makeup even within a single category [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]].

## Dose-Response Evidence Across Chronic Disease

The main evidence is a September 2026 meta-analysis of 51 prospective cohorts with about 8.8 million people, covered by three vault notes [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. The heaviest consumers faced higher risk of digestive disease, depression and anxiety, cardiovascular events, metabolic syndrome or diabetes, obesity, death and cancer, and each extra 100 grams a day added risk with no apparent safe threshold [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. Because these conditions often occur together, the authors treat cutting UPFs as a single lever against multimorbidity [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. Smaller studies point the same way. An NHANES analysis of 17,024 US men found 24-31% higher prostate cancer risk among higher consumers after adjusting for age, race, smoking and poverty [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], and a UK Biobank analysis of 56,964 adults found that higher intake raised type 2 diabetes risk while meeting the weekly activity target offset part of the genetic risk [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]].

## Brain Health: Silent Damage, Dementia, Stroke and Reward

A McMaster-led study of nearly 9,000 Canadian adults aged 35 to 69 found the heaviest UPF consumers 40% more likely to show white matter hyperintensities and silent brain infarcts on MRI, with the association nearly twice as strong in women [[ultra-processed-foods-brain-injury-mri-middle-age]]. The absolute baseline is low, roughly 10% rising to 14%, and the cross-sectional design cannot show causation [[ultra-processed-foods-brain-injury-mri-middle-age]]. A second write-up of the same study adds that top consumers also carried more body and visceral fat, that whole foods made up 41% of their intake against 68% for the lowest consumers, and that the damage appeared in middle-aged adults without health concerns; it is the same evidence rather than a replication [[ultra-processed-foods-silent-brain-damage-mcmaster-jama-fox]]. The evidence reaches younger people too. A yearlong USC study of 46 children aged 7 to 10 linked higher UPF intake to stronger dorsal striatum responses to appetizing food images, and those responses were associated with greater body fat gain a year later [[ultra-processed-foods-childrens-brain-reward-response-body-fat-usc]]. Accounting for the brain response weakened the UPF-BMI and UPF-body fat links by 31% and 44%, but because the study is observational it cannot show that UPFs alter the brain or cause weight gain, or which way the link runs [[ultra-processed-foods-childrens-brain-reward-response-body-fat-usc]]. A Healthline news summary reports that high UPF consumption correlates with higher rates of dementia and stroke and that whole-food substitution mitigates the risk [[ultra-processed-food-intake-dementia-stroke-risk]], though as a secondary summary it supplies the claim without study details. The Globe and Mail roundup adds an Annual Review of Nutrition article describing several brain pathways that may act together, namely inflammation, oxidative stress, impaired vessel function, insulin resistance and gut-microbiome disruption [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]].

## Early-Onset Bowel Cancer and Colorectal Cancer: A Test Case for Better Studies

Digestive disease showed the largest relative increase in the meta-analysis [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and bowel cancer in younger adults is the focus of the most ambitious new study in the vault. Early-onset bowel cancer rose in 27 of 50 countries in a 2024 PROSPECT analysis, and UK rates in 25- to 49-year-olds are up more than 50% since the early 1990s, yet no single diet or lifestyle factor has been shown to drive the trend [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. PROSPECT-UK aims to recruit 250,000 adults aged 18 to 50 and records diet at brand level, linked to long-term health records, so that researchers can look past the broad "ultra-processed" label; the scale is needed because the disease is rare [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. The strongest counterpoint is an NCI analysis of 461,682 adults followed for nearly 19 years, which found no association between total intake and colorectal cancer, with ultra-processed whole grains, fibre, dairy and calcium linked to lower incidence and ultra-processed meat to higher [[ultra-processed-foods-colorectal-cancer-nci-plos-medicine]]. Replacing 10% of ultra-processed weight with minimally processed food was tied to 2% lower incidence, and the coverage advises screening from age 45 [[ultra-processed-foods-colorectal-cancer-nci-plos-medicine]]. Five UK nutrition scientists reacting to it agreed that UPF is too heterogeneous to treat as one group, but argued that a single mid-1990s food frequency questionnaire cannot reliably capture processing or additives, that the older, mostly white cohort says little about early-onset disease, and that a null result does not show UPFs are harmless; they suggested the findings may reflect overall diet quality [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]].

## Mental Health: From Association to a Small Intervention Test

Mental health is the outcome where UPF research has moved furthest from association toward intervention, which ties this topic to the wider question of diet and mood. The meta-analysis's 27% higher depression and anxiety risk is observational [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and Food Tank speakers cited strong evidence linking UPF-heavy diets to mental health conditions [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]]. In a UCSF pilot published in *JAMA Psychiatry*, 20 metabolically unhealthy adults with depression cut UPF intake by more than 85% in a crossover design and showed moderate to large improvements on three depression measures, with modest anxiety gains; the authors believe it is the first before-and-after comparison within the same people [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. Scores fell 11 points on the low-processed diet against 4 on the usual diet, a gap that a 2-point shift in clinical terms would make large [[cutting-ultra-processed-food-depression-pilot-caveats-mindgrow]]. The design, however, cannot say whether removing bad food or adding good food drove the effect: there was no alternative healthy-diet arm, no blinding, participants knew what they were eating, and expectation or research-team attention could contribute [[cutting-ultra-processed-food-depression-pilot-caveats-mindgrow]]. All participants had metabolic disease, so the result may not carry over to metabolically healthy people, and the sample is small and the diets lasted four weeks [[cutting-ultra-processed-food-depression-pilot-caveats-mindgrow]] [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]]. The UCSF team plans a larger phase 2 trial with enrolment expected from 2028 [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]].

## Proposed Mechanisms

The notes keep returning to the gut and inflammation, though none of them has yet measured the full chain. The meta-analysis authors list additive combinations, gut-microbiome disruption, heat-formed acrylamide and acrolein, nutrient loss and packaging microplastics as candidate routes [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]] [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. Lead author David Xiao Liu's "cocktail effect" hypothesis, that additive mixtures may do more harm than any single additive, draws on a French cohort of about 100,000 people whose additive exposures clustered into recognisable patterns [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. In the UCSF pilot, Nyasha Chagwedera describes a "leaky, out-of-balance gut" whose low-grade inflammation may drive depression [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], Eric Verdin links diet-driven inflammation to impaired brain function [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]], and PROSPECT-UK is studying gut bacteria, fungi and viruses alongside Harvard findings that cells may keep a "memory" of inflammation that shapes bowel cancer [[prospect-uk-study-early-onset-bowel-cancer-diet-lifestyle]]. A behavioural route also runs through the same study: manufacturers tune sugar, salt and fat to trigger dopamine-driven craving, which raises consumption [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. The USC children's findings add a reward-circuit angle, since the dorsal striatum response sits on the path from UPF intake to body fat [[ultra-processed-foods-childrens-brain-reward-response-body-fat-usc]]. For the ageing brain, the pathways listed in the roundup may act together, and the small-vessel injury seen on MRI fits this picture; displacement of fruit, vegetables and whole grains is another suggested route that food questionnaires cannot capture [[ultra-processed-foods-brain-injury-mri-middle-age]] [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]]. A caution comes from the depression pilot's authors, who note that an earlier theory that gut problems cause mental illness collapsed for lack of a measurable mechanism, and that the trial measured only food and mood, not the gut steps [[cutting-ultra-processed-food-depression-pilot-caveats-mindgrow]].

## Processing or Diet Quality? Limits of the Evidence

The main counterweight is the Harvard analysis of more than 200,000 US adults followed for four decades, which found that healthy diets lowered and unhealthy diets raised the risk of heart disease, type 2 diabetes and death by similar amounts whether foods were ultra-processed or minimally processed [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]]. That result is preliminary, presented at a conference and not yet peer-reviewed [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]]. Other studies have gaps of their own: the meta-analysis relied on self-reported diets and could not fully rule out lifestyle confounders [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], the prostate study's highest-intake group was not statistically significant, probably because it had few cases [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]], the brain-imaging work measured participants only once [[ultra-processed-foods-silent-brain-damage-mcmaster-jama-fox]], and the children's study cannot establish direction [[ultra-processed-foods-childrens-brain-reward-response-body-fat-usc]]. The notes therefore leave open whether harm comes from processing itself or from the poorer nutrition that usually accompanies it. Even the UCSF diet replaced UPFs with healthier foods, so it cannot separate the two [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], and the Globe and Mail roundup notes that researchers still cannot disentangle processing, additives and the typically poor nutrient profile, while some packaged whole-grain breads, fortified cereals and flavoured yogurts still supply calcium, protein, fibre and iron [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]]. The NCI colorectal result points the same way, and the expert reaction adds that it may reflect general diet quality rather than UPF itself [[expert-reaction-ultra-processed-food-colorectal-cancer-plos-medicine]].

## Practical Reduction: Gradual Swaps Over Elimination

Dietitians Johannah Katz and Vahista Ussery read the dose-response data as support for harm reduction rather than elimination: keep whole foods as the base, choose smaller packaged portions, add a side salad and cook more at home [[ultra-processed-foods-multimorbidity-dietitian-guidance]]. Liu likewise advises building habits around vegetables, fruit and whole grains rather than strict rules [[ultra-processed-foods-dose-response-disease-risk-meta-analysis]]. The Harvard results suggest choosing by nutrient density rather than by processing label, since a healthy plant-based diet that included some processed foods beat an unhealthy minimally processed one [[diet-quality-versus-food-processing-level-study]]. Change is hard because UPFs are cheap, convenient and engineered to be craved, and even the UCSF participants made their cut with research-team guidance on what to buy [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]]. The USC study frames the same problem as one of environment: affordability, convenience and access shape what families can realistically eat, and its proposed trial would swap in minimally processed foods [[ultra-processed-foods-childrens-brain-reward-response-body-fat-usc]]. Beck's advice is reduction rather than perfection, such as fruit, nuts or plain popcorn in place of granola bars and oatmeal in place of sugary cereal [[ultra-processed-foods-diabetes-mood-brain-aging-research-roundup]], and the Healthline summary frames whole-food substitution as the preventive step [[ultra-processed-food-intake-dementia-stroke-risk]].

## Awareness, Policy and Food Is Medicine

Public perception lags well behind intake. In a Talker Research survey of 2,000 Americans, respondents put UPFs at 16% of their diet against the CDC's 53%, and only 9% could correctly identify common items such as bread, cereal and deli meat as ultra-processed; 61% thought avoiding them was nearly impossible [[americans-underestimate-ultra-processed-food-intake-talker-survey]]. Respondents estimated about $128,149 in lifetime spending on these foods, while health economist Lindsay Allen puts the hidden healthcare and productivity costs above $350,000, and 42% said knowing the long-term costs might lead them to eat less [[americans-underestimate-ultra-processed-food-intake-talker-survey]]. Regulation lags the research: only Colombia requires national labels identifying ultra-processed products [[ultra-processed-foods-disease-risk-meta-analysis]], and the prostate cancer researchers compare the UPF evidence to tobacco, where decades passed between early signs of harm and policy action [[ultra-processed-foods-prostate-cancer-risk]]. Change is appearing first in medicine, as the AMA's 2026 food-is-medicine policy recognised medically tailored meals, groceries and produce prescriptions and expanded its UPF position to cover public education and multilingual patient materials [[ama-food-is-medicine-policy-2026]]. At Food Tank's Capitol Hill event, speakers called the US system "sickcare," noted that 90% of 2022's $4.5 trillion in healthcare spending went to patients with chronic disease, and pointed to rare bipartisan interest in nutrition policy [[food-tank-nutrition-metabolic-mental-health]].

**T2. Risk rises with every increment of exposure** — When harm increases steadily with intake and no cutoff appears, there is no safe "moderate" zone, so each partial reduction carries some benefit. · *model:* [[Dose-Response Relationship]]

**T3.
