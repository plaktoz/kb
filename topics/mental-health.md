# Mental Health

This topic tracks what the vault knows about depression, anxiety and PTSD: where the risk comes from, how hidden or habitual forms show up, and which treatments (from exercise and heat to brain stimulation and AI chatbots) have evidence behind them. Diet gets a deeper treatment in [[diet-mental-health]], and youth-specific findings in [[child-adolescent-health]].

## Early-Life Adversity Leaves Lasting Marks

Several large studies point to childhood as the period when mental-health risk is most strongly shaped, though not in one uniform way. The UCLA-led ENIGMA mega-analysis of 3,711 people found that more severe abuse and neglect go with a "neurobiological scar" of structural deviations that thin some regions while thickening others, varying by maltreatment type, age and sex and strongest in young women; the authors tie this to childhood plasticity and note that maltreatment is a major risk factor for both major depression and PTSD [[childhood-abuse-brain-structure-enigma-mega-analysis]]. Adversity is also wider than trauma, since in roughly 30,000 children household unpredictability (irregular routines, parental mood swings) predicted depression better than traditional adversity screens, with a 12-fold versus 8-fold risk gap [[childhood-unpredictability-screening-mental-health]]. Even early nutrition seems to count: UK adults who had less sugar in their first 1,000 days showed 11% lower depression risk and 20% lower anxiety risk, plus gray-matter differences decades later [[sugar-restriction-infancy-dementia-alzheimers-risk]].

## Separating Cause from Correlation

Much of the field's evidence is observational, and the vault's notes keep showing how raw associations shrink or change once shared causes are controlled. The Karolinska study of 21,000 Swedish twins found that genes and upbringing explain much of the link between heavy screen use and depression or anxiety, with the association weakening most in identical-twin comparisons but not disappearing for interactive use in adolescence [[twin-study-screen-time-mental-health-family-factors]]. Detection is a confounder too, because women with type 2 diabetes developed more anxiety and depression than men (8.1% vs. 5.3%) but also averaged 92 primary-care visits to men's 32, so part of the gap may just be earlier recording [[type-2-diabetes-mental-health-sex-differences]]. Causation can also run backward, as the UK Biobank sleep study warns that disrupted sleep may be a symptom of depression rather than its cause [[rem-sleep-lower-risk-83-diseases]], and cross-sectional findings such as higher PHQ-9 scores among calorie-restricted dieters carry the same limits [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]].

## Comorbidity and High-Exposure Populations

Depression and anxiety often arrive alongside physical illness or occupational strain, and general-population services can miss them. About one in three stroke survivors develops depression and up to a quarter develop anxiety, both of which slow rehabilitation, while nearly 44% of stroke caregivers show depressive symptoms, which is why the 2026 guidelines made mental-health screening standard [[stroke-rehab-guidelines-2026-whole-person-recovery]]. In diabetes care, women aged 16–39 had the highest probability of developing mental-health conditions, and experts called for routine psychological screening [[type-2-diabetes-mental-health-sex-differences]]. First responders and military personnel face cumulative occupational trauma that differs from single-incident PTSD, and because their training teaches them to suppress distress, systems that depend on self-reporting fail them structurally [[mental-health-first-responders-military-indiana-2026]].

## Hidden Depression and Anxiety as Habit

Distress does not always look like withdrawal: "overfunctioning" describes depression hidden behind full calendars, where busyness stops giving stability and becomes a way to avoid internal experience, and one sign is that finishing a task brings no relief because another immediately takes its place [[productivity-hides-depression-overfunctioning]]. Jud Brewer's account of anxiety as a learned habit runs on the same loop, because worry feels productive under uncertainty and the brain reinforces it, while under stress willpower is unreliable and curiosity-driven awareness is what breaks the cycle [[anxiety-as-learned-habit-brewer-browns-staff-day]]. Both accounts suggest the problem behavior survives because it relieves discomfort in the short term, not because it helps. Stigma reinforces the hiding, with 42% of surveyed employees fearing that discussing mental health would hurt their careers [[productivity-hides-depression-overfunctioning]].

## Movement as a Transdiagnostic Treatment

Exercise evidence is moving from "it helps" toward explaining how it helps: in a secondary analysis of the 400-person ImPuls trial, lower perceived stress and less repetitive negative thinking fully explained the programme's benefit across depression, anxiety disorders, PTSD and insomnia, which suggests shared mechanisms rather than disorder-specific effects [[exercise-mental-health-stress-negative-thinking]]. Formats that combine movement with social contact also show results, as an eight-week salsa programme cut PHQ-9 scores by a clinically meaningful 2.45 points versus waitlist in young adults and also reduced social anxiety [[salsa-dancing-depression-young-adults]]. In older adults, a meta-analysis of 18 RCTs found that the low-intensity mind-body exercise Baduanjin reduced depressive symptoms and improved sleep, though high heterogeneity calls for caution [[baduanjin-exercise-sleep-depression-older-adults-meta-analysis]].

## Sleep as Mediator and Target

Sleep keeps appearing as a link between mental-health symptoms and how people function. In early adolescence, sleep quality explained 36.3% of the association between anxiety and worse academic and social outcomes, and 18.5% for depression, far more than screen time did [[adolescent-sleep-academic-social-outcomes]]. In adults, irregular sleep went with anxiety and major depression, and deep sleep with lower depression risk, subject to the reverse-causation caveat [[rem-sleep-lower-risk-83-diseases]]. That has made sleep a direct treatment target: the NEUSLeeP ultrasound patch brought REM onset 43 minutes earlier and raised heart-rate variability, and it is headed for trials in PTSD, depression and insomnia, since REM is framed as the brain's emotional reset [[wearable-ultrasound-patch-rem-sleep]].

## Emerging Physiological Therapies

A new set of interventions works on the body's physiology rather than on thoughts or neurotransmitters. Heat therapy rests on the observation that depressed people "run hot," since in wearable data from 20,000 people worse depression went with higher skin temperature and less nightly cooling; a small RCT of whole-body infrared heating to 38.5°C halved symptoms within a week for most patients, with effects lasting six weeks, and pilots pairing CBT with heat left most participants in remission, possibly via an exercise-like anti-inflammatory response and serotonin activation [[heat-therapy-sauna-mental-health-depression-research]]. Separately, a single 20-minute session of noninvasive temporal interference stimulation reached the ventral striatum, the reward region underlying anhedonia, and reduced anxiety for about a week [[temporal-interference-stimulation-reward-anxiety-emory]]. Like the NEUSLeeP patch [[wearable-ultrasound-patch-rem-sleep]], it promises deep-brain targeting without surgery.

## Diet as Risk and Treatment

Diet is moving from a correlate toward an intervention for depression: the SMILES trial found 32% remission with Mediterranean-style dietary counseling versus 8% with social support, and the proposed mechanisms are inflammation and gut-derived serotonin [[nutrition-and-mental-health-link]]. A 20-person UCSF crossover pilot in which participants cut ultra-processed food by more than 85% produced moderate-to-large improvements on three depression measures, which researchers attribute to gut-lining damage and the inflammation it triggers [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], and adding fruit juice to a 5-a-day intervention lowered PHQ-9 scores by 2.52 points over four weeks [[fruit-juice-lower-depression-scores]]. Restriction can backfire through nutrient deficits [[low-calorie-diets-may-increase-depression]], and the fuller synthesis is in [[diet-mental-health]].

## AI Chatbots and the Treatment Gap

AI tools are being pitched at a large unmet need, since about 25% of people worldwide experience a mental-health condition and about 85% get no adequate care [[ai-mental-health-chatbots-empathetic-safety-lags]]. A scoping review of 21 studies found that LLM chatbots, mostly built on CBT and aimed at depression and anxiety, score well on satisfaction and perceived empathy, but engagement falls off over multi-week use, empathy does not reliably lead to better clinical outcomes, and crisis handling remains a critical failure mode [[ai-mental-health-chatbots-safety-evidence-review]]. Georgia Tech researchers see promise in AI that spots behavioral changes before a crisis but warn that more AI interaction may deepen isolation: "The use of AI is encouraged, but reliance on AI is not" [[ai-healthcare-revolution-diagnosis-wellness]]. The 2026 state bans on therapy chatbots, which protect the role of licensed human professionals in complex trauma care, reflect this evidence gap [[mental-health-first-responders-military-indiana-2026]].

## A Field of Promising but Preliminary Evidence

Many of the most striking findings come from very small or early studies, so the vault holds them as hypotheses rather than settled results. The heat-therapy RCT had 30 participants and its CBT pilots were not powered to prove efficacy [[heat-therapy-sauna-mental-health-depression-research]], the UCSF diet pilot had 20 participants [[ultra-processed-food-reduction-depression-ucsf-pilot]], and the fruit-juice trial had 42 and was funded by the Fruit Juice Science Centre [[fruit-juice-lower-depression-scores]]. Temporal interference stimulation is still investigational [[temporal-interference-stimulation-reward-anxiety-emory]], and most chatbot studies test early prototypes rather than validated clinical tools [[ai-mental-health-chatbots-safety-evidence-review]]. The better-established findings rest on larger designs, such as the 400-person ImPuls trial and a 2026 behavioral-activation meta-analysis of 105 trials with benefits lasting 12 months [[exercise-mental-health-stress-negative-thinking]] [[productivity-hides-depression-overfunctioning]].

**T1. **T2. Inputs during developmental windows have outsized, lasting effects on mental-health risk** — Because the brain is most plastic while its networks are forming, early adversity, instability and nutrition shape brain structure and adult risk for depression and anxiety in ways that do not follow a single fixed pattern. · *model:* [[Critical Period Hypothesis]]

**T3. Interventions on shared upstream mechanisms improve many conditions at once** — Stress reactivity, repetitive negative thinking, sleep, inflammation and thermoregulation sit beneath several diagnoses, so acting on one of them improves depression, anxiety and PTSD together rather than one disorder at a time.

**T4. Behaviors that bring momentary relief are reinforced whether or not they help** — Worry and compulsive busyness persist because they ease uncertainty or inner discomfort in the short term, so avoidance can pass for coping until it is consciously noticed. · *model:* [[Habit Loop]]

**T5. Easy-to-measure proxies diverge from the mental-health outcomes they stand in for** — When satisfaction, perceived empathy or physical recovery metrics become the target, systems optimize for them while clinical benefit, safety and harder-to-quantify mental health go unmeasured or decline. · *model:* [[Goodhart's Law]]
