# AI in Education

AI in education pairs the promise of patient, personalized tutoring at scale with mounting evidence that the same tools, deployed with answer-giving defaults, without human accountability, or against unredesigned assessments, produce the feeling of learning without its substance, so interaction design, the human layer around the tool, and equity remain the decisive variables, while the newest work adds that usage counts are a weak proxy for learning and that the evidence base is thinner than its headlines suggest.

## The Promise: Personalization at Scale

Large language models are turning historically rule-based Intelligent Tutoring Systems into conversational environments that generate explanations, exercises, and feedback on demand, which is why researchers see them dissolving the old tradeoff between costly one-to-one tutoring and generic mass instruction [[generative-ai-personalized-intelligent-tutoring-systems]]. In classrooms the promise mostly arrives as an interactive layer over existing material: a Fresno professor lets students ask for another explanation in the moment [[ai-in-classroom-fresno-teachers]], districts facing record-low reading scores use tools like Reading Progress to flag read-aloud errors faster than a teacher with 150 students could [[k12-districts-boost-literacy-with-ai-technology]], and CS50's Duck shortened office-hour waits that once stretched to an hour while making it socially cheaper to ask questions [[cs50-duck-ai-tutor-malan-interview]]. Maya Bialik separates a "learning assistant" that tutors on demand from a "doing assistant" that should take over only tasks not central to the learning goal, much as calculators freed students for harder work once basics were mastered [[irreplaceable-maya-bialik-ai-teaching-framework]]. Learners are receptive without being enthusiastic: in a Kaspersky survey of 7,200 people across 18 countries, Gen Z was only slightly likelier than others to name personalised AI tutors the most promising education technology (18% versus 16%) [[gen-z-ai-tutors-virtual-classrooms-kaspersky-survey]].

## The Tutoring Evidence: Design Decides the Outcome

The research question has shifted from whether AI tutoring works to which kind does. A review from the Overdeck Family Foundation puts the central pattern plainly: AI tutors lift performance while students are using them, but the gains often fail to transfer to new problems unless the tutor is built to preserve reasoning [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]]. Its examples fall on both sides. Medly, a tutor of the reasoning-preserving kind, was associated with a 0.33 SD gain on new, unpracticed science questions across 929 students, while the Goblins platform showed gains of 0.11 to 0.31 SD that rose with use, though only correlationally. The Bastani et al. PNAS experiment with about 1,000 Turkish high schoolers found an unguarded GPT-4 interface boosted assisted practice yet cut unassisted exam scores 17%, while a hint-based tutor largely removed that harm without beating control [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]] [[ai-learning-findings-august-2026]] [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]]. [[current-evidence-ai-tutoring-impact-learners]] sets that result against Harvard's custom physics tutor, which roughly doubled active-learning gains in a 194-student crossover trial, with effect sizes of 0.73 to 1.3 SD. The 6,000-student Tennessee experiment found AI help lifted fractions scores about three points only when paired with a three-in-a-row mastery rule and mistake-specific walkthroughs, with gains confined to easy, practiced questions [[ai-mastery-learning-tennessee-fractions-study]], and a World Bank evaluation in Edo State, Nigeria, found a six-week, teacher-guided Copilot English program produced gains comparable to roughly 1.5 to 2 years of typical progress in similar settings [[world-bank-nigeria-ai-tutoring-six-week-program]].

Human-AI combinations offer the strongest gains reported so far. Tutor CoPilot, a Stanford tool that assists human tutors, produced a four-point mastery gain, and nine points for lower-rated tutors, while in Eedi and Google DeepMind's human-supervised program tutors approved more than three-quarters of AI messages with little editing [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]]. The same review is cautious about the rest of the picture: a hybrid of i-Ready and human tutors showed no difference from controls on state assessments, and the field still lacks evidence that AI tutoring matches high-impact human tutoring, which costs roughly $1,200 to $2,000 or more per student. Reviews converge on the same conditional verdict. A 35-study analysis cited by the University of Dayton found a net positive effect of ChatGPT that depended on instructional method [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]], a University of Minnesota consensus of 30 learning scientists placed AI's promise at the practice stage rather than initial acquisition or application [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]], and even purpose-built tutors fall short unevenly, since Khanmigo neither tailored tasks nor supported metacognition [[current-evidence-ai-tutoring-impact-learners]].

The most detailed human-supervised trial to date comes from Eedi and Google DeepMind. Across ten weeks and 1,179 students in ten UK schools, students working with AI or human tutors answered 56 to 58% of in-lesson questions correctly, against 43% in a static-content group, yet the standardized Star Maths test showed no clear gain. Tutors left 94.7% of AI drafts unchanged, and false mathematical statements were rare in both arms (seven AI messages, 0.01%, and seven human messages, 0.02%). The results were uneven. Girls appeared to benefit and boys to be harmed, though the gap is tentative, and adding learning history did not clearly help. Tutors also found supervising drafts less rewarding than writing their own messages. The program's earlier gains appeared only after a year or more, and the next trial runs for a year with more than 15,000 students under human-on-the-loop supervision [[eedi-google-deepmind-ai-tutor-rct-full-findings]].

## Why Frictionless Help Fails

[[guidelines-for-learning-and-ai]] locates the failure in the See, Do, Feedback loop: AI can legitimately aid perception and review, but when it performs the doing, the productive struggle that builds skill vanishes. [[five-rules-learning-age-of-ai-scott-young]] adds an autopilot warning, that habitual automation erodes the very competence meant to serve as a backup. A systematic review of 22 studies names the mechanism as cognitive offloading, finding that direct answers move monitoring and evaluation onto the AI and induce "metacognitive laziness," and that ChatGPT can raise performance while students' self-evaluation fails to track it [[ai-impact-student-metacognition-systematic-review]]. The damage can appear quickly. Three randomized trials with 1,222 participants found that ten minutes of AI help left people making more errors or quitting once it was withdrawn, while unassisted groups kept going, an effect the researchers link to getting used to instant answers so that slower effort feels inefficient [[ten-minutes-ai-use-erodes-persistence-study]]. The same pattern surfaces in reporting on people performing worse and giving up sooner after AI access ended [[ai-harming-young-minds-happiness-guardian]] and in a global education digest's early signs of skills erosion [[daily-ai-summary-education-skills-erosion-policy-oct-2026]]. Longer-run evidence points the same way. Trent Cash of the University of Waterloo, summarized in Science News Explores, notes that doctors' polyp detection worsened when they worked alone after three months of AI help, that high-school maths students did worse once a ChatGPT-like tool was removed, and that SAT practice users struggled without AI and skipped more questions [[ai-skill-retention-offloading-vs-tutoring-cash-sungu]]. Teachers interviewed across 13 countries for Cambridge describe the classroom version, with students turning to AI before attempting a task and losing confidence in their own thinking, and with AI helping most the learners who already study independently [[cambridge-study-ai-english-teaching-teachers-drivers-seat]]. Erosion also hides behind a sense of momentum: 63% of 5,050 Preply respondents felt busy without improving [[preply-progress-report-ai-learning-progress-gap]]. Learners often sense the cost without changing course, as 39% of teens feel they miss out on learning when they lean on AI even as roughly seven in ten keep using it for schoolwork [[teens-ai-academic-support-common-sense-survey]], and 67% of students in RAND's panel fear AI harms critical thinking while homework use climbed from 48% to 62% [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]].

Stanford's review of 20 causal studies, drawn from more than 800 examined, sharpens the picture by tool type: general-purpose chatbots were the most detrimental, while scaffolded and Socratic tools showed less clear harm [[stanford-cognitive-offloading-ai-learning-tradeoffs]]. A Brazilian study reported in the same note found that middle schoolers who lost AI access scored worse on creative tasks than students who never had it, with the drop concentrated among heavy users. Its takeaway, that no thinking means no learning, turns into a design task of balancing the friction that builds thinking against the abandonment that friction can cause.

A University of Minnesota expert panel has tried to map where AI helps and harms learning process by process. Of 65 learning processes, experts reached consensus on 42, and their pattern, which they call the GenAI Paradox, is that acquisition is vulnerable, practice and consolidation are enhanced, and higher-order thinking is vulnerable, with 10 of 18 higher-order items judged vulnerable rather than enhanced. Help-seeking was the only self-regulation process that reached consensus for enhancement. Experts rated these processes as highly important but rated the supporting evidence as weak, and they judged elementary students the most vulnerable [[umn-expert-consensus-genai-paradox-k20-learning]]. A companion survey report led by Kendeou warns that careless GenAI use could cause severe and one-sided disruption, especially in the elementary grades, and recommends building foundational skills before introducing AI, with cognitive thinking, learning skills, collaboration, social-emotional development, and human connection named as the areas at risk [[umn-report-student-ai-use-severe-threat-learning-mpr]].

## Defaults and Engineered Friction

Because the same model can scaffold or supply, its configuration largely determines what students do with it, and availability alone does not produce use. Khanmigo illustrates the gap: in a two-year Tennessee study, 96% of students tried it, yet they used it in only 17% of mistake exercises, and in a separate case nearly half of elementary students assigned an AI literacy platform never used it [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]]. When students are free to choose, the default reasserts itself. In a Khanmigo study in 18 Tennessee middle schools, as summarized by Kentz, students who could pick a tool chose the answer-giving chatbot, median use was about a third of school days, and conversations rarely concerned math. Mandated use fixed participation but not effort, which led Kentz to propose grading the AI conversation transcript itself, with role-play "friction bots" and teacher-in-the-loop evaluation. The support for that proposal is a small pilot in which 18 of 21 students changed their approach and confident users rose from 2 to 11, with five calculus retakers averaging about 34 points higher. There was no control group, and the author's company sells the tool [[ai-tutors-optional-fail-conversation-as-assessment-kentz]]. The metacognition review found every one of five Guided Interaction studies reported positive metacognitive outcomes, yet 15 of 20 classifiable studies used Direct Feedback, mostly through general-purpose tools [[ai-impact-student-metacognition-systematic-review]]. Maryland's Virtual Study Assistant shows the default effect in the wild: few instructors switched from answer-giving mode to tutor mode, and 73.8% of users sought information or solutions while only 0.7% asked for feedback on their own work [[umd-virtual-study-assistant-rct-low-uptake]]. Friction can be engineered on purpose. Sungu's hint tool, which withheld answers, matched book study, and Benjamin Lira Luttges found AI-rewritten cover letters taught as well as human feedback when learners tinkered with them rather than accepting them [[ai-skill-retention-offloading-vs-tutoring-cash-sungu]]. CS50 layers Socratic prompts, code-rejecting responses, and a Zelda-style "heart system" rate limit, yet the Duck still emits code blocks about a quarter of the time [[cs50-duck-ai-tutor-malan-interview]]. Google's Guided Learning mode, built around productive struggle, is the same idea in a consumer product. It is coming to Search in a pilot at select US schools and is globally available in Classroom [[google-guided-learning-search-classroom-school-controls]]. Policy is beginning to codify the principle, with the UK Department for Education requiring AI tutors to withhold direct answers by default under human oversight [[current-evidence-ai-tutoring-impact-learners]], StarSpark aiming for the smallest nudge that keeps a student at the edge of their ability [[ai-learning-findings-august-2026]], and Dayton drawing its line between "legitimate" use that amplifies a student's own work and "corrosive" use that replaces it [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]].

Texas and Stanford show the same choice at the level of policy and course design. Aroutis Foster proposes asking whether an AI tool makes a student better at the skill or merely produces a better finished product, and the Texas examples favour tools that guide rather than answer. Its math example contrasts a hint offered after a student's attempt with a generated solution [[texas-schools-ai-enhance-not-replace-learning]]. Dallas ISD bars AI for elementary and middle school students, Houston ISD is expanding its "Future 2" program to 25 more campuses, and nearly three-quarters of parents say their children use AI occasionally. At Stanford, the STEPS tutor and BungoBot ask students questions instead of answering them, and students first resented the slowdown before valuing it [[stanford-instructors-ai-tools-deeper-learning-share-outs]]. LCPS's planned Gemini Notebook pilot takes a configuration route as well, grounding responses in source material and urging users to verify results against the originals [[lcps-gemini-notebook-ai-study-tool-pilot]].

## Why Effects Resist Attribution

Strong results tend to come from bundles, which makes the AI's own contribution hard to isolate. The World Bank researchers credit the Nigerian gains to the whole program of curriculum alignment, structured prompts, teacher support, peer learning, and AI, explicitly declining to attribute them to the chatbot [[world-bank-nigeria-ai-tutoring-six-week-program]]. Justin Reich's view that field trials are "a bundle of interventions" echoes Brookings' argument that RCTs assume a stable intervention that constantly updated models do not provide [[ai-learning-findings-august-2026]], and the Tennessee finding that AI helped only in combination with mastery repetition is another instance of the effect living in the pairing rather than the tool [[ai-mastery-learning-tennessee-fractions-study]].

Two other studies show how far the answer depends on design and context. Goblins, an Arizona math platform used by about 6,000 middle schoolers, associated at least two problems a week with a 0.11 SD gain, roughly three months of learning, but the analysis is correlational. Only about 5% of ed-tech users use tools as directed, half of Goblins' users reached the two-problem dose, and just 6% used it for 30 minutes or more a week. Its engagement drivers were a talking teacher avatar, multilingual support, and teacher agency over assignments [[goblins-ai-math-tutor-vs-umd-ai-assistant-opposite-results]]. The University of Maryland's randomized trial pointed the other way. Fifteen of 30 professors were assigned the assistant, and about 1,100 treated students earned grades 0.11 points lower than about 1,200 controls, with less engagement and fewer office-hours visits, a harm that fell hardest on first-generation students. Only 15% of students used the tool, mostly for answers, and UMD had tested it before a 40,000-student rollout. One possible mechanism is that the tool's presence signaled that AI use was encouraged, so students turned to outside tools, though that explanation is unconfirmed [[goblins-ai-math-tutor-vs-umd-ai-assistant-opposite-results]] [[umd-virtual-study-assistant-rct-low-uptake]]. Maryland's 2,379-student trial shows the same problem from another angle: students had ChatGPT and Gemini anyway, and instructor-readable chat logs may have deterred honest use, which left access slightly lowering grades before UMD paused rollout [[umd-virtual-study-assistant-rct-low-uptake]].

## The Human Layer: Accountability Over Content

[[ai-tutoring-equity-gap-human-accountability]] argues that content was rarely what held struggling students back. Stability, motivation, and an adult who notices when a student disappears mattered more, which is why affluent families keep buying human tutors in a private market projected to grow from about $92B to $155B by 2030, and why a well-designed product should route disengagement to a teacher rather than to an automated nudge. University of Phoenix researchers reach a parallel conclusion for working-adult online learners: persistence is an institutional outcome, sustained by instructor acknowledgement, belonging, and psychological safety, while GenAI mainly eases task initiation by removing "the panic of a blank page" [[trauma-informed-ai-persistence-model-online-learners]]. The Nigerian program framed AI as an "assistant teacher" under human supervision, with teachers helping students spot wrong answers [[world-bank-nigeria-ai-tutoring-six-week-program]]. Adult learners rank human feedback (46%) far above AI reminders (18%) as a driver of consistency [[preply-progress-report-ai-learning-progress-gap]]. [[ai-era-higher-education-human-connection-ubuntu]] turns the point into a design test drawn from Ubuntu: AI is good when it deepens participation, as when nursing students critique an AI diagnosis in teams, and harmful when it isolates. The division of labour in the tutoring trials above follows the same logic, in which AI widens support and humans keep the judgment about what a student needs.

## Educators as Designers, Not Just Users

A growing strand insists that teachers should shape AI rather than absorb it. Penn GSE's new Living Laboratory for Human Agency and AI is built as a network to co-design and test tools in real classrooms, on the premise that educators and students should be the designers of AI in education and build evidence of what their designs make possible [[penn-gse-living-lab-human-agency-ai]]. Bialik wants AI that elevates teacher ingenuity in planning, feedback, and coaching rather than adding "slop" [[irreplaceable-maya-bialik-ai-teaching-framework]]. Google frames its free educator series the same way, quoting a trust leader who sees AI assisting with resources and planning while teachers continue to interpret, inspire, and guide [[google-ai-educator-series-uk-ireland-650000-educators]]. Teacher-facing use is where adoption concentrates, with 63% of AP teachers using AI to prepare materials but only 15% for grading [[ai-spurs-college-board-redesign-ap-courses]], and weekly users in a Gallup survey estimating 5.9 hours saved per week, a self-reported outcome distinct from student learning [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]].

Faculty adoption shows what moves teachers to engage with AI. The Cottrell Scholars Collaborative's mixed-methods study of 90 faculty found that about 78% use AI in at least one context, and that adoption tracks pedagogical orientation more than institutional policy, access, or general beliefs about AI. Institution-wide initiatives were reported by 64% of respondents, against 8% for department-level efforts, and the authors describe a continuum of practice rather than two camps. They recommend discipline-specific conversations, low-stakes trials, and assignments that require students to validate AI output [[faculty-pedagogical-orientation-shapes-ai-adoption-cottrell-study]]. Stanford's instructors show what happens when faculty build the tools themselves. Across 23 instructors in six schools over two years, the share-outs describe curated AI tools, weekly student feedback, and a prerequisite analysis that Khalfan cut from months to weeks [[stanford-instructors-ai-tools-deeper-learning-share-outs]]. Institutions building their own platforms take a different design logic. SP Jain's in-house system pairs an AI Tutor for pre-class learning with an AI Mentor for research framing, moves class time toward case discussion and judgment, and argues for building capability rather than delivering content, since skills depreciate while learning capacity compounds. The note stresses that AI does not do the research for students [[sp-jain-ai-tutor-mentor-capability-over-content-delivery]].

## Widespread Use, Lagging Preparation

Use has outrun guidance at every level. About 70% of teens use AI for academic help, with 25% taking answers unchanged, yet 70% say no teacher has discussed safe use [[teens-ai-academic-support-common-sense-survey]]. Only 18% of U.S. K-12 teachers have formal AI training [[ai-in-classroom-fresno-teachers]], just 21% of faculty feel confident guiding classroom AI [[aix-weekly-ai-literacy-gate-higher-education]], and about a quarter of AP teachers work without any school AI policy [[ai-spurs-college-board-redesign-ap-courses]]. Higher education shows the same mismatch from several sides. A commentary by Petra Recter, which promotes a publisher's own AI literacy module, cites HEPI's finding that 95% of UK students use AI, DEC data showing that 21% find problems harder without it, and AAC&U/Elon figures that 68% of faculty say their institutions have not prepared them to use generative AI for teaching while 62% think graduates are unprepared. It also reports that 51% of new graduates are not actively building AI skills despite employer demand [[oup-ai-literacy-module-higher-education]]. Those vendor-sourced figures should be read as framing, but they match the pattern elsewhere. Maryland's trial showed what thin preparation costs, as faculty received only "light training" and the lead researcher concluded instructors need "much more extensive" support [[umd-virtual-study-assistant-rct-low-uptake]], while Penn's Living Lab starts from the diagnosis that educators experiment daily without clear evidence of what works [[penn-gse-living-lab-human-agency-ai]]. Vendors are moving into the gap at scale, with Google opening 15-to-45-minute Gemini modules to some 650,000 UK and Ireland educators after an AI Works pilot of just 150 staff across 14 schools [[google-ai-educator-series-uk-ireland-650000-educators]], and Kyndryl and Skillsoft offering free coursework to up to 25,000 U.S. high schoolers and educators [[kyndryl-skillsoft-ai-education-high-school]].

Teacher training is where that gap bites hardest. A one-off workshop gives staff a common language but not the situated judgment they need, and teachers come to value AI when it meets a real "I-need-it" problem. That judgment grows through practice with trusted colleagues and coaches who share their failures, so the case for support rests on measuring judgment rather than usage, and on supporting the coaches themselves [[one-off-ai-training-will-not-prepare-teachers]]. Mary Burns, interviewed about a 63-student study, reports that many middle schoolers stopped inquiring without asking follow-ups and could not tell strong prompts from weak ones, because inquiry and AI both demand domain knowledge. Her position is that there are no AI-literate students without AI-literate teachers, and that professional development must be sustained and focused on content. She also flags teacher attrition and a possible AI education bubble as risks [[ai-classroom-preserving-inquiry-mary-burns-iap]].

Student surveys add a misperception that shapes behaviour. In a survey of more than 1,500 Dartmouth students and 1,000 US college students, 11% reported using AI for problem sets while 49% believed their peers did, and 8% admitted copy-pasting while 50% assumed others did. Students also see the arrangements as unfair, with 70% calling take-home work unfair and 39% feeling pressured to use AI. Use is not only corrosive, since 61% use AI to explain concepts and 52% prefer back-and-forth with an AI to working alone, yet 84% nationally (92% at Dartmouth) believe AI can hurt writing. Most students learned on their own, with 79% saying they mostly taught themselves AI skills, and 74% of Dartmouth students (55% nationally) see AI skills as critical to their careers. Science students (70%) were far likelier than arts and humanities students (48%) to say AI deepens their understanding [[dartmouth-ai-survey-students-overestimate-peer-ai-use]].

## The Contested Meaning of "AI Literacy"

AI literacy is the standard prescription for that gap, but the term carries incompatible meanings. Kaspersky treats it as verification hygiene [[gen-z-ai-tutors-virtual-classrooms-kaspersky-survey]]. Cambridge's teachers stress knowing when to use AI, when not to, and how to evaluate its output [[cambridge-study-ai-english-teaching-teachers-drivers-seat]]. Malan wants default skepticism because models simulate understanding rather than possess it [[cs50-duck-ai-tutor-malan-interview]], and Penn's algorithm-auditing project teaches high schoolers to investigate AI bias [[penn-gse-living-lab-human-agency-ai]]. Kyndryl and Skillsoft pitch prompt engineering and credentials as workforce readiness for every student [[kyndryl-skillsoft-ai-education-high-school]], and Dayton cites employers who say "awareness of AI is no longer enough" while requiring every first-year to study AI alongside human dignity [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]]. The Oxford University Press module treats literacy as a six-part course covering fundamentals, integrity, pros and cons, critical thinking, prompting, and a course-specific activity, a curricular definition that is broader than tool skill but still sold as a product [[oup-ai-literacy-module-higher-education]]. [[ai-literacy-fallacies-kamperman-graff-higher-education]] targets the workforce framing directly, arguing that campus programs presume use, treat ethics as an afterthought, and rest on the myth that effective AI use is a simple skill that secures economic futures. Mary Burns adds that global AI-literacy frameworks still lack consensus [[ai-classroom-preserving-inquiry-mary-burns-iap]]. In the workplace the term often measures nothing at all. Pluralsight's finding that only 52% of organizations call most staff AI literate reflects an undefined self-perception [[pluralsight-2026-tech-skills-report-ai-literacy-gap-critique]], which is the verification gap Handshake targets with badges tied to published projects rather than self-reported skills [[handshake-ai-skills-studio-project-based-ai-badges]].

Media literacy is being taught alongside AI literacy through a different lens. Microsoft's MAIL toolkit, built with Cyberlite, teaches students to check information through lateral reading and judges trust by a claim's evidence and provenance rather than by whether AI produced the content. It reports that 53% of educators and 77% of students have had no AI training, has reached 10 countries in 11 languages since 2023, and cites South Korea, where 93% of educators reported improved understanding. The OECD plans to assess media and AI literacy in PISA from 2029, and the note links the toolkit to UNESCO's parallel "Think Critically, Click Wisely" campaign [[microsoft-ai-classroom-toolkit-media-ai-literacy]].

## Collaboration and Student Agency

Group work is where AI's effects on thinking become social. Pearson's DEC focus groups with 23 students from 21 institutions, where 88% use AI, find that shared AI inputs homogenize ideas and reduce debate, that AI is used to settle disputes, and that split-and-combine work hides unequal effort. Timing decides the direction: AI that challenges ideas a group has already developed can deepen its reasoning, while AI that generates the first ideas flattens them. Peer rules are hard to enforce under fatigue and deadlines, so the report urges institutions to define AI's role at each stage of group work and to assess individual understanding [[ai-collaborative-learning-students-dec-pearson]]. Agency also varies inside a single student's work. A six-tier taxonomy applied to 962 prompts from 105 first-year software engineering students at the University of Sydney found that 84.2% of prompts showed student contribution, and that agency tracks the project phase more than the student or the deadline. The highest tier (T5) made up 48.1% of Week 4 prompts against 9.5% later, the deadline effect did not hold up after clustering, and specifying constraints was rare. The note's limits are self-reported journals and a single course, and it recommends using the taxonomy formatively rather than for marks, since grading it would invite the same proxy-chasing [[human-agency-depth-taxonomy-first-year-students-ai-prompts]].

## Assessment and Integrity Under Pressure

AI agents have escalated cheating from pasted chatbot essays to impersonating a student across an entire course, a threat sharpest in asynchronous online programs that now reach over half of U.S. college students and lack oral or handwritten countermeasures [[ai-agents-cheating-online-courses]], and an estimated 9% of students in a 95,000-student study use AI to cheat [[aix-weekly-ai-literacy-gate-higher-education]]. UK universities are abandoning the early "prohibition and detection" model for pedagogy-led integrity, combining AI literacy, closed assessments, open assessments that design AI in, validation through declarations and vivas, and a culture of openness, though students report that vague guidance turns declarations into a source of anxiety [[keeping-the-learning-real-academic-integrity-ai-coherence]]. Detection's failure is also driving the College Board's 2027-28 split, in which AP Computer Science Principles adds an AI Create Project while AP Seminar blocks generative AI during scored writing because detectors produced false positives [[ai-spurs-college-board-redesign-ap-courses]]. Dayton's course-by-course honor code leaves faculty "stuck between a rock and a hard place" with no reliable way to discourage misuse [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]]. Kamperman and Graff draw the deeper consequence that once AI can plausibly produce the essay, educators must finally articulate why students write at all [[ai-literacy-fallacies-kamperman-graff-higher-education]]. Burns offers a constructive answer: assessment should ask what intellectual work the student actually performed [[ai-classroom-preserving-inquiry-mary-burns-iap]]. Kentz's proposal to grade the student's conversation with an AI tool points the same way, treating the dialogue as evidence of thinking rather than as something to police [[ai-tutors-optional-fail-conversation-as-assessment-kentz]].

## Governance: Infrastructure, Privacy, and Procurement

Shared infrastructure and contracts are becoming the governance layer beneath classroom tools. The CZI-backed Learning Commons gives more than 70 integrators a Knowledge Graph of all 50 states' standards and Evaluators that score AI content, though critics warn that its curators effectively define valid pedagogy for every tool built on top [[czi-learning-commons-open-ai-platform-k12]]. Districts report that rollout works best when it starts from the learning standard rather than the technology [[k12-districts-boost-literacy-with-ai-technology]]. Admin controls are beginning to set defaults too. Google keeps Gemini, Gemini Notebook, Gemini in Classroom and Search AI off for K-12 student accounts until an administrator enables them, and its Classroom rollout comes with real-time teacher insights [[google-guided-learning-search-classroom-school-controls]]. On privacy, NYC paused student-facing generative AI for about 600,000 students from 2-K through 8th grade and banned companion chatbots in all grades. The AFT/UFT/Microsoft standard makes protections enforceable only through each district's contract and excludes general productivity, search, and cloud tools, leading the article to argue that every purchase needs both learning evidence for the use and enforceable terms for the product [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]]. Build-versus-buy is unsettled too. Maryland's experience prompted Stephen Aguilar to argue that universities "can't compete against the market" on bespoke ed-tech [[umd-virtual-study-assistant-rct-low-uptake]], even as Dayton runs its own capped multi-model FlyerGPT platform [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]].

Local pilots are starting to write those terms into practice. LCPS plans a Gemini Notebook (NotebookLM) pilot in eight schools beginning in November, for grades 6 to 12, with source-grounded responses, a commitment that student data will not be used to train public models, and feedback-based evaluation [[lcps-gemini-notebook-ai-study-tool-pilot]].

## Equity Fault Lines

The equity case for tutoring at scale [[generative-ai-personalized-intelligent-tutoring-systems]] is contested from several directions. Rundell forecasts wealthier schools retreating from classroom AI while poorer schools lean in to cut costs [[ai-harming-young-minds-happiness-guardian]]. Early data fits, with 20% of teens in households under $30K doing most homework with AI versus 7% above $75K [[should-schools-use-ai-tools-student-learning]]. Kacharia notes that the income achievement gap widened about 40% over the decades personalized content scaled, recasting the digital divide as "who gets a human" [[ai-tutoring-equity-gap-human-accountability]]. The counterevidence comes from deliberately targeted programs, including the UK's planned trial with 450,000 disadvantaged students and Third Space Learning's Skye, which helped struggling and anxious students most [[current-evidence-ai-tutoring-impact-learners]]. The Nigerian researchers say the results could narrow the private-tutoring gap only if access is deliberately extended and schools have electricity, internet, computers, and trained teachers [[world-bank-nigeria-ai-tutoring-six-week-program]]. Dayton justifies its universal requirement on the same logic, that leaving AI literacy to chance would widen a gap [[university-of-dayton-ai-literacy-requirement-flyergpt]]. Maryland's harm, which fell hardest on first-generation students, shows the same risk inside a single campus [[goblins-ai-math-tutor-vs-umd-ai-assistant-opposite-results]]. [[ai-era-higher-education-human-connection-ubuntu]] adds that models trained mostly on non-African material can misread local practice, which leaves local educators as indispensable interpreters. Risk also concentrates among the youngest learners, since the UMN expert panel judged elementary students the most vulnerable to AI's effect on learning [[umn-expert-consensus-genai-paradox-k20-learning]].

## Measuring Learning, Not Adoption

The sources converge on a measurement problem: dashboards count seats and prompts but cannot show whether students reason or perform without the tool [[aix-weekly-ai-literacy-gate-higher-education]]. The core distinction is that a chatbot-produced answer is not evidence that the student acquired the skill [[ai-us-schools-learning-evidence-student-privacy]], which is the reason [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]] insists that performance with assistance is not learning. Usage measures carry their own trap. Measured by access or logins, the Khanmigo and "5% problem" cases look like adoption, yet only use during the hard steps, such as the 17% of mistake exercises, reflects the dosage that matters [[early-evidence-ai-tutoring-overdeck]]. The Eedi trial shows the same divergence under controlled conditions, with in-lesson correctness rising from 43% to 56–58% while the standardized Star Maths test showed no clear gain [[eedi-google-deepmind-ai-tutor-rct-full-findings]]. Usage can also mislead about which use helps. In a study of 6,060 professional learners on tax and accountancy courses, the type of question asked tracked exam performance more than how often the tutor was used. Just above the pass mark, conceptual questions made up just over half of prompts; below passing, they were about 20% and chat-style follow-ups nearly 60%. Heavy users, about twelve times as active, tended to score lower in five of six subjects, though the design is observational [[kaplan-tutorbot-study-ai-tutor-queries-reveal-learning]]. Workplace learning shows the same failure: Preply urges employers to stop treating time spent, completions, or streaks as progress [[preply-progress-report-ai-learning-progress-gap]], and Whitfield notes that 77% of tech executives have seen staff finish training yet struggle to apply it, proposing role-specific observable tasks across Kirkpatrick levels and staggered rollouts that leave an untrained team as a control [[pluralsight-2026-tech-skills-report-ai-literacy-gap-critique]].

The evidence base needs the same scrutiny. Patrick O'Neill's audit finds that 14 positive AI-learning meta-analyses fail basic standards, with mismatched studies, outcomes bucketed to suit the result, and some impossible statistics or hallucinated references. Those reviews were cited more than 3,000 times, and one Nature-journal paper was retracted. Stanford found only 20 of more than 800 studies had strong causal designs, none with K-12 students, and incentives to publish quickly push results upward. The remedy the audit offers is to define discrete questions before meta-analysis [[ai-education-research-meta-analyses-flawed-audit]]. Moderators matter even in the better-built syntheses. A meta-analysis of 34 studies on AI-assisted self-regulated learning reports an overall Hedges' g of 0.76, with 0.48 for cognitive, 0.73 for metacognitive and 0.93 for resource-management outcomes. Educational level, the AI's pedagogical role and the application type moderate the effect, while discipline, duration, control type, sample size and the SRL measure do not [[meta-analysis-ai-assisted-self-regulated-learning-zhang-li]]. Headline figures warrant the same caution. Google's claim that 74% of UK education roles could be enhanced by AI measures potential rather than realized gains [[google-ai-educator-series-uk-ireland-650000-educators]], and Nafukho proposes tracking access, learning, workload, well-being, integrity cases, and language performance, with whether students grow more connected as the real test [[ai-era-higher-education-human-connection-ubuntu]]. Cambridge's teachers reach the assessment conclusion directly, arguing that when polished work is effortless to generate, evaluation should shift to progress, reflection, and independent thinking [[cambridge-study-ai-english-teaching-teachers-drivers-seat]]. The NAIS report lists assessment redesign and formal school policy among the choices leaders must now ground in evidence rather than anecdote [[nais-2026-ai-in-independent-schools-report]].
