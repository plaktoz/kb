# Learning Science

Learning science is the empirical study of how humans acquire, retain, and transfer knowledge — a field whose most useful output is not theory but actionable design principles for instruction, self-directed study, and organizational training.

## The Working Memory Bottleneck

[[information-processing-theory-cognitive-stages]] establishes the foundational architecture: cognition moves through three stages — sensory memory (a 1-2 second perceptual filter), working memory (the active processing stage), and long-term memory (durable storage whose retrievability depends on encoding quality). The critical constraint is working memory's capacity: George Miller's original finding of roughly seven items has been refined downward in later research, and [[science-of-learning-cognitive-load-gradual-release]] builds an entire instructional design framework around it. Cognitive Load Theory holds that the working memory bottleneck is the primary failure point in instruction — if a lesson introduces too many novel elements simultaneously, the learner's working memory saturates before any encoding into long-term memory can occur. Chunking (grouping related items into meaningful units) and scaffolding (controlling the rate at which new complexity is introduced) are the two practical responses, and they explain why the same content can produce mastery or confusion depending purely on how it's sequenced.

## How the Brain Encodes and Consolidates

[[educational-neuroscience-attention-retrieval-practice]] grounds the consolidation picture in Stanislas Dehaene's Four Pillars of learning: attention (the brain cannot encode what it hasn't selected), active engagement (passive receipt produces weaker traces than effortful processing), error feedback (the anterior cingulate cortex lights up mistake-detection circuits that enhance subsequent encoding), and consolidation (memory is physically reconstructed during rest, not during active study). This last pillar has a non-obvious operational implication: the brain consolidates during breaks, not during continuous teaching, which means that instructional designs that eliminate unstructured recovery time in favor of maximum content delivery are working against the biology. [[smart-learning-framework]] converges on the same finding from a practitioner angle — daytime nappers showed 20% better recall at 6pm versus non-nappers, and the SMART framework treats sleep and rest as first-class learning variables, not luxuries.

## Retrieval Practice as the Core Technique

The most robust single finding across learning science is that actively retrieving information from memory strengthens its encoding more than any passive re-exposure. [[educational-neuroscience-attention-retrieval-practice]] cites the University of Edinburgh's result that active learning produces 30% higher participation and retention than lecture-based approaches. [[smart-learning-framework]] provides the mechanism: the anterior cingulate cortex fires on mistakes, creating a neurological alarm that makes subsequent focus stickier — which is why the 85/15 rule (succeed 85% of the time, fail 15%) identifies the optimal zone for retrieval practice difficulty. Too easy and the retrieval effort is insufficient to strengthen encoding; too hard and stress-based shutdown overrides the benefit. The practical implementation is the start-of-session recall habit: spending two minutes retrieving the prior session's material before introducing new content is an informal spaced repetition loop that flattens the forgetting curve without requiring dedicated review sessions.

## Instructional Design Principles

[[science-of-learning-cognitive-load-gradual-release]] synthesizes the pedagogical literature into the I Do / We Do / You Do gradual release model: start with teacher-modeled examples that show both product and process, transition to guided collaborative practice where the learner is active but supported, then release to independent practice only after demonstrated near-mastery. This sequence directly manages cognitive load by first externalizing the process (reducing the learner's cognitive burden to observation), then sharing it, then transferring it. The field's most credentialed success story is the Science of Reading — the phonics-grounded, evidence-based literacy curriculum that has displaced whole-language approaches in many districts after repeated empirical validation. [[educational-neuroscience-attention-retrieval-practice]] adds an environmental prerequisite that purely cognitive models miss: emotional safety is a hard gating condition for attention and retrieval. A learner whose cognitive resources are partially allocated to social threat-monitoring has reduced effective working memory available for the actual learning task.

## Meta-Learning and Self-Directed Practice Design

[[smart-learning-framework]] synthesizes findings from Peak, Deep Work, Make It Stick, Spark, and Why We Sleep into the SMART meta-learning framework — five practices that address the full learning cycle. State optimization uses pre-session moderate exercise (15-30 minutes) to flood the brain with dopamine, noradrenaline, and BDNF, creating peak neurochemical readiness before the study session begins. Reduction — compressing learned material into a single acronym, sentence, or visual — functions as a comprehension test: if you cannot compress it, you do not fully understand it. This aligns with [[information-processing-theory-cognitive-stages]]'s finding that durable retention depends on how well new material was connected to prior knowledge, not how many times it was reviewed. Eliminating attention residue (writing down lingering thoughts before studying) and the end-of-session teaching recap (explaining the session's content aloud as if to someone else) are the two highest-leverage procedural habits — together they ensure the session ends with both consolidation and a clean working memory for the next session.
