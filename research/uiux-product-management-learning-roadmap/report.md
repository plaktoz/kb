## UI/UX Fundamentals

### What Is User Experience Design? (Nielsen Norman Group)

- **Source**: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/definition-user-experience/
- **Summary**: UX covers all aspects of the end-user's interaction with a company, its services, and its products — far broader than just screens or interfaces. True UX requires cross-discipline collaboration (engineering, marketing, graphic design, interface design) and is not simply ticking feature boxes. UX, UI, and usability are related but distinct: UI is one component of UX; usability is one quality attribute within UX.
- **Relevance**: Establishes the foundational definition of UX and prevents common beginner misconceptions (e.g., equating UX with UI).

### UI Design Explained (Figma)

- **Source**: https://www.figma.com/resource-library/what-is-ui-design/
- **Summary**: UI design handles the visual detail and interactivity of digital products — buttons, menus, icons, navigation, and voice/gesture commands. UI cannot exist without UX; the two disciplines must collaborate. Key UI elements include input controls, informational components, navigational elements, and containers. Figma is the industry-standard collaborative tool supporting real-time design, shared component libraries, and browser-based workflows.
- **Relevance**: Clarifies the distinction between UI and UX, and introduces the primary tool (Figma) a beginner should prioritize.

### Breaking Into UX Design: Career Advice (Nielsen Norman Group)

- **Source**: https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-career-advice/
- **Summary**: Hiring managers rank soft skills above technical abilities when evaluating UX candidates. Diverse educational backgrounds are welcome; many practitioners began by gradually incorporating UX practices into existing roles rather than starting over. Key skills for designers include prototyping, visual design, and research; front-end coding is a plus. Confidence and ownership of career direction matter more than having the "perfect" background.
- **Relevance**: Directly addresses how to break into UX from another field, reassuring developers that their background is an asset, not a barrier.

### How to Become a UX Designer (CareerFoundry)

- **Source**: https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/ux-design/how-to-become-a-ux-designer/
- **Summary**: An 8-step path covers exploring the field, learning core principles (hierarchy, consistency, accessibility), studying key methods (wireframing, prototyping, user research), enrolling in a structured course, practicing constantly, mastering tools (Figma, Sketch, Hotjar), building a portfolio, and networking. Essential books include *The Design of Everyday Things* and *Don't Make Me Think*. With a structured program, a beginner can be job-ready in 5–10 months; average U.S. UX designer salary is ~$91,225/year.
- **Relevance**: Provides a concrete, actionable step-by-step roadmap with tools, timelines, and course recommendations.

## Product Management Fundamentals

### What Is Product Management? (ProductPlan)

- **Source**: https://www.productplan.com/learn/what-is-product-management/
- **Summary**: Product management is "the practice of strategically driving the development, market launch, and continual support and improvement of a company's products." PMs focus on strategy — not day-to-day development details. Core responsibilities: research, strategy, communication, cross-functional coordination, and iteration. Career path runs from Associate PM → Senior PM → Director → VP → Chief Product Officer. Key skills include strategic thinking, market research, customer empathy, and prioritization.
- **Relevance**: Provides the canonical definition of PM and the full scope of the role for someone evaluating it as a career.

### What Does a Product Manager Actually Do? (Mind the Product)

- **Source**: https://www.mindtheproduct.com/what-exactly-is-a-product-manager/
- **Summary**: A PM sits at the intersection of business, technology, and user experience — simultaneously optimizing for business goals, understanding technical feasibility, and being "the voice of the user inside the business." Unlike engineers or designers who go deep in one domain, PMs must be conversant across all three domains, rapidly switching between strategy and tactical execution. Daily responsibilities span vision-setting, roadmapping, execution with dev teams, and post-launch analysis.
- **Relevance**: Gives the most practical picture of what a PM does day-to-day — essential reading before pursuing the role.

### Top Product Manager Skills (Coursera)

- **Source**: https://www.coursera.org/articles/product-manager-skills
- **Summary**: Key PM skills fall into three categories: (1) Technical — technical writing, data analysis, UX/UI, agile/rapid prototyping; (2) Business — market research, resource efficiency, KPI tracking; (3) Interpersonal — cooperation, negotiation, critical thinking, and cross-functional collaboration. Learning paths include degree programs, online courses, entry-level roles, and community engagement through books and podcasts.
- **Relevance**: Provides a structured skills taxonomy and multiple learning paths, useful for gap analysis when coming from a developer background.

## Learning Resources & Roadmap

### How to Get Into Product Management (Lenny's Newsletter)

- **Source**: https://www.lennysnewsletter.com/p/how-to-get-into-product-management
- **Summary**: Lenny Rachitsky identifies four entry paths: internal company transfer (easiest), junior/APM programs at large companies, startup roles, or founding your own company. Seven core skills to develop: strategic thinking, execution, communication, influencing without authority, data-driven decisions, product taste, and preparedness. The journey typically takes 1–3 years. A self-assessment check matters first — the role suits those energized by solving problems and influencing without authority.
- **Relevance**: One of the most-cited PM career guides; especially relevant for the "developer going independent" path since internal transfers and startups both play to technical strengths.

### How to Become a Product Manager (CareerFoundry)

- **Source**: https://careerfoundry.com/en/blog/product-management/how-to-become-a-product-manager/
- **Summary**: Six-step path: build background knowledge → develop skills → get certified → build a portfolio → network → apply. Recommended courses by cost: CareerFoundry PM Program (3–6 months, $6,900), Product School Certification (8 weeks, $4,499), General Assembly (10 weeks, $3,950), BrainStation (5 Saturdays, $2,900). No degree required; career changers from engineering, UX, or sales backgrounds all transition well. Portfolio case studies hosted on a personal website are the key credentialing artifact.
- **Relevance**: Provides a realistic cost and timeline overview for formal PM education, useful for planning a structured transition.

### Core Product Manager Skills & Learning Roadmap (Product School)

- **Source**: https://www.productschool.com/blog/product-management/product-manager-skills/
- **Summary**: Outlines 18 PM skills across four categories: foundational (strategic thinking, market sensitivity, user-centric design), technical (requirements writing, data analysis, UX/UI, product lifecycle), advanced (agile/scrum, roadmapping, prioritization with RICE/MoSCoW, performance metrics), and leadership/soft skills (communication, empathy, stakeholder management). Suggested roadmap: start with free micro-certifications, build technical depth through courses and engineering collaboration, practice frameworks on real or simulated projects, then advance to full certification.
- **Relevance**: The most structured skills taxonomy available; the 4-category breakdown maps cleanly onto a developer's existing strengths and gaps.

## Developer-to-PM/Designer Transition

### Software Engineer to Product Manager (Product School)

- **Source**: https://www.productschool.com/blog/product-management/software-engineer-to-product-manager/
- **Summary**: Technical skills that transfer well include: technical fluency for collaborating with dev teams, familiarity with testing/iteration cycles, data-driven thinking, and understanding of system architecture. Gaps to fill: customer empathy and user research, business strategy and roadmap ownership, stakeholder communication and cross-functional leadership, and go-to-market awareness. Practical path: learn PM fundamentals (prioritization frameworks, discovery, metrics), build AI/prototyping skills, pursue structured certifications, practice articulating product decisions, and prepare for PM interviews. Core insight: engineers already think in systems and data — the shift requires adding customer obsession and business judgment.
- **Relevance**: Most directly relevant article for the user's situation; maps from developer strengths to PM gaps with a concrete action plan.

## Tools & Practical Portfolio-Building

### Product Manager Tools Guide (Product School)

- **Source**: https://www.productschool.com/blog/product-management/product-management-tools/
- **Summary**: PM tools by category — Roadmapping: Productboard, Airfocus, Chisel; User Research: Dovetail, Maze, UserTesting, Sprig; AI Prototyping: Lovable, Bolt, Cursor, v0, Replit; Project Management: Linear, Jira, Monday.com, Asana; Feature Management: LaunchDarkly, Statsig; Design/Prototyping: Figma, Balsamiq; Analytics: Amplitude, Mixpanel, Pendo, FullStory. For a developer transitioning to PM, the highest-leverage new tools to learn are Figma (design), Amplitude or Mixpanel (analytics), and Productboard or Linear (roadmapping/project management).
- **Relevance**: Gives a comprehensive toolset map for PMs; a developer already familiar with Jira/Linear has a head start and can prioritize learning design and analytics tools.
