Why Timing Matters: The Cost Curve of Security

Security fixes obey the same economics as any software defect: the earlier you find a problem, the cheaper it is to correct. A threat you identify during design can be eliminated by changing an approach before a single line of code is written. A threat found during code review requires refactoring existing work. A threat discovered after deployment may demand a hotfix, user notifications, incident response, and potentially regulatory involvement.

This is not a hypothetical gradient. Studies of defect costs in software consistently show an order-of-magnitude difference between design-time and post-deployment remediation. Security is no exception. When you defer threat modeling to a late-stage audit, you are accepting that fixing whatever is found will be expensive — sometimes prohibitively so.

The practical takeaway for a web developer is direct: threat model before you write the code, not after. Identify risks during the same conversation where you sketch the feature. Adjust the design. Then build with the adjusted approach from day one.

When threat modeling happens Relative cost to fix findings What you can change
Design & planning Low Architecture, data flows, authentication model, trust boundaries
Code review / pull request Medium Implementation details, library choices, specific code paths
After deployment to production High Mitigations only — the underlying design is usually locked in

The Microsoft SDL Five-Step Process

Microsoft's Security Development Lifecycle (SDL) is one of the most widely referenced frameworks for embedding security into routine software work. Its threat modeling component is not a one-time gate but a repeating practice that fits inside each development cycle. The five steps are iterative: you do not complete them once and move on; you revisit them as the application evolves.

Here is what each step means in practice, and where it naturally lands in a two-week sprint:

# SDL Step What you do When in a sprint
1 Define security requirements Translate business goals and compliance needs into concrete security properties the feature must satisfy (e.g., "all user data must be encrypted in transit; authentication must use the existing SSO provider"). Sprint planning — agree requirements before stories are estimated
2 Create an application diagram Draw a simple data-flow diagram (DFD) showing how data moves through the feature: external actors, processes, data stores, and trust boundaries. Even a whiteboard sketch counts. Design session at sprint start — usually day 1 or 2
3 Identify threats Walk through the diagram and ask what could go wrong at each element. Use a structured taxonomy such as STRIDE to avoid blind spots. After the diagram is ready — typically day 2 or 3, or during a dedicated 30-minute session
4 Mitigate threats For each identified threat, decide on a control: accept, transfer, mitigate, or eliminate. Log mitigations as acceptance criteria on the relevant stories. Immediately after identification; mitigations become part of the sprint backlog
5 Validate mitigations Confirm that the agreed mitigations were actually implemented. This is a check — not a new audit — that fits naturally into code review and the definition of done. Code review and sprint review — verify before stories are closed

Notice that steps 1 through 4 happen in the first few days of the sprint, before significant code is written. Step 5 is a lightweight verification woven into your existing review process. The whole cycle adds very little ceremony while dramatically reducing the cost of late discoveries.

Martin Fowler's Agile Three-Activity Loop

The thoughtworks community has developed a complementary framing that fits especially well with agile teams. Rather than a linear sequence, it describes threat modeling as a short, repeating loop of three activities — each of which is conversational rather than document-heavy:

Activity 1 — Explain & Explore

Someone — usually the developer or product owner proposing the feature — walks the rest of the group through what is being built. The goal is not a polished presentation but a shared mental model: what data does the feature handle, who interacts with it, and where does it connect to other systems? Five to ten minutes of explanation is usually sufficient.

Activity 2 — Identify Threats

The group, now with a shared picture of the system, asks adversarial questions. What could go wrong? Who might misuse this? Where is sensitive data exposed? This activity benefits from diverse participation: developers know implementation details that product owners do not, and product owners know business context that developers may underweight.

Activity 3 — Prioritise & Fix

Not every identified threat needs immediate action. The group assigns rough severity and likelihood, then decides what to fix in this sprint, what to defer, and what to accept as tolerable risk. Accepted risks get a note in the design document. Mitigations get added to the sprint backlog.

The loop is designed to run in 15 to 30 minutes when used consistently. The key insight from Fowler's framing is that the loop works because it is short enough to run frequently. A group that threat models weekly builds intuition. A group that threat models annually builds anxiety.

A 15-minute threat modeling session during sprint planning is worth more than a day-long security workshop held once a year.

The "Little and Often" Principle

Both the Microsoft SDL and the Fowler agile model converge on the same operational principle: threat modeling works best when it is embedded in ordinary development rhythms rather than treated as a separate event.

Annual or quarterly threat modeling workshops produce thick documents that are often stale before the ink is dry. Software changes continuously. New features introduce new attack surfaces. Refactors shift trust boundaries. Dependency updates introduce new libraries with their own vulnerabilities. A threat model from six months ago may not reflect the application you actually have today.

"Little and often" addresses this directly. Fifteen minutes at the start of every sprint keeps the threat model current with zero catch-up effort. The format can be light: a quick sketch on a whiteboard, a few bullet points in the sprint notes, a short discussion in the daily standup when a risky piece of work begins.

Think of it as continuous integration for security. Just as CI runs your tests on every commit so that broken code never accumulates, continuous threat modeling checks your security assumptions on every sprint so that blind spots never accumulate.

Security as a Team Sport

A widespread barrier to starting threat modeling is the belief that you need a dedicated security expert in the room. This belief is understandable — security feels like a specialist domain — but it is also one of the most common reasons teams never begin.

The reality is that effective threat modeling draws on knowledge that is already distributed across your team:

  • Developers bring deep knowledge of how the system is actually implemented — which libraries handle authentication, where database calls are made, how input is validated. This knowledge is indispensable for identifying real attack surfaces, not theoretical ones.
  • Product owners and business analysts bring business risk context — which data is genuinely sensitive, what a breach would mean for customers, which regulatory obligations apply. A developer focused on implementation may underestimate the impact of exposing a particular field; a product owner will not.
  • Testers and QA engineers bring a holistic usage perspective — they are practiced at thinking about edge cases, unexpected inputs, and flows that developers do not typically exercise. That adversarial mindset transfers directly to threat identification.

No single person on your team has all three of these perspectives. That is precisely why threat modeling produces better results as a brief collaborative exercise than as a solo expert review. The security specialist, when available, can add vocabulary and structure — but the knowledge required to identify what is risky about your specific feature is already in the room.

You do not need a security expert in the room to start. Fresh perspectives from developers and testers often surface risks that specialists overlook.
