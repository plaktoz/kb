LINDDUN, CVSS, and Choosing the Right Framework

Module 2 — Frameworks Lesson 7 of 12 ~12 min read

You have already seen STRIDE and PASTA. Those two frameworks cover a lot of ground, but they were designed with security threats in mind — confidentiality, integrity, availability, and the like. What happens when your primary concern is privacy? Or when you need a single number you can show to a product manager to explain why a vulnerability is urgent? That is where LINDDUN, CVSS, OCTAVE, and VAST come in.

This lesson introduces each framework at a level of detail you need as a junior developer, then gives you a practical decision guide so you can choose the right tool for the job.

1. LINDDUN — Privacy Threat Modeling

LINDDUN (pronounced "lin-dun") was developed at KU Leuven to fill the gap that STRIDE leaves open: it does not ask "can an attacker spoof a user?" but instead "can this system link a browsing session back to a real person?" Every letter in the acronym represents a category of privacy threat.

The seven LINDDUN threat categories

Letter Category What it asks
L Linking Can an attacker (or the system itself) combine separate data items to build a profile of a person?
I Identifying Can a person be singled out — identified from supposedly anonymous data?
N Non-repudiation Is it impossible for a user to deny an action they performed (e.g., a log entry proves they visited a page)?
D Detecting Can an observer detect that a person is using the system, even without seeing the content?
D Data disclosure Can personal data leak to parties who should not have access to it?
U Unawareness Are users unaware of how their data is collected, stored, or shared?
N Non-compliance Does the system violate applicable privacy laws or regulations (e.g., GDPR, CCPA)?

When to use LINDDUN

Use LINDDUN whenever your application handles personal data — names, email addresses, location, browsing history, health information, financial records, or any data that can be traced back to an individual. It is especially important if your users are in the EU (GDPR) or California (CCPA), where failing to identify privacy threats at design time can lead to significant regulatory penalties.

LINDDUN is applied to a Data Flow Diagram in the same way as STRIDE: you walk each element of the diagram and ask which of the seven threat categories could apply. The output is a list of privacy threats, each mapped to a specific data flow, store, or process.

If your web app collects any personal data — names, emails, location, browsing history — you should run LINDDUN alongside STRIDE. STRIDE finds security threats; LINDDUN finds privacy threats. They are complementary, not competing.

2. CVSS — Scoring Severity So Everyone Understands It

The Common Vulnerability Scoring System (CVSS) is not a threat-discovery framework. It is a scoring system — a standardised method for rating the severity of a vulnerability once you have already identified it. CVSS produces a number between 0 and 10 that maps onto four named severity bands.

CVSS Score Severity Band Typical response priority
0.0 None No action required
0.1 – 3.9 Low Address in regular maintenance cycle
4.0 – 6.9 Medium Plan remediation within weeks
7.0 – 8.9 High Remediate urgently — within days
9.0 – 10.0 Critical Immediate response required

What goes into a CVSS score?

A CVSS score is calculated from three metric groups. You do not need to memorise the formula at this stage, but understanding the inputs helps you estimate severity intuitively:

  • Base metrics — inherent properties of the vulnerability: attack vector (network vs. local), attack complexity (low vs. high), privileges required, user interaction needed, and the impact on confidentiality, integrity, and availability.
  • Temporal metrics — how mature the exploit is and whether a fix exists yet. A vulnerability with a public exploit and no patch scores higher than one with no known exploit.
  • Environmental metrics — how the vulnerability's impact changes in your specific deployment context (for example, if the affected data is particularly sensitive).

Why CVSS matters for junior developers

CVSS gives you a shared language with product managers, executives, and external auditors who are not security specialists. When you say "this SQL injection scores 9.8 Critical on CVSS," everyone in the room understands the urgency without needing a security background. It is also the scoring system used by the National Vulnerability Database (NVD), so you will encounter it whenever you look up a CVE entry for a library dependency.

You do not need to pick one framework and use it exclusively. Most mature teams combine STRIDE for design-time security threats and CVSS for scoring severity once threats are identified. CVSS is not a replacement for STRIDE or LINDDUN — it is used after threat discovery to communicate and prioritise the threats you found.

3. OCTAVE — Organisational Risk Assessment

OCTAVE (Operationally Critical Threat, Asset, and Vulnerability Evaluation) was developed at Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute. Where STRIDE and LINDDUN focus on technical threats to a specific system, OCTAVE takes a step back and asks: what assets matter most to the organisation, and what organisational risks threaten those assets?

OCTAVE is typically run in three phases:

  1. Build asset-based threat profiles — identify critical assets (customer data, intellectual property, financial systems) and describe what would happen if they were compromised.
  2. Identify infrastructure vulnerabilities — assess the technical weaknesses in the systems that host those assets.
  3. Develop security strategy and plans — prioritise risks in business terms and create a remediation roadmap.

OCTAVE suits larger organisations where risk decisions are made at a business level and involve multiple departments (legal, finance, IT, operations). It is less common in small web-development teams because it requires significant workshop time and organisational participation. For a junior developer on a product team, you are more likely to encounter OCTAVE results as input to your team's security requirements than to run the process yourself.

4. VAST — DevOps-Scale Threat Modeling

VAST (Visual, Agile, and Simple Threat modeling) was designed to solve a specific problem: STRIDE and PASTA are thorough, but they are slow. Running a full STRIDE session for every microservice in a continuous delivery pipeline is impractical when you are shipping multiple times a day.

VAST addresses this by producing two types of threat models:

  • Application threat models — similar to STRIDE diagrams, focused on process flows and developer concerns.
  • Operational threat models — infrastructure-focused, aimed at DevOps and security operations teams.

VAST integrates into CI/CD tooling so that threat models are updated automatically as code changes, rather than requiring a separate workshop. It is used primarily by organisations with mature DevSecOps practices and dedicated application security teams. As a junior developer you are unlikely to configure VAST yourself, but you may work in a pipeline that generates VAST outputs alongside your pull requests.

5. Choosing the Right Framework

No single framework is universally best. The right choice depends on four factors: what you are trying to protect, how mature your team is, who needs to use the outputs, and what resources you have available.

Framework Best for Primary audience Complexity
STRIDE General web application security threats; design-time analysis on DFDs Developers, architects Low–Medium
LINDDUN Privacy threats in apps that handle personal data; GDPR/CCPA compliance Developers, privacy officers Low–Medium
PASTA Business-risk-aligned analysis; linking threats to business impact Executives, security architects High
CVSS Scoring and prioritising already-identified vulnerabilities All stakeholders Low
OCTAVE Organisation-wide asset risk assessment Management, IT leadership High
VAST Scalable, automated threat modeling in CI/CD pipelines DevOps teams Medium–High

A practical decision guide

Work through these three questions to narrow your choice:

  1. What are you trying to protect?
    Functional security (authentication, authorization, data integrity) → start with STRIDE. Personal data and privacy rights → add LINDDUN. Both usually apply to real web applications.
  2. How experienced is your team?
    Beginners or first threat model → STRIDE is well documented, tool-supported, and widely understood. Experienced team comfortable with risk language → consider PASTA for richer business alignment.
  3. Who needs to consume the output?
    Developers fixing code → STRIDE produces actionable, technical mitigations. Executives making investment decisions → PASTA or OCTAVE frame threats in business-risk terms. Privacy and legal teams → LINDDUN maps directly to regulatory categories. Everyone needs severity communicated → layer CVSS scores on top of whatever framework you used.

The OWASP Threat Modeling Cheat Sheet also notes that STRIDE pairs well with MITRE ATT&CK: once you have identified a threat category with STRIDE, you can map it to known attack techniques in the ATT&CK matrix to make your mitigations more specific.

Combining frameworks in practice

In practice, the question is rarely "STRIDE or LINDDUN" — it is "STRIDE and LINDDUN, scored with CVSS." A typical lightweight process for a small product team looks like this:

  1. Draw a Data Flow Diagram of the feature you are designing.
  2. Apply STRIDE to find security threats on each DFD element.
  3. Apply LINDDUN if any personal data flows through the diagram.
  4. Assign a CVSS score to each threat to communicate severity.
  5. Prioritise by likelihood × impact and hand off mitigations to the sprint backlog.
