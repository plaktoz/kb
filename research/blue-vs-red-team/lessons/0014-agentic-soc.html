The Alert Triage Problem at Scale

A modern enterprise SIEM can generate tens of thousands of alerts per day. Each alert is a candidate incident: it may indicate a breach, a misconfiguration, normal-but-noisy behavior, or a false positive. The only way to know is to investigate — query the relevant logs, correlate events across systems, check threat intelligence, and make a judgment call.

In the traditional SOC model, every step of that investigation requires a human analyst. An alert fires; the analyst reads it; the analyst manually opens the SIEM, queries the relevant data, cross-references context from a separate threat intel feed, writes up a summary, and decides what to do next. Then the next alert arrives. Then another.

This model has two structural problems. First, alert volume grows faster than analyst headcount. Hiring cannot keep pace with the expanding attack surface driven by cloud adoption, remote work, and an increasingly sophisticated adversary ecosystem. Second, repetitive triage work drives burnout and inconsistency. Analysts doing the same investigative steps hundreds of times a day tire, make mistakes, and leave — taking institutional knowledge with them.

The result is well-documented: alert queues back up, analysts start triaging by feel rather than by process, and lower-fidelity alerts are ignored even when they contain genuine signals. The bottleneck is not detection — it is the human time required to turn a detected event into an actionable verdict.

The Agentic SOC Model and the Human-on-the-Loop Principle

Elastic's response to this structural problem is the agentic SOC: an architecture where AI agents perform the investigation and correlation work autonomously, then surface a complete, contextualized response plan for analyst review. The analyst is no longer in the critical path of investigation; they are elevated to a decision-review role.

Elastic captures this shift in a single phrase:

“Human on the loop rather than human in the loop.”

In the agentic model, AI agents do the following autonomously for each alert:

  1. Investigate — query the relevant logs, endpoint telemetry, and threat intelligence to build a complete picture of what happened
  2. Correlate — link the alert to related events, identify affected assets, and map the activity to known attack patterns
  3. Draft a response plan — propose specific remediation actions (isolate a host, block an IP, reset a credential) with the reasoning attached to each step

The analyst's role shifts from doing the investigative work to reviewing, judging, and approving the AI agent's proposed plan. Humans retain full decision authority over every proposed action. The AI accelerates; the human judges.

Key Concept Human-on-the-loop is a governance model, not a technology choice. The AI agent handles data assembly and hypothesis generation — the work that scales poorly with human attention. The analyst handles judgment, accountability, and approval — the work that requires human authority. Neither role is eliminated; they are separated at the point where automation genuinely helps.

Human in the Loop vs Human on the Loop

Dimension Human in the Loop Human on the Loop
Who investigates? Analyst manually queries logs and correlates events AI agent investigates and correlates autonomously
Who drafts the response? Analyst writes the response plan from scratch AI agent drafts a structured plan with reasoning
Analyst’s primary role Perform the investigation work step by step Review, judge, and approve the AI’s proposed plan
Throughput bottleneck Human attention and available analyst hours Quality of AI reasoning and agent design
Scales with alert volume? No — headcount must grow proportionally Yes — compute capacity scales; analyst time does not
Risk profile Fatigue, inconsistency, alert queue backlog AI overconfidence or hallucinated reasoning
Human accountability Analyst is accountable for every step taken Analyst is accountable for every action approved
Watch Out The human-on-the-loop model does not eliminate analyst responsibility — it concentrates it. When an analyst approves an AI-proposed response, they own that decision. Rubber-stamping AI recommendations without genuine review defeats the purpose of the governance model and can introduce a new failure mode: analyst accountability for actions they did not truly evaluate. The shift requires analysts to develop strong skills in critically reading AI-generated reasoning, not just executing it.

Model-Agnostic Architecture and Enterprise Adoption

Elastic's agentic SOC is deliberately model-agnostic: the AI investigation layer is not tied to a single LLM provider. The platform supports integration with OpenAI, Anthropic (Claude), Google (Gemini), and on-premises models for organizations with data sovereignty requirements or air-gapped environments.

This matters for enterprise adoption for three reasons:

  • Procurement flexibility — security teams can choose their AI provider based on performance benchmarks, cost, existing enterprise contracts, or compliance requirements without being locked into a single vendor’s model ecosystem
  • Data sovereignty — organizations subject to strict regulatory regimes (financial services, defense, healthcare) can run on-premises models and ensure that security telemetry never leaves their environment
  • Auditability — full decision transparency is maintained regardless of which model is used; analysts can inspect the reasoning behind every AI recommendation, which is a prerequisite for regulated industries where automated decisions must be explainable

Elastic reinforces this positioning with its pricing model. Rather than charging per-endpoint fees — which penalize organizations for having comprehensive coverage — the platform prices on compute and storage consumption. Historical data can be queried without “rehydration” penalties that are common in competing platforms and often cause teams to archive data they cannot practically retrieve when an investigation demands it.

The tagline “built to secure, not to tax” reflects this design intent: every pricing and architecture decision is aligned with the goal of maximizing security coverage rather than maximizing revenue from the security team’s constraints.

The 1,300+ Detection Rules as an Agent Knowledge Base

Elastic ships more than 1,300 open, customizable detection rules published publicly on GitHub. For a human analyst, these rules are the detection layer — each rule defines a behavioral pattern that, when matched against incoming telemetry, raises an alert. For an AI agent, they represent something additional: a structured, community-curated knowledge base of known adversary behaviors.

The openness of the rule set has direct implications for agentic architectures. When an alert fires, the agent can inspect the detection rule that triggered it — the specific conditions, the MITRE ATT&CK technique mapping, the expected data sources — and use that context to scope its investigation. Rather than approaching an alert with no prior hypothesis, the agent begins with a structured description of what was detected and what behavior it represents.

This is qualitatively different from a black-box detection system. Agents working with transparent, readable rules can reason about why a rule fired, what adjacent techniques might be in play, and what evidence would confirm or refute the initial hypothesis. The rule corpus becomes part of the agent’s working context, not just the trigger for an alert.

To ensure high-quality signal retrieval from the underlying data, Elastic uses a hybrid BM25 + vector search architecture. BM25 provides traditional keyword-based relevance scoring — effective for matching known indicators like IP addresses, file hashes, and process names. Vector search adds semantic similarity, allowing the agent to surface related events that do not share exact keywords but are behaviorally relevant. Together, they reduce the noise that exhausts SOC teams and causes genuine signals to be buried in false positives.

Unified ingestion is the foundation that makes this possible. Elastic consolidates four categories of telemetry under a single pipeline: logs (system, application, security events), infrastructure metrics (CPU, memory, network throughput, process activity), application traces (distributed tracing data revealing how requests move through services), and security events (endpoint telemetry, authentication records, DNS queries, network flow data). An AI agent investigating a lateral movement alert can query endpoint process trees, authentication logs, and network flow data in a single pass — without bridging multiple siloed systems.

What This Means for Building Self-Serve Security Tooling

The Elastic agentic SOC is not just a product — it is an architecture pattern applicable to any self-serve security tool built on top of LLMs. The lessons from its design translate directly into how you construct an alert triage agent, a detection engineering assistant, or an incident response draft generator.

Four principles are worth extracting explicitly:

  1. Scope the agent’s authority precisely. Elastic’s agents investigate and draft; they do not execute. Your agent design should have equally explicit boundaries. Write them into the system prompt. The agent’s scope is part of its specification, not a runtime guardrail.
  2. Structure the output for human review, not for autonomous action. If the output is a structured plan with reasoning attached, a human reviewer can evaluate it in seconds. If the output is a free-text summary, the reviewer must re-interpret and re-validate it. Structured output formats — JSON with verdict, confidence, reasoning, and next action — are a design choice that enables the human-on-the-loop model to function.
  3. Feed the agent context, not just the alert. An alert without the detection rule, relevant log excerpts, affected system context, and severity level gives the agent almost no footing for investigation. Richer input context produces richer and more reliable output. The input format is as important as the model selection.
  4. Prefer model-agnostic designs. Coupling your triage agent to a single model provider creates a dependency that complicates procurement, data residency compliance, and model upgrades. Abstract the model layer so that swapping providers requires changing one configuration value, not rewriting the agent.

The agentic SOC pattern also demonstrates that native workflow integration is more valuable than pipeline integration. When the agent’s proposed response can be approved and executed from the same interface where the investigation summary is displayed — without switching to a separate SOAR tool — analyst friction drops and response times improve. If you are building security tooling, designing for integration rather than handoff is a structural advantage.

Practice Exercise

Write a system prompt for a Claude-powered alert triage agent. Your system prompt should specify:

  1. The agent’s role and scope — what it is and is not allowed to do
  2. The input format — what structured data it receives per alert (alert type, severity level, relevant log excerpt, affected system, timestamp)
  3. The output format — the structured JSON or markdown template it should always return, including: verdict (benign/suspicious/malicious), confidence level, reasoning in 2–3 sentences, recommended next action, and escalation flag
  4. One hard constraint — something the agent must never do autonomously

Write the complete system prompt as if you were going to paste it into the Claude API today.
