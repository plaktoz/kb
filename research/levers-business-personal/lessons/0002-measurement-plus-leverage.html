The Wealth Formula — Measurement + Leverage

Module 1 — The Leverage Map Lesson 2 of 12 ~10 min
Primary Source

Paul Graham, "How to Make Wealth" — paulgraham.com/wealth.html

Core Insight Wealth creation requires both measurement and leverage — not one or the other. Measurement means your individual contribution is trackable. Leverage means your decisions have outsized impact. Miss either ingredient and income is capped: the sweatshop worker has measurement without leverage; a bureaucrat may have neither.

The Two-Ingredient Formula

Paul Graham opens his wealth essay with a deceptively simple claim: wealth is not money, it is stuff people want. But creating wealth at scale — rather than just trading time for money — depends on satisfying two conditions simultaneously.

Measurement means that the connection between what you produce and what you earn is clear and direct. When your contribution is visible, you can negotiate to be paid proportionally to what you actually create. A sweatshop worker has measurement: the factory can count their output. But that measurement alone does not make them wealthy, because the second ingredient is missing.

Leverage means that your decisions or techniques affect outcomes at a scale far beyond the hours you personally put in. A CEO of a large company has both: their strategy is identifiable as theirs, and a single decision can move thousands of people and millions of dollars. Graham's formulation: "Measurement comes from working with a small group, and leverage from developing new techniques."

"If you have a choice of working on something that will have a big effect and something that won't, choose the thing with the big effect." — Paul Graham

Why Small Groups Unlock Measurement

In a large corporation, individual contributions blur into collective output. When five thousand people ship a product, it is genuinely difficult to say how much of the result belongs to any one engineer or designer. This is not a failure of the accounting system — it is a structural property of large groups.

A startup's small size is what makes measurement possible. When five people ship a product that reaches a million users, each person's contribution is legible to everyone in the room. This is what lets a startup worker say, with credibility, "pay me proportionally to what I produce" — the causal chain is short enough to trace.

Small group size is therefore not just a social preference; it is an enabling condition for one half of the wealth formula. This is why the startup, despite its chaotic early life, is a remarkably efficient mechanism for turning individual effort into individual reward.

Common Misconception Many people assume leverage is about working harder or putting in more hours. It is not. Graham's point is that leverage comes from technique — a new way of doing things whose value scales independent of your personal time. More hours in a low-leverage role is still low-leverage work. The multiplier must come from the nature of the work, not its duration.

Technology as the Most Powerful Modern Lever

Of all the forms leverage can take, Graham argues technology is the most powerful because of its zero marginal cost of replication. When you cut someone's hair, you serve exactly one customer. When you write software that solves a problem, every person who downloads and uses that software benefits — and the cost of serving the millionth user is essentially the same as serving the first.

This is what Graham means when he says technology is "technique" — it is a new method or approach whose value is not consumed by being used. A better cloth-weaving process does not wear out when the Florentines apply it; a more efficient ship design does not degrade when the Dutch copy it to their entire fleet.

Historical examples Graham cites:

  • Florentines (1200s): New cloth-weaving techniques created regional commercial dominance. The technique was the lever; it scaled across the entire production system.
  • Dutch (1600s): Shipbuilding innovations unlocked Far East trade routes. Better ship designs compounded into geopolitical and economic advantage.
  • McDonald's and Walmart: These companies did not grow by selling more individual transactions — they grew by reproducing a scalable system. The franchise or supply-chain blueprint, once proven, could be stamped out across thousands of locations.

In each case, the leverage mechanism is the same: a technique or system, once developed, scales beyond the capacity of any individual acting alone.

Barriers to Entry: Why Hard Problems Pay More

Graham's rule is blunt: always take the harder problem. This is not masochism — it is a strategic calculation. Hard technology creates "barriers to entry" in the form of difficult terrain that competitors cannot easily cross.

If you solve a problem that any reasonably competent engineer could solve in a weekend, your leverage disappears the moment someone else solves it too. The value of your technique collapses to near zero because supply has equalized demand. But if you spend months or years developing a genuinely difficult solution — a new algorithm, a new methodology, a new approach to an unsolved problem — the difficulty itself becomes a moat.

This explains a counterintuitive phenomenon: the most valuable work is often the work that feels most uncomfortable in the short run. Choosing the easier problem feels safer; choosing the harder problem builds the barriers that protect long-term leverage.

Applying the Formula Beyond Software

Graham wrote primarily about startups and software, but the measurement + leverage formula applies across domains. Consider two analogous examples:

Scenario Measurement Leverage Outcome
Solo consultant, no methodology High — clients see your work Low — each engagement starts from scratch Income capped by available hours
Solo consultant + proprietary methodology or tool High — clients see your work Higher — methodology delivers results at scale Significantly higher rates than generalists
5-person startup shipping a product High — contribution is visible to the whole team High — product reaches potentially millions Both conditions met; wealth creation possible
Employee in a 500-person company Low — contribution blurs into team output Variable — depends on role Hard to negotiate proportional pay

For a consultant or knowledge worker, developing a proprietary methodology or tool takes roughly six to eighteen months of focused work. That is the investment required to move from selling time (low leverage) to selling a system or approach (higher leverage). The methodology is the technique — Graham's lever in professional services form.

Skill Exercise

Think of one deliverable from your current consulting work or job — a report, a product, a service you deliver regularly. Rate it on two axes:

  1. Measurement: Is your individual contribution to this deliverable clearly visible and attributable to you? (1 = invisible / blurs into team; 5 = fully attributable to you)
  2. Leverage: Does this work scale beyond your direct involvement — will it reach more people or compound over time without you being present for each instance? (1 = one person at a time; 5 = scales to many)

Now write a single sentence describing one concrete action to improve whichever axis scored lower. Example: "I will document my analysis process into a repeatable template so I can apply it to ten clients instead of one."

Dig deeper: Ask a tutor or thinking partner: "In my current role, what is the single change that would move me from measurement-only to measurement plus leverage?" Prompt them to push back if your answer is vague — a good answer names a specific technique or system, not just "work harder."
