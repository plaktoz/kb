What Is Leverage? Naval's Three Classes

Module 1 — The Leverage Map Lesson 1 of 12 ~12 min
Primary Source

Naval Ravikant, The Almanack of Naval Ravikant — "Find a Position of Leverage"

Why Leverage Is the Right Frame

Most people think the primary divide in modern economies is between rich and poor, or between white-collar and blue-collar work. Naval Ravikant argues that framing is outdated. The division that actually matters — the one that explains why two people working equally hard end up with radically different outcomes — is whether or not they are operating with leverage.

"Forget rich versus poor, white-collar versus blue. It's now leveraged versus un-leveraged."
— Naval Ravikant

Leverage means your decisions, your effort, and your judgment multiply. Without it, you trade hours for dollars in a one-to-one exchange. With it, a single decision — a piece of code shipped, a book published, a dollar invested — can ripple out across thousands or millions of people, compounding your output without requiring more of your time.

This lesson gives you the taxonomy: Naval's three classes of leverage, how they differ in access and power, and how they can stack.

The Three Classes of Leverage

1. Labor Leverage — Other People Working for You

Labor leverage is the oldest form. You hire or manage people; their collective output exceeds what you could produce alone. A general contractor is the canonical example: she coordinates five subcontractors on a renovation and delivers a project no single person could complete.

Naval calls this leverage "incredibly messy." Managing people requires constant attention — hiring, motivating, resolving conflict, replacing those who leave. It scales, but slowly and noisily. Crucially, it requires permission: someone has to agree to work for you.

2. Capital Leverage — Money Multiplying Every Decision

Capital leverage is historically the most dominant form for building large fortunes. Instead of managing people, you deploy money: a dollar invested in the right opportunity returns multiples without requiring proportional time. A real estate fund manager deploying capital across twenty developers exemplifies this — each additional dollar she invests scales her output far faster than adding another employee would.

Naval notes: "If you get good at managing capital, you can manage more and more capital much more easily." The catch is the same as labor — it requires permission. Someone must give you capital to manage, or you must already have it.

3. Code and Media — Products With No Marginal Cost

The newest and, for most individuals, most accessible form of leverage is code and media. A piece of software, a book, a podcast, a video — once created, it can reach one person or one billion at essentially the same cost. There is no marginal cost of replication.

Naval's examples are instructive in scale:

  • Joe Rogan records one podcast episode and reaches 15 million listeners.
  • PewDiePie uploads one video and it plays for millions simultaneously.
  • Jeff Bezos and Mark Zuckerberg built software platforms — Amazon and Facebook — that now serve billions of users with no proportional increase in effort per user.

The defining characteristic of this class is that it is permissionless. You do not need to convince anyone to give you labor to manage or capital to deploy. You write the code, record the episode, publish the post — and the leverage is available immediately. Naval says: "Code is probably the most powerful form of permissionless leverage."

Key Insight Code and media are permissionless leverage — you deploy them without anyone's approval, and each copy costs nothing to distribute. This is the fundamental shift that separates the current era from all previous eras of wealth-building.

Stacking Leverage: The Real Estate Progression

Naval's real estate example illustrates how these classes can compound when you climb the ladder deliberately. Each step multiplies income and increases your ownership of the upside:

Role Leverage Type What Scales
Laborer on site None Only your own hours
General contractor Labor 5 subcontractors' output
Property developer Capital Borrowed money across projects
Real estate fund manager Capital at scale 20+ developers' capital deployed
Tech platform (Zillow / Redfin) Code compounding capital Every transaction on the platform, for free

The laborer exchanges hours for wages — no leverage. The contractor layers in labor leverage. The developer adds capital leverage on top. The fund manager scales that capital to an entire market. The tech platform at the top adds code leverage that compounds across every interaction, indefinitely, at zero marginal cost per transaction.

Each transition multiplies income not by working harder but by changing which type of leverage you operate with. The goal is not to climb to the top in one leap; it is to identify where you are on the ladder now and what the next step up looks like.

Common Misconception Hiring more people is not always the most powerful next move. Labor leverage is the oldest and weakest of the three classes. If you have access to capital or the skills to build code or media, scaling through those channels will typically compound faster and with less management overhead than adding headcount.

Summary

Naval's taxonomy gives you three levers, ordered roughly by age and accessibility:

  1. Labor — oldest, always requires permission, messy to manage
  2. Capital — historically dominant, powerful but still requires permission
  3. Code / Media — newest, permissionless, zero marginal cost to replicate

In practice, the highest leverage positions stack all three. But you do not need all three to begin. The most important shift is moving from un-leveraged work — trading hours one-for-one — to any form of leveraged work, however modest. From there, the question is always: what would the next rung look like?

Check Your Understanding

Which type of leverage does Naval identify as "permissionless" — something you can deploy without needing anyone else's approval or resources?
Skill Exercise — ~5 min

Map your current career or work situation onto Naval's leverage ladder. Think honestly about the work you spend most of your time on right now.

Write your answers to two questions:

  1. What type of leverage are you currently operating with? (labor, capital, code/media, or none — trading hours directly for income)
  2. What would the next rung up look like for you specifically? What would you need to build, learn, or change to access that next level of leverage?
Tutor Prompt: After completing the exercise, ask yourself whether your answer surprised you. Many people discover they have more labor leverage available than they use, or that a modest side project already represents the beginning of code leverage. The goal is clarity, not immediate transformation — diagnosis first, then action.
