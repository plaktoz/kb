Theory of Constraints — Find and Fix the One Bottleneck

Module 2 of 4 Lesson 4 of 12 ~12 min
Primary Source

Lean Production — Theory of Constraints overview.
https://www.leanproduction.com/theory-of-constraints/

Every system — a factory floor, a consulting firm, a personal workflow — has a single slowest step. That step, the constraint (or bottleneck), determines the maximum output of the entire system. No matter how fast every other step runs, total throughput cannot exceed what the constraint allows.

The Theory of Constraints (TOC), developed by Eliyahu Goldratt, turns this observation into an actionable management method. Rather than spreading improvement effort across the whole system, TOC concentrates all energy on the single weakest link — because that is the only place where improvement actually increases total output.

Key Insight Improving any step that is not the constraint produces zero gain in total throughput. All lasting profitability leverage lives at the bottleneck — and only there.

What Is a Constraint?

A constraint is the single activity that limits the total output of a system — the "weakest link in the chain." You can identify it by watching for three signals:

  • Work-in-progress (WIP) buildup — inventory or tasks pile up just before one step.
  • Frequent expediting — the team routinely rushes work to keep that step fed.
  • Long cycle times — one station consistently has the highest average processing time.

Every process has exactly one binding constraint at any given moment. Fixing it inevitably shifts the constraint elsewhere, which is why TOC prescribes a repeating cycle rather than a one-time project.

The 5 Focusing Steps

Goldratt's method is structured as five sequential steps. The order matters — skipping ahead wastes resources and may accidentally hide the true constraint.

Step 1 — Identify

Locate the constraint. Map the process end-to-end and look for WIP accumulation, expediting requests, and long cycle times. In a factory this might be a single machine; in a service firm it might be a senior person whose sign-off is required on everything.

Step 2 — Exploit

Maximize throughput through the constraint using only resources you already have. Buffer inventory or work directly before it so it never sits idle. Assign your best operators there. Eliminate any quality defects that consume its capacity — a defect processed at the constraint is double waste.

Step 3 — Subordinate

Align every other process in the system to support the constraint's needs. Non-bottleneck stations should produce at the pace the constraint can absorb, not at their own maximum speed. This feels counterintuitive — running other stations below capacity — but overproducing upstream only creates WIP that chokes the bottleneck further.

Step 4 — Elevate

If the constraint still limits throughput after you have fully exploited it, make larger investments: new equipment, additional headcount, outsourcing, redesigned workflows. Elevation costs money, which is why exploit and subordinate come first — many constraints can be broken with zero capital by simply removing waste and idle time.

Step 5 — Repeat

Once the original constraint is broken, a new bottleneck will emerge elsewhere. Return to Step 1 immediately. Inertia — the tendency to declare victory and stop improving — is itself a constraint on continuous improvement.

Step Action Cost
1. Identify Find the weakest link via WIP, cycle time, expediting Time only
2. Exploit Maximize output with existing resources; buffer before it Minimal
3. Subordinate Pace all other steps to the constraint; accept spare capacity Minimal
4. Elevate Invest in capacity if constraint persists after steps 2–3 Capital required
5. Repeat Return to Step 1; a new constraint will have emerged Ongoing

Three Types of Constraints

Equipment Constraint

A machine or tool with the longest cycle time limits total output. The response is to focus maintenance schedules, overtime, and quality checks on that machine first. A laser cutter that is the slowest station in a factory will increase total factory output if fed 24/7, staffed by the best operators, and protected by buffer stock upstream.

Policy Constraint

Outdated rules — minimum lot sizes, batch-release procedures, bonus structures that reward local efficiency — can throttle throughput more than any machine. A consulting firm where every client deliverable requires a partner's approval creates a policy bottleneck: the partner becomes the constraint regardless of how fast the rest of the team works. Delegating approvals to senior managers unlocks throughput without any capital investment.

Market Constraint

When internal capacity exceeds external demand, the market itself becomes the limiting factor. No internal improvement will raise output; the constraint now lives outside the firm and must be addressed through sales, marketing, or product differentiation.

Common Misconception Most improvement programs try to increase efficiency everywhere at once — cutting costs broadly, raising utilization at every station. TOC holds that this is largely wasted effort. Gains at non-constraint steps do not increase total throughput; they only build up WIP ahead of the bottleneck. The correct goal is to increase throughput at the single constraint, not to maximize local efficiency across the board.

TOC in Practice: Examples

The 5 focusing steps apply across very different contexts:

  • Factory: A laser cutter is the slowest machine in a production line. Feeding it 24/7, buffering raw stock immediately before it, and assigning the most experienced operators to it increases total factory output — even though every other machine was already running below capacity.
  • Consulting firm: A partner approval step gates all project deliverables. Delegating approvals to senior managers for routine engagements breaks the bottleneck and unlocks throughput for the entire firm — no new hires required.
  • Personal workflow: Your bottleneck is often energy or focus, not time. Fixing sleep hygiene and scheduling deep work blocks during peak cognitive hours unlocks everything downstream — more time spent on low-energy tasks does not compensate for impaired high-focus capacity.

Time to see results is short. In most operational contexts, identifying and exploiting the first constraint takes one to four weeks, and output improvements become visible within days of making the change.

Practice Exercise

List the 3–5 main activities in a project or system you currently manage (a work process, a side project, a daily routine). For each activity, ask: what happens to total output if this activity becomes 2× faster?

The activity where doubling speed most improves total output is your constraint. Write what you would do tomorrow — with no new budget — to exploit it.

Take it further: After identifying your constraint, look for a policy — a rule, habit, or approval step — that is artificially slowing it down. Policy constraints are often invisible and can be broken faster, and at lower cost, than equipment constraints.
