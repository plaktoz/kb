## Module 1 — The Leverage Map
*Foundations first: before applying any specific lever, you need the vocabulary and the diagnostic hierarchy. These three lessons give you the map of the entire territory.*

### Lesson 1: What Is Leverage? Naval's Three Classes
**File:** `lessons/0001-what-is-leverage.html`
**Key concepts:** labor leverage · capital leverage · code leverage · media leverage · permissionless leverage
**Source:** https://www.navalmanack.com/almanack-of-naval-ravikant/find-a-position-of-leverage — Naval's three-class taxonomy: labor (oldest, weakest), capital (powerful but requires capital base), code/media (newest, permissionless, no marginal cost to replicate)
**Skill:** Map your current career/work situation onto Naval's leverage ladder. Write down: what type of leverage are you currently using, and what would the next step up look like?
**Reference doc:** `reference/leverage-types.html`

### Lesson 2: The Wealth Formula — Measurement + Leverage
**File:** `lessons/0002-measurement-plus-leverage.html`
**Key concepts:** measurement · leverage · scalability · barriers to entry · technology as lever
**Source:** https://paulgraham.com/wealth.html — Graham's argument that wealth creation requires *both* measurability (your contribution is trackable) and leverage (your decisions have outsized impact). Technology is the most powerful lever because it scales at zero marginal cost.
**Skill:** Take one deliverable from your consulting or work life. Rate it on two axes: (1) Is your individual contribution clearly measurable? (2) Does this work have leverage — will it reach more people or compound over time? Write a 1-sentence plan to improve the lower-scoring axis.
**Reference doc:** *(extend reference/leverage-types.html)*

### Lesson 3: Donella Meadows — Where to Intervene in Any System
**File:** `lessons/0003-meadows-leverage-points.html`
**Key concepts:** system intervention · parameters · information flows · system goals · paradigm · leverage hierarchy
**Source:** https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/ — Meadows' ranked hierarchy from least to most impactful: parameters (most attention, least change) → information flows → feedback loops → system goals → self-organization → paradigm (hardest to change, most powerful when changed)
**Skill:** Pick a stuck problem in your life or work. List the intervention you most want to make. Then check: is it a parameter-level fix (lowest leverage) or a goal/information-flow level fix (higher leverage)? Reframe the intervention one level higher.
**Reference doc:** `reference/meadows-leverage-hierarchy.html`

## Module 2 — Business Levers
*With the map in hand, Module 2 shows how each lever manifests in business systems — from diagnosing what's slowing you down, to building momentum that accelerates itself, to platforms that compound through other people's actions.*

### Lesson 4: Theory of Constraints — Find and Fix the One Bottleneck
**File:** `lessons/0004-theory-of-constraints.html`
**Key concepts:** bottleneck · throughput · 5 focusing steps · exploit · subordinate · elevate
**Source:** https://www.leanproduction.com/theory-of-constraints/ — Every system has exactly ONE constraint limiting total throughput. The 5 Focusing Steps: Identify → Exploit → Subordinate → Elevate → Repeat. Optimizing non-constraints wastes effort; only the constraint matters.
**Skill:** List the 3–5 main activities in a project or system you manage. For each, estimate: what happens to total output if this activity becomes 2× faster? The one where 2× speed most improves total output is your constraint. Write what you would do tomorrow to exploit it.
**Reference doc:** `reference/theory-of-constraints.html`

### Lesson 5: The Flywheel Effect — Momentum That Compounds
**File:** `lessons/0005-flywheel-effect.html`
**Key concepts:** flywheel · momentum · cumulative effort · doom loop · self-reinforcing cycle
**Source:** https://www.jimcollins.com/concepts/the-flywheel.html — Lasting greatness builds through consistent, cumulative effort in one direction. Amazon's flywheel: lower prices → more customers → more sellers → larger selection → better experience → lower costs → lower prices again. The "doom loop" is the opposite: reversing direction kills compounding momentum.
**Skill:** Sketch your personal or professional flywheel. Start with one thing you do that creates a result that feeds back into enabling you to do more of that thing. Draw the cycle. Then identify one place in the cycle where you've been inconsistent — that's where the flywheel slows.
**Reference doc:** `reference/flywheel-and-compounding.html`

### Lesson 6: Network Effects — The Platform Lever
**File:** `lessons/0006-network-effects.html`
**Key concepts:** network effects · proof-of-work systems · evaporative cooling · early-mover advantage · platform moat
**Source:** https://www.eugenewei.com/blog/2019/2/19/status-as-a-service — Platforms leverage network effects by designing "proof-of-work" systems generating scarce, earned status. Early participants gain disproportionate reach. The fragility caveat: Marc Andreessen notes network effects "unwind just as fast" — when high-status users exit, platforms collapse rapidly.
**Skill:** Name one platform or professional community you could be participating in earlier or more deeply. Estimate: what would your reach be worth if you built it in year 1 vs. year 5 of the platform's growth? What is the "proof-of-work" that earns status in that community?
**Reference doc:** *(extend reference/leverage-types.html)*

## Module 3 — Investing and Compounding
*Module 3 shifts from diagnosing systems to making decisions within them — particularly how time, risk structure, and thinking quality multiply returns in investing and beyond.*

### Lesson 7: Compounding — The Lever Everyone Underestimates
**File:** `lessons/0007-compounding.html`
**Key concepts:** compounding · exponential vs linear thinking · early base · uninterrupted growth · Buffett's wealth curve
**Source:** http://collabfund.com/blog/the-freakishly-strong-base/ — $80.7B of Buffett's $81B fortune accumulated after age 50; had he started at 22 instead of childhood, he'd be worth only ~$1.9B today (a 97.6% reduction). Multiplying 8×8 nine times yields 134 million; adding 8 nine times yields 72. Munger's rule: "Never interrupt compounding unnecessarily."
**Skill:** Pick one area you're compounding (savings, skills, relationships, content, reputation). Calculate — or estimate — what it looks like in 10 years if you never interrupt it. Then write the single most likely way you would interrupt it and what you'll do to prevent that.
**Reference doc:** `reference/flywheel-and-compounding.html`

### Lesson 8: Asymmetric Bets and Optionality
**File:** `lessons/0008-asymmetric-bets.html`
**Key concepts:** asymmetric returns · optionality · barbell strategy · capped downside · uncapped upside
**Source:** https://fs.blog/mental-models/ — Leverage amplifies both gains AND losses. The key is to seek asymmetric positions: capped downside, uncapped upside. Taleb's barbell: 90% safe assets + 10% extremely asymmetric bets — you can't lose more than 10%, but the 10% can 10× or 100×.
**Skill:** Review your current "bets" (investments, projects, commitments). For each, rate: (1) What is the maximum I can lose? (2) What is the realistic upside? Flag any bets where the downside exceeds the upside — these are the ones to restructure first.
**Reference doc:** `reference/investing-mental-models.html`

### Lesson 9: Second-Order Thinking — The Investor's Edge
**File:** `lessons/0009-second-order-thinking.html`
**Key concepts:** second-order effects · "and then what?" · first-order negative / second-order positive · Howard Marks · edge through non-consensus thinking
**Source:** https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/ — First-order thinking is what everyone can do; extraordinary returns come from seeing what others miss. Howard Marks: "If everyone reaches the same first-order conclusion, no edge exists." Many opportunities are "first-order negative, second-order positive."
**Skill:** Take a recent decision you made (or are about to make). Write out the first-order consequence. Now write the second-order consequence — what happens *because* that first-order thing happens? Is the second-order effect better or worse than the first-order? Does this change your decision?
**Reference doc:** `reference/investing-mental-models.html`

## Module 4 — Applying Levers Daily
*The final module ties everything together into a practical operating system — how to design information flows that keep you honest, how to build the permissionless leverage stack as an individual, and how to run the cadences that compound all the levers you've learned.*

### Lesson 10: Information Flow as a System Lever
**File:** `lessons/0010-information-flow.html`
**Key concepts:** feedback loops · information design · transparency effects · accountability structures · dashboard as leverage
**Source:** https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/ — Adding feedback where none existed is one of the highest-leverage interventions. Requiring factories to publicly report toxic emissions dropped pollution ~40% with no new laws or fines. Power over the rules — incentives, constraints, punishments — is "real power."
**Skill:** Identify one area of your life or work where you lack a reliable feedback signal. Design a simple dashboard (even just one number tracked weekly) that would give you that signal. Describe what decision you would make differently if you had this data every Friday.
**Reference doc:** *(extend reference/meadows-leverage-hierarchy.html)*

### Lesson 11: Knowledge, Skills, and the Permissionless Stack
**File:** `lessons/0011-knowledge-leverage.html`
**Key concepts:** knowledge leverage · permissionless leverage · distinctive perspective · content as leverage · consultant leverage ladder
**Source:** https://fs.blog/mental-models/ and https://paulgraham.com/wealth.html — An author whose book reaches millions has leveraged ideas through publishing. Graham: cutting hair serves one customer; solving a software problem helps everyone who adopts it. The AI employee example: using AI multiplies organizational impact far beyond experience level.
**Skill:** Write a 3-sentence "leverage thesis" for your expertise: (1) What do you know that most people in your field don't? (2) How could that knowledge reach 100× more people than you directly serve? (3) What is the one thing you could create (article, tool, methodology) that would start that reach?
**Reference doc:** `reference/leverage-types.html`

### Lesson 12: Your Personal Operating Cadence
**File:** `lessons/0012-personal-cadence.html`
**Key concepts:** daily constraint identification · weekly flywheel review · monthly leverage audit · yearly paradigm check · GTD capture
**Source:** https://gettingthingsdone.com/what-is-gtd/ and synthesis across all report sections — GTD: externalizing thinking frees mental capacity for leverage decisions. TOC daily: identify and protect your one constraint. Meadows yearly: paradigm check — what assumptions limit your leverage ceiling?
**Skill:** Design your personal leverage operating system. Fill in one concrete action for each cadence: (1) Daily — what single constraint will you identify and protect each morning? (2) Weekly — what 1-hour review will you run every Friday? (3) Monthly — what leverage audit question will you answer? (4) Yearly — what paradigm assumption will you challenge?
**Reference doc:** `reference/operating-cadence.html`

## Suggested Teaching Order

Follow modules 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 in sequence. Module 1 gives the vocabulary and hierarchy needed to make sense of all subsequent lessons. Module 2 grounds theory in business mechanics before Module 3 moves to investment and risk. Module 4 only makes sense after the learner has all the levers in hand — it's the "assemble and run" phase.

Within each module, lessons build sequentially: don't skip Lesson 1 (Naval's types) before Lesson 3 (Meadows), as Meadows' hierarchy is easier to apply once you know what type of lever you're dealing with.
