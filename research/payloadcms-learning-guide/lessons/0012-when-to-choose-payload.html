When to Choose Payload — and When Not To

~12 min Module 4 — Industry Assessment
Primary Source
payloadcms.com/compare

Payload's official comparison page — where the team documents the honest trade-offs between Payload and competing CMS tools, covering framework coupling, ecosystem maturity, and target use cases.

The Core Premise: Payload Is a Bet on Next.js

Every evaluation of Payload CMS should start with one question: Are you building with Next.js? Unlike Strapi, Directus, or Contentful — which are framework-agnostic and connect to any frontend via API — Payload is architecturally coupled to Next.js. The admin panel, routing, and server components are all built on top of the Next.js runtime. This is not an incidental implementation detail; it is a deliberate design choice that Payload's team has leaned into as a core value proposition.

The payoff for teams on Next.js is significant: your CMS logic, API routes, and frontend live in the same repo, share the same TypeScript types, and deploy as a single unit. The cost for teams not on Next.js is equally significant: you lose every benefit of that co-location and are left with a heavyweight system that requires unnecessary scaffolding just to adapt to a different stack.

Getting this question right saves you from investing weeks in a tool that was never a fit. The sections below walk through all material trade-offs — not just the framework coupling — so you can make an informed call.

Payload's Trade-offs in Detail

No CMS is uniformly superior. Here are the genuine weaknesses you should weigh before committing to Payload:

Trade-off What it means in practice Who it hurts most
Next.js dependency Payload's admin UI, routing, and server components are built on Next.js internals. Teams using Remix, SvelteKit, Nuxt, or a plain Express/Node backend must fight the grain. Any team not on Next.js
Newer ecosystem Compared to WordPress (20+ years of plugins) or even Strapi (large community integrations), Payload has a smaller library of ready-made plugins and community extensions. Expect to build more from scratch. Teams that rely on off-the-shelf integrations
Admin UI theming complexity Payload's admin panel is itself a React app. Surface-level customisation (logo, colours) is straightforward, but deep visual overrides require knowledge of React component overrides and SCSS custom properties. Teams without front-end engineering capacity
Figma acquisition uncertainty Payload was acquired by Figma. The open-source licence has not changed, but strategic direction, feature prioritisation, and future licensing decisions are now influenced by a corporate roadmap. Teams that require long-term open-source commitment guarantees
Code-first learning curve Every collection, field, and access rule is defined in TypeScript config files — not through a GUI wizard. This is a superpower for developers and a blocker for non-technical content managers who need to modify the schema themselves. Non-technical teams or agencies managing client schemas

When to Choose Payload

Payload is the right call when several of these conditions align:

  • You are building a Next.js application and want your CMS, API, and business logic in a single deployable repo. The developer experience benefit is real and compounding — shared types mean you catch schema mismatches at compile time rather than at runtime.
  • You need custom content workflows, complex access control, or multi-tenancy. Payload's hooks, access functions, and collection-level permissions are designed for non-trivial authorization logic. If your project has user roles, tenant isolation, or approval workflows, Payload's code-first config handles it without plugins.
  • You want to avoid SaaS pricing as you scale. Self-hosted and open-source, Payload does not charge per seat, per API call, or per environment. For high-traffic or large-team projects, the total cost of ownership compares favourably to Contentful, Sanity, or similar hosted options.
  • Your team is comfortable with TypeScript and wants full ownership of data and infrastructure. Payload stores data in MongoDB or Postgres — your database, your schema, your backups. There is no proprietary data layer that traps your content.

When NOT to Choose Payload

Equally important: the scenarios where a different tool will serve you better.

  • Your team is not using Next.js. This is the clearest disqualifier. If your stack is Remix, Astro, SvelteKit, or a decoupled backend, Payload's tight Next.js coupling creates friction at every layer — routing, middleware, deployment. Choose Directus or Strapi instead; both are framework-agnostic.
  • Your team is non-technical and needs a GUI-first admin experience. Payload's schema is code. A content manager who needs to add a new field or restructure a collection will require a developer to update the config and redeploy. WordPress and even Strapi offer GUI schema editors that non-developers can operate.
  • You need a large third-party plugin ecosystem. WordPress has over 50,000 plugins. If your requirements include a specific payment gateway, form builder, SEO suite, or CRM integration and you want a maintained plugin rather than custom code, WordPress or Strapi are far ahead.
  • You require stable long-term open-source commitment guarantees. Directus, for example, is backed by a community-governed foundation with an explicit open-core model and no pending acquisition. Payload's Figma ownership adds a corporate variable to its roadmap that pure community-driven projects do not carry.

The Figma Acquisition — What We Know and Don't Know

In 2024, Figma acquired Payload CMS. At the time of writing, the MIT licence remains in place and active development has continued. Figma's stated intention is to help Payload grow as an open-source project. That is the honest summary of what is publicly documented.

What is less certain is the medium-term roadmap. Acquisitions change incentive structures. Features that benefit enterprise Figma customers may be prioritised over community requests. Open-source licences can change in future versions — a pattern seen with projects like HashiCorp Terraform, Redis, and Elastic. None of these changes have happened with Payload, but the structural risk exists in a way that it does not for independently governed projects like Directus.

The practical guidance: if you are building a project with a three-to-five-year horizon, treat the acquisition as a factor to monitor rather than a current blocker. If your organisation has legal or procurement requirements around open-source licence stability, document your due diligence now and set a review checkpoint at each major Payload version release.

Decision Criteria at a Glance

Question Favours Payload Favours an alternative
Frontend framework? Next.js Remix, SvelteKit, Nuxt, or framework-agnostic
Team technical profile? TypeScript developers comfortable with code-first config Non-technical editors or GUI-first preference
Plugin needs? Willing to build custom integrations Relies on large third-party plugin library (→ WordPress)
Deployment model? Self-hosted, full infrastructure ownership Managed SaaS preferred (→ Contentful, Sanity)
Access control complexity? Multi-tenant, custom roles, complex workflows Simple public/private toggle is sufficient
Open-source stability priority? Acceptable with monitoring Hard requirement for community governance (→ Directus)
Core insight: Payload is the ideal CMS for TypeScript developers who are already committed to Next.js and want to own their data, their infrastructure, and their content model — without paying SaaS pricing at scale. It is purpose-built for that profile, and it excels there. Outside that profile, its trade-offs accumulate quickly.
Figma acquisition watch: Unlike Directus, which is governed by a community foundation with an explicit open-core model, Payload's open-source roadmap is now shaped by a corporate acquirer. The MIT licence is intact today, but history shows that corporate ownership introduces licence-change risk over time. Teams with strict long-term open-source requirements should track Payload's major version releases and licence file changes as part of routine vendor due diligence.
