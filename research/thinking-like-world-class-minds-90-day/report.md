## The Foundation (Days 1–15)

### First Principles: The Bedrock Technique

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/first-principles/
- **Summary**: First-principles thinking means separating verified truths from inherited assumptions, then reasoning upward from irreducible facts. The key technique is Socratic questioning ("Why? What do I actually know?") combined with the Five Whys. Elon Musk discovered rocket materials cost only ~2% of market price by asking *what rockets are actually made of* rather than accepting conventional pricing — and founded SpaceX. Most people reason by analogy, iterating on existing solutions; first principles reveals genuinely new possibilities.
- **Relevance**: This is the foundation technique used by Musk, Feynman, and Torvalds — the habit of stripping every problem down before building back up.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before accepting a project estimate, ask "what are the actual constraints here?" — time, resources, dependencies. Don't inherit the assumption that something takes 2 weeks because it always has.
- *At home*: Your commute takes 45 min. Ask: what's the actual bottleneck? Route? Departure time? Transport mode? One question can surface a 15-min solution hiding in an assumption.
- *For learning*: Instead of "I need to study all of Chapter 3," ask "what is the one thing in Chapter 3 I don't actually understand?" Then study that.

**Time to implement**: 5 minutes per problem. Write the problem at the top of a page, then list every assumption underneath it. Circle the ones you haven't verified. That's your work.

### Charlie Munger's Latticework of Mental Models

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/mental-models/
- **Summary**: Mental models are compressed tools for navigating complexity. Munger advocated pulling the strongest concepts from multiple disciplines and weaving them together — no single field holds all the answers. Key models with real-world applications: Inertia (start with one push-up, not fifty), Bottlenecks (fix the slowest process first), Trade-offs (every "yes" carries an invisible "no"), Red Queen Effect (companies must keep innovating just to maintain position). The power emerges from *combination*, not mastery of any one discipline.
- **Relevance**: Munger's framework explains why all 11 thinkers draw from biology, physics, economics, and mathematics simultaneously — cross-domain thinking is the operating system, not a bonus skill.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Apply the Bottleneck model before a project review. Ask: where does work *actually* pile up? That's your leverage point — everything else is optimization theater.
- *At home*: Apply Inertia to any habit you're building. Don't design the perfect system. Just make the smallest possible first step invisible to your resistance.
- *For career*: Apply Trade-offs every time you say yes to a meeting or task. Make the invisible cost visible: "Yes to this means no to what?"

**Time to implement**: Build one new mental model per week (15 days = 2 models). Write one page: what is it, one example from life, one place you'll apply it this week.

### Combining the Feynman Technique & Mental Models

- **Source**: https://jamesclear.com/feynman-mental-models
- **Summary**: Feynman's edge wasn't raw intelligence — it was a *different toolbox*. He self-taught from advanced texts outside the standard curriculum and applied one core calculus technique (differentiating under the integral sign) across many different problems. The practical approach: master one mental model deeply, then practice applying it broadly. Build a personal model library of 80–90 key models from biology, economics, physics, psychology. Cross-pollinate: ask the same question through multiple lenses simultaneously. "All perspectives hold some truth; none contain the complete truth."
- **Relevance**: Shows the compound effect of combining Feynman's simplification discipline with Munger's multi-model approach — the two techniques reinforce each other.

**Real-life examples:**
- *For learning*: Pick one model (e.g., Inversion). Spend a week applying it to everything — your career decisions, a coding problem, a relationship conflict. Depth before breadth.
- *At work*: When stuck on a problem, ask "how would a biologist see this? An economist? A physicist?" The first alien perspective often breaks the deadlock.

## Separating Signal from Noise (Days 16–30)

### Signal vs. Noise: The Frequency Problem

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/noise-and-signal-nassim-taleb/
- **Summary**: More information doesn't mean more understanding — as data volume grows, the *ratio* of noise to signal worsens. Checking data daily yields ~95% noise; hourly is ~99.5% noise. Overexposure transforms calm, signal-focused thinkers into reactive people who mistake randomness for meaningful patterns. Truly important information tends to reach you regardless — "significant signals have a way to reach you." The solution: focus only on *large* changes, ration information intake deliberately, resist the illusion that more consumption equals more insight.
- **Relevance**: Directly explains why Taleb, Munger, Dalio, and Knuth all famously limit information intake — it's not discipline for its own sake, it's because frequency of checking amplifies noise, not signal.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Stop checking Slack/email every 20 minutes. Set 3 fixed check-in times (morning, midday, end of day). After 2 weeks, notice how many "urgent" messages resolved themselves before you saw them.
- *For investing/news*: Ask "will this matter in 10 years?" before reacting. If not, it's almost certainly noise amplified by recency.
- *For learning*: Don't skim 20 articles. Read 2 deeply. Depth extracts signal; breadth accumulates noise.

**Time to implement**: Set a phone timer — check news/social media maximum 2x daily. Track for 2 weeks. The anxiety drops; important things still reach you.

### Circle of Competence

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/circle-of-competence/
- **Summary**: Everyone possesses a domain of earned knowledge. The critical skill isn't expanding that circle — it's recognizing when you're approaching its edge. Confidence erodes *slower* than competence when you venture outside your area of expertise. Munger and Buffett skipped pharmaceuticals entirely because others had superior knowledge there. Rose Blumkin (Nebraska Furniture Mart) mastered furniture and real estate, ignoring everything else, and built an empire. The bottom line: depth over breadth, honest calibration of where your perimeter lies.
- **Relevance**: All 11 thinkers are radically honest about what they don't know. This isn't humility as personality — it's a cognitive tool that prevents expensive errors from overconfidence outside your edge.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before giving advice in a meeting, ask: "Is this inside my circle?" If you're guessing, say "I'm not sure, but here's how I'd approach finding out." This builds trust faster than confident guessing.
- *For career*: Know which 3 areas you have genuine earned advantage in. Compete there. Say no to everything else (or learn explicitly before weighing in).

**Time to implement**: Write your circle honestly this week. Three columns: "I know deeply," "I know a little," "I'm guessing." Review it quarterly.

### Inversion: Thinking Backwards

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/inversion/
- **Summary**: Inversion means tackling problems by reasoning in reverse. Instead of only asking "how do I achieve X?", ask "what would guarantee the *opposite* of X?" then avoid those things. Munger: "Invert, always invert." His famous line: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there." Applied: instead of "how do we foster innovation?" ask "what kills innovation?" then systematically remove those things. Key insight: avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance.
- **Relevance**: Inversion is the fastest path to identifying constraints and failure modes — used by Munger, Dalio (who obsessively stress-tests strategies against worst-case scenarios), and Linus (who designs for failure modes first).

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before a project kickoff, spend 10 minutes on a "pre-mortem" — imagine it's 6 months later and the project failed. What went wrong? Fix those things now.
- *For decisions*: When considering a new habit or commitment, don't just ask "what's the upside?" Ask "what would make this definitely fail?" If you can't avoid those failure conditions, don't start.
- *For learning*: Instead of "what should I study?" ask "what gaps in my understanding have caused me the most problems?" Study those.

**Time to implement**: Add a 10-minute inversion step to every important decision. Write: "What would guarantee failure here?" before writing "what would lead to success?"

## Structured Thinking in Practice (Days 31–45)

### The Feynman Technique: 4 Steps to Deep Understanding

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/feynman-technique/
- **Summary**: Nobel physicist Feynman believed jargon and complexity often hide shallow understanding. His 4-step method: (1) Map what you know on a blank page. (2) Explain it as if teaching a 12-year-old — if you struggle to simplify, that reveals gaps. (3) Review and refine: wherever your explanation breaks down, return to source material and study those weak areas. (4) Test by teaching to an actual person. As Feynman said: "Anyone can make a subject complicated but only someone who understands can make it simple."
- **Relevance**: This is Andrew Ng's core teaching methodology, Karpathy's "explain from scratch" approach, and the reason Feynman became the most important physics communicator of the 20th century. Deep understanding = ability to simplify.

**Real-life examples:**
- *For learning anything new at work*: After a meeting or training session, take 5 minutes to write "explain this to a new hire in 3 sentences." Where you can't, you identified what you need to re-read.
- *For career skills*: Pick one skill you use daily (e.g., SQL queries, stakeholder communication, system design). Write a 1-page explanation for a smart 12-year-old. Notice the gaps. Fill them.
- *At home*: When you're stuck on a decision, try explaining the situation out loud to someone outside your field. The act of simplifying often reveals what you already know but haven't articulated.

**Time to implement**: 15–20 minutes per topic. One topic per week minimum during Days 31–45.

### Andrej Karpathy: Learn by Reproducing

- **Source**: https://karpathy.github.io/2022/03/14/lecun1989/
- **Summary**: Karpathy re-implemented a 1989 deep learning paper from scratch to extract timeless principles. His method: isolate variables systematically (swap one component at a time, measure each independently), use history as a mirror (benchmark old methods against new to identify what *actually* matters), and project forward from patterns. Key insight: "Deep understanding comes from *doing* — rebuilding, measuring, and projecting — not just reading about results." He found that simple improvements (better loss functions, better optimizers) cut errors ~60% without architectural overhaul.
- **Relevance**: This is the "active reconstruction" method used by Knuth (re-derive algorithms), Feynman (re-derive physics), and the core of how Jeff Dean builds intuition for systems at scale.

**Real-life examples:**
- *For learning a new tool/framework*: Don't just follow a tutorial. Build the tutorial's output from scratch, without looking. Where you get stuck = where you need to understand more deeply.
- *At work*: When you inherit a system or process, try to reproduce the logic from scratch before modifying it. You'll find assumptions the original author didn't document.
- *For career skills*: Pick one skill you think you know (e.g., writing a performance review, building a spreadsheet model). Rebuild it from memory without your notes. Gaps surface immediately.

**Time to implement**: One "reproduce from scratch" exercise per week, ~1 hour each. Block it on your calendar.

### Karpathy's Reasoning Framework: Paradigm Shifts as Design Problems

- **Source**: https://karpathy.medium.com/software-2-0-a64152b37c35
- **Summary**: Karpathy builds understanding through analogy-driven abstraction — mapping unfamiliar concepts onto familiar ones to make abstract ideas concrete. He surveys multiple domains to demonstrate a pattern, letting empirical breadth build the case rather than arguing theoretically. Key move: ask "what does this paradigm shift demand of everything surrounding it?" — turning observations into design problems. He also practices honest limitation acknowledgment, balancing enthusiasm with candor about failure modes.
- **Relevance**: This is the cross-domain reasoning pattern shared by Munger (latticework), Feynman (analogies to make physics tangible), and Dalio (systems thinking across macro + micro).

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: When a new technology or process arrives, don't just ask "how does this work?" Ask "what does this change demand of our *current* infrastructure, people, and processes?" That's where the real work is.
- *For career*: When you see a paradigm shift in your field, ask "what skills/tools/roles does this make obsolete? What does it demand that doesn't exist yet?" Position yourself for the demand, not the obsolescence.

## Communicating to Clarify & Influence (Days 46–60)

### Paul Graham: Writing IS Thinking

- **Source**: https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html
- **Summary**: Graham's central claim: "Writing doesn't just communicate ideas; it generates them." Key implications: thinking and writing are inseparable; roughly "80% of the ideas in an essay happen after you start writing" — the draft *is* the thinking. Starting imperfectly is essential; perfectionism blocks the thinking process. Publishing amplifies thought generation. The act of revision isn't polish applied after thinking — it IS the thinking. Poor writers aren't just bad communicators — they're leaving a significant portion of their intellectual potential untapped.
- **Relevance**: This explains why Feynman kept notebooks obsessively, why Dalio wrote "Principles" before publishing it (years of internal memos), and why Hamming said 50% of great work is presentation. Writing is how these thinkers *find* their ideas, not just share them.

**Real-life examples:**
- *For career*: Before any important meeting or decision, write a 1-page brief — not to share, just to think. You will discover 3–4 things you hadn't considered while writing.
- *For learning*: After reading an article or attending a talk, write 3 sentences: what's the key idea, what it changes about how you think, one way you'll use it. 5 minutes. Transforms passive consumption into active understanding.
- *At home*: Keep a decision journal. When facing a big decision, write the situation, your options, and your reasoning *before* you decide. Review it 3 months later. You'll learn your own blind spots faster than any book can teach you.

**Time to implement**: 10 minutes of writing every day. No audience required. A blank document or notebook. The constraint is: write, don't type notes to yourself — full sentences, complete thoughts.

### Paul Graham: Independent Thinking

- **Source**: https://www.paulgraham.com/think.html
- **Summary**: Independent thinking has three components: (1) fastidiousness about truth — carefully calibrating degrees of belief rather than letting opinions rush toward extremes, (2) resistance to being told what to think — not passive skepticism but active delight in ideas that subvert conventional wisdom, (3) curiosity — the actual engine of novel ideas, which amplifies rather than satisfies itself. Key technique: seek "unfashionable" areas, since overlooked ideas are disproportionately likely to lead somewhere. Watch how ideas spread through groups like waves; notice intellectual fashions and resist them.
- **Relevance**: This describes exactly how Torvalds built Linux (nobody thought a free OS kernel was viable), how Sutskever bet on scaling when the field was skeptical, and how Munger built his mental models outside mainstream finance.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: When consensus forms in a meeting, pause and ask: "What's the strongest argument against this?" Not to be contrarian — to find what the group is missing.
- *For career*: Identify one "unfashionable" area in your field — something others aren't paying attention to because it's not trending. Study it. The signal-to-competition ratio is highest there.

### How to Think: Slow Down and Concentrate

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/how-to-think/
- **Summary**: Research shows heavy multitaskers perform worse at filtering information, organizing thoughts, and even switching tasks. Thinking means "concentrating on one thing long enough to develop an idea about it." Great thinkers (Joyce, Eliot) worked at a crawl, producing finest work by depth not speed. Initial reactions are recycled conventional wisdom — original ideas emerge only by staying patient with a problem, allowing the mind to form unexpected connections. Key insight: "If you're thinking without writing, you only think you're thinking." (Leslie Lamport)
- **Relevance**: This is the cognitive basis for why Knuth doesn't use email, why Dalio schedules "thinking time," and why Feynman's famous "I have no responsibilities" sabbaticals produced breakthroughs.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Block 90 minutes of "no-meeting, no-Slack" time each morning for deep work. Protect it. The first week is uncomfortable. The second week is when the best ideas arrive.
- *For learning*: When you read something complex, close all other tabs. One article, full attention, 20 minutes. This is 3x more effective than skimming 10 articles.

### Forming Strong Opinions: The Munger Standard

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/the-work-required-to-have-an-opinion/
- **Summary**: "I never allow myself to have an opinion on anything that I don't know the other side's argument better than they do." — Charlie Munger. Truly holding an opinion requires: understanding opposing arguments deeply, actively seeking contradictory evidence, testing ideas against the strongest counterarguments. Darwin's method: collect inconvenient facts instead of dismissing them, ensuring theory accounts for *all* evidence. Practical test: ask opinionated people to write down their reasoning — most won't, which reveals everything. Strong thinkers remain calm when challenged and willingly change their minds when evidence demands it.
- **Relevance**: This separates the 11 thinkers from confident-but-wrong generalists. Feynman famously said "The first principle is that you must not fool yourself — and you are the easiest person to fool." Pearl built causal inference theory by systematically invalidating what everyone "knew" about correlation.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before advocating for a decision in a meeting, write one paragraph of the *strongest case against it*. If you can't write that paragraph convincingly, you don't understand your own position well enough yet.
- *For career decisions*: Before making a big move, actively seek out people who would say "that's a bad idea." Listen to understand, not to rebut. Their best argument = your most important thing to resolve.

## Deciding What to Do Next (Days 61–75)

### Richard Hamming: Work on Important Problems

- **Source**: https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html
- **Summary**: "It is not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack." Maintain a mental list of 10–20 significant open questions so you can pounce when a new idea connects to one of them. Reserve time for big-picture thinking — Hamming dedicated Friday afternoons to "great thoughts only." Key mindset: "Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest" — consistent extra effort compounds dramatically. Communicate your work — at least 50% of effort should go toward presentation: clear writing, formal talks, in-meeting advocacy.
- **Relevance**: This is Hamming's core framework, shared with Jeff Dean (who maintains a mental list of open systems problems), Feynman (who kept a "Feynman problems" notebook), and Musk (who asks "what is the most important thing I could be doing?").

**Real-life examples:**
- *For career*: Write your 10 most important open questions — problems in your field or life you'd most like to solve. Review monthly. When you learn something new, ask "does this connect to one of my 10 questions?"
- *At work*: Block Friday afternoon (or any equivalent) for thinking that's NOT about current tasks — strategy, learning, big-picture reflection. Guard it like a meeting with your CEO.
- *For learning*: When you read or study, ask "which of my 10 open questions does this touch?" Learning with a question activates retention 3–4x better than passive reading.

**Time to implement**: 30 minutes to write your first 10 open questions. This is the most important single exercise in this entire 90-day system.

### Second-Order Thinking

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/
- **Summary**: Second-order thinking means pushing beyond immediate consequences to examine downstream effects. Howard Marks: first-level thinking is "fast, simple, superficial"; second-level is "deep, complex, and convoluted — asking what others are missing." Ray Dalio: "Failing to consider second- and third-order consequences is the cause of a lot of painfully bad decisions." Practical: always ask "And then what?" — project consequences across 10 minutes, 10 months, 10 years. Many great opportunities appear first-order negative but second-order positive.
- **Relevance**: This is the core decision-making upgrade used by Dalio (macro investing), Musk (long-arc tech bets like EVs and reusable rockets in 2002), and Munger (refusing to make financially obvious moves that had second-order downsides).

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before any team decision, add one question to your process: "And then what?" Run it 2–3 times. The third iteration almost always surfaces the hidden consequence.
- *For career*: A promotion looks first-order positive. Second-order: more management, less technical work, higher visibility for mistakes. Is that still positive for *you*? Second-order thinking reveals the real tradeoff.
- *At home*: Before starting a new commitment (course, project, responsibility), ask: "If I add this, what does it crowd out? What happens to those things? And then what?"

**Time to implement**: Add "And then what?" as a standard closing question in any important decision. Takes 2 minutes. Changes outcomes significantly.

### Hard vs. Important: Don't Confuse the Two

- **Source**: https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/
- **Summary**: "Don't look for hard problems — important ones are ultimately more fun." Difficulty and importance are separate dimensions that often don't overlap. Education rewards puzzle-solving with clear grades, training people to seek increasingly hard technical challenges. This creates a flawed mental model for real-world impact. Exceptional thinkers identify *important* problems, not just hard ones — and redirect intelligence toward better problem selection and building systems that solve problems at scale. The practical test: "Is this where I want to spend my cleverness?"
- **Relevance**: This explains why Torvalds spent effort on tooling (git) that others thought was beneath them, why Knuth spent decades on TeX (seemingly a solved problem), and why Andrew Ng chose to democratize ML education over publishing papers.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: List your 5 most time-consuming tasks. For each, ask: "Is this hard, or is this important?" Hard-but-not-important tasks are candidates for delegation or elimination.
- *For career*: The trap is optimizing skills in your current role rather than asking "is this role pointed at an important problem?" Technical mastery is compounding — but only if aimed at the right problem.

## Daily Habits & 90-Day Practice System (Days 76–90)

### Deliberate Practice for Cognitive Skills

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/deliberate-practice/
- **Summary**: Deliberate practice means improving with "a clear awareness of the specific components of a skill we're aiming to improve." For thinking: break it into sub-skills (logic, bias recognition, mental models, probabilistic reasoning), work in the learning zone (problems just beyond current comfort), measure outcomes (track predictions vs. results), use feedback partners. Daily structure: Morning (~20 min): review one mental model, apply it to a current decision, write a prediction. Midday (~10 min): reflect on a recent decision, identify one bias. Evening (~15 min): review decisions against outcomes, log errors honestly. Research suggests meaningful cognitive improvement follows a long arc — small daily gains compound into large capability shifts.
- **Relevance**: This is the operational framework that makes the entire 90-day system work — without deliberate practice structure, mental models stay theoretical and don't transfer to real decisions.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Keep a prediction log. Before a meeting, write: "I predict the main concern will be X." After: "Was I right? Why or why not?" 5 minutes per meeting. After 30 days, your prediction accuracy will measurably improve.
- *For learning*: One "stretch problem" per week — a problem just beyond your current ability. The discomfort = growth. Comfort = stagnation.
- *At home*: Before sleep, 5 minutes: "What was my best decision today? What was my worst? What would I change?" This is the single highest-ROI reflection habit.

**Time to implement**: 45 minutes total per day — 20 min morning, 10 min midday, 15 min evening. The daily structure is the system.

### The Eisenhower Matrix: Daily Prioritization

- **Source**: https://fs.blog/eisenhower-matrix/
- **Summary**: Tasks sorted into 4 quadrants: (1) Important + Urgent → Do it, (2) Important + Not Urgent → Schedule it (deep work), (3) Urgent + Not Important → Delegate or minimize, (4) Not Important + Not Urgent → Eliminate. Eisenhower: "What is important is seldom urgent and what is urgent is seldom important." Daily application: morning for important non-urgent work (deep writing, strategic planning, skill-building), afternoon for urgent+important deadlines, ongoing elimination of Quadrant 4. Ruthlessly cutting 30–50% of low-value activities frees energy for what actually matters.
- **Relevance**: This is the daily operational layer that Dalio, Knuth (extreme focus), and Hamming (Friday afternoon thinking time) use to protect their most important cognitive work from urgency theater.

**Real-life examples:**
- *At work*: Before you open Slack in the morning, spend 5 minutes sorting your task list into the 4 quadrants. Then work Quadrant 2 first. This single habit separates productive people from busy ones.
- *For career*: Learning a new skill is almost always Quadrant 2 (important, not urgent). It's the first thing cut when Quadrant 1 floods. Protect it explicitly or it won't happen.
- *At home*: Apply the matrix to personal commitments. Most social obligations that feel urgent are actually Quadrant 3 or 4 with someone else's urgency leaking into your schedule.

**Time to implement**: 5 minutes each morning. Pen and paper. Three columns: do today, schedule, eliminate.

## The 90-Day Progression: Integrated Practice

| Phase | Days | Core Focus | Daily Time | Key Exercise |
|-------|------|-----------|-----------|--------------|
| **Foundation** | 1–15 | First principles + mental model #1 and #2 | 30 min | Weekly: write one model page + one real-world application |
| **Signal/Noise** | 16–30 | Filter inputs, map circle of competence | 30 min | Daily: 2x info check-in max; write your competence circle |
| **Structured Thinking** | 31–45 | Feynman technique + reproduce-from-scratch | 45 min | Weekly: one topic explained to a 12-year-old + one rebuild exercise |
| **Communication** | 46–60 | Daily writing as thinking, teach one concept | 45 min | Daily: 10 min writing; weekly: explain one thing to someone |
| **Prioritization** | 61–75 | 10 open questions, second-order thinking | 45 min | Write your 10 questions; add "And then what?" to every decision |
| **System** | 76–90 | Full daily loop: predict → act → review | 45 min | Morning model review, midday decision check, evening journal |

**The compounding principle**: Each phase builds on the last. By Day 90, you're not doing 6 separate habits — you're running one integrated loop: *strip assumptions → filter noise → reason from structure → communicate clearly → prioritize what matters → review and improve.*

This is the operating system shared by Musk, Feynman, Munger, Dalio, Torvalds, Jeff Dean, Knuth, Ng, Karpathy, Sutskever, and Pearl — not identical in form, but identical in structure.
