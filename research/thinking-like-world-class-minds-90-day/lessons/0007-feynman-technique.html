The Feynman Technique — 4 Steps to Deep Understanding

⌛ ~20 min Module 3: Structured Thinking & Communication
Primary Source
The Feynman Technique: How to Learn Anything — Farnam Street fs.blog/feynman-technique — Nobel Prize-winning physicist Richard Feynman believed that jargon and complexity often disguise shallow understanding. His four-step method: map what you know, explain it to a 12-year-old, identify gaps where the explanation breaks down, and test by teaching a real person. Where explanation fails, genuine learning begins.

Jargon as Disguise: What Complexity Hides

Richard Feynman, who won the Nobel Prize in Physics, noticed something uncomfortable about the way experts talk: the more technical the language, the easier it is to hide that you do not fully understand what you are saying. Jargon lets people sound authoritative while glossing over the exact places where their reasoning is fuzzy.

This is not only a problem for physicists. It happens in every field. A software engineer who can recite the definition of a microservice but struggles to explain why you would use one over a monolith is operating on borrowed vocabulary, not genuine understanding. A manager who talks about "synergizing cross-functional stakeholders" without being able to explain what specific outcome that produces is doing the same thing.

Feynman's insight was that simplicity is the test of understanding, not its enemy. If you cannot explain something in plain language — without invoking specialized terms as a shortcut — you are revealing the boundary of what you actually know. The Feynman Technique makes that boundary visible, then gives you a method for pushing it outward.

Core Insight
"Anyone can make a subject complicated but only someone who understands can make it simple." — Richard Feynman. Simplification is not dumbing down. It is the highest proof of understanding, because it requires that every part of a concept be clear enough to translate into ordinary language.

The Four Steps of the Feynman Technique

Step 1 — Map Your Knowledge

Take a blank page and write down everything you currently know about the topic. Do not organize it yet; just get it all out. Feynman suggested using different colors to track newly acquired information as your understanding grows — so you can see at a glance what you brought in from prior knowledge versus what you added in this study session. The blank page is a starting inventory, not a final document.

Step 2 — Explain It Simply

Rewrite what you know as though you are teaching someone around twelve years old — smart and curious, but without your background. Do not assume shared vocabulary. Anywhere you reach for jargon or abbreviate an idea with a technical label, stop: that is a signal that you are avoiding the explanation, not giving it.

If you struggle to simplify a part of the topic, that difficulty is diagnostic. It reveals precisely where your grasp of the material is incomplete. You have not failed — the technique is doing its job. The struggle is the information.

Example: suppose you are studying quantum entanglement. A genuine 12-year-old explanation might be: "Two particles behave like twins, reacting to each other instantly no matter the distance." Notice where that sentence becomes harder to extend — what it means for "instantly," what "reacting" involves — and you have found the gaps.

Step 3 — Review and Refine

Reread your simplified explanation critically. Every place where your explanation is vague, circular, or relies on undefined terms is a gap. For each gap, return to the source material — the textbook, the paper, the original documentation — and study that specific weak area until you can explain it plainly. Then rewrite those sections of your explanation.

This step is a loop, not a one-time pass. You may cycle through Steps 2 and 3 several times before an explanation holds together end to end without any shortcuts.

Step 4 — Test and Archive

Teach the concept to an actual person. Not a hypothetical 12-year-old — a real colleague, friend, or family member who is unfamiliar with the topic. Try not to rely heavily on notes. Real-time questions from a real listener will surface gaps that solo writing misses, because a listener will not politely accept vague phrases the way you might accept them from yourself.

Once you are satisfied that your explanation holds up under questioning, write it out cleanly and store it for future review. Writing forces clearer thinking, reveals hidden gaps, and helps organize ideas in a retrievable form. Your stored explanation becomes a reference you can revisit — and the act of returning to it weeks later is itself another round of testing.

Common Misconception
The most common mistake is concluding that a concept is simply "too complex to explain simply." Feynman explicitly rejected this. Complexity is not a property of topics — it is a property of incomplete understanding. The fact that quantum mechanics is genuinely difficult does not exempt anyone from applying this technique to it; Feynman built his career on doing exactly that. When a concept feels irreducibly complex, the Feynman Technique is most needed, not least applicable.

Applying the Technique Outside the Classroom

The Feynman Technique is not confined to studying for exams. It applies wherever understanding matters more than the appearance of it.

At Work: The New-Hire Test

After any meeting or training session, take five minutes to write: explain this to a new hire in three sentences. Wherever you cannot produce three coherent sentences, you have identified what you need to re-read before you act on what you heard. This is a faster and more honest form of meeting notes than bullet-point summaries, because it exposes what you absorbed versus what you merely heard.

For Career Skills: Write the 12-Year-Old Explanation

Pick one skill you use daily — SQL queries, stakeholder communication, system design, financial modeling, whatever is central to your work. Write a one-page explanation for a smart 12-year-old. Notice where the writing stalls. Those are the gaps in your professional knowledge that formal training and daily habit may have papered over for years. Fill them deliberately.

At Home: Speak It Out Loud

When you are stuck on a difficult decision, try explaining the situation out loud to someone outside your field. The act of simplifying for a listener who does not share your context often reveals what you already know but have not yet articulated — and what you are still assuming without basis.

As a Teaching Philosophy

The technique scales to full curricula. Andrew Ng's core teaching methodology builds on the same principle: design every explanation so it requires no prior specialized vocabulary. Andrej Karpathy's "explain from scratch" approach — rebuilding neural networks from first principles in lectures and essays — is a direct application of Feynman's Step 2. Both have produced unusually clear instructional material because both treat simplification as rigor, not compromise.

Check Your Understanding

When your simple explanation breaks down during Step 2, what does Feynman say it reveals?

Skill Exercise — The 12-Year-Old Explanation

Pick a concept you use at work but have never tried to explain simply — a technical process, a framework you apply, a skill others ask you about. Write a 150-word explanation for a smart 12-year-old: no jargon, no acronyms, no shortcuts. When you are done, go back and mark the first sentence where you felt stuck or vague. That sentence is your real learning target for this week. Return to the source material and study only that gap.
