Work on Important Problems — The Hamming Framework

⌛ ~25 min Module 4: Deciding What to Do Next
Primary Source: Richard Hamming — "You and Your Research" (1986) cs.virginia.edu — YouAndYourResearch.html — A lecture by Bell Labs mathematician Richard Hamming on why some researchers do great work and others do not. Core finding: work on problems that matter and that you have a real angle of attack on. Maintain a live list of open questions. Guard time for big-picture thinking. Treat communication as at least half the job.

The Two-Filter Test: Consequence and Reasonable Attack

Most people who want to do important work think the key variable is picking the most consequential problem they can identify. Hamming's research into why certain Bell Labs scientists produced great results and others did not revealed a more precise criterion: consequence alone is not enough.

His formulation is direct: "It is not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack." A problem that matters enormously but that you have no viable angle on is not, in practice, yours to work on. The "reasonable attack" condition is what converts a consequential abstract concern into an actual research agenda.

This two-filter test changes how you should evaluate potential work. When you encounter a problem, run it through both filters: Is this genuinely consequential? And — do I have, or can I develop, a viable way in? A problem that passes only the first filter belongs on a watch list, not on your calendar. A problem that passes both is where your energy should go.

The same framework shows up independently across multiple fields. Jeff Dean maintains a mental list of open systems problems and looks for technical developments that give him new traction on one of them. Richard Feynman kept a notebook of "Feynman problems" — a standing list of questions he returned to whenever he encountered a fresh idea. Elon Musk asks, "What is the most important thing I could be doing?" — but the implicit follow-up is always: and is there something I can actually do about it now?

"It is not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack." The two-filter test is Hamming's most actionable insight: importance without a viable approach is just anxiety. The pairing of consequence and traction is what makes a problem worth scheduling.

Maintaining Your 10 Open Questions

The practical instrument Hamming recommends is a standing mental list of 10 to 20 significant open questions — problems in your career, field, or life that you genuinely want to solve. The list is not a to-do list. It is a filter you carry with you permanently, so that when a new idea, technique, or conversation crosses your path, you can immediately ask: does this connect to one of my 10 questions?

The power of the list is that it lets you pounce. Hamming observed that breakthroughs often come not from grinding on a problem directly but from recognizing an unexpected connection — a technique from a neighboring field, an offhand remark in a talk, a dataset that becomes available. Without a live list of open questions, these moments pass unrecognized. With it, you catch them.

This habit converts passive learning into active retrieval. When you read or study anything, you are running a background match against your 10 questions. Research on learning suggests that reading with an active question in mind improves retention and comprehension substantially compared with passive reading, because the question focuses attention and creates a retrieval hook that the new material can attach to.

The list should be reviewed monthly and revised as your understanding deepens. Some questions will get answered and drop off. Others will sharpen. New ones will emerge. What matters is that it stays live and current — a genuine representation of what you most want to figure out, not a legacy list you have stopped thinking about.

Protecting Time for Big-Picture Thinking

Identifying important problems is necessary but not sufficient. You also have to protect time to think about them, which is harder than it sounds. Day-to-day work expands to fill available time, and the most urgent tasks are almost never the most important ones. Hamming solved this problem with a structural commitment: he dedicated Friday afternoons to what he called "great thoughts only" — no current tasks, no deadlines, just thinking about where his field was going and what the big open questions were.

The Friday afternoon practice is not about having good ideas on a schedule. It is about creating the conditions under which important ideas can surface. Hamming believed that deep emotional investment in a problem — genuine commitment — feeds the subconscious, which does much of the real creative work. Protecting regular thinking time is how you keep that background process running.

Hamming also stressed that the right kind of commitment requires holding two attitudes simultaneously: believe in your current theory enough to act on it, but doubt it enough to notice its flaws. He called this ambiguity tolerance — the capacity to work confidently on a hypothesis while remaining genuinely open to evidence that you are wrong. This is distinct from either blind conviction or paralytic uncertainty.

The compound interest argument reinforces why protecting this time matters so much over a career. Hamming observed that "knowledge and productivity are like compound interest." A modest consistent investment in important, generative problems — just ten percent more effort each year directed at questions that actually matter — compounds dramatically over a decade. The researcher who spends every Friday afternoon thinking about the right questions is, by year ten, working with a fundamentally different picture of their field than the one who never makes that investment.

One structural nudge Hamming offered: keep your door open. An open door invites interruptions, which feel like productivity losses, but those interruptions carry information about what colleagues are actually struggling with — which is usually a more reliable signal about what matters than your own planning assumptions. The willingness to be interrupted is a form of staying calibrated.

A common misconception is that the Friday afternoon practice is a luxury for people who are not busy enough, or that blocking off reflection time is only possible once you have achieved enough seniority. Hamming's point is the opposite: the practice is most valuable precisely when you are busy, because busyness is the condition that makes it hardest to notice when you are working hard on the wrong things. Protecting thinking time is not a reward for having your schedule under control — it is the mechanism by which you regain control of it.

Communication as Half the Work

Hamming estimated that at least 50% of a researcher's effort should go toward communicating results. This estimate surprises most people, because the implicit model of research work is that the real effort is in the doing — the experiments, the analysis, the synthesis — and communication is a cleanup task afterward. Hamming rejected this model entirely.

His argument is practical: results that are not communicated effectively do not propagate. They do not influence other people's work, do not attract resources and collaborators, and do not survive into the next generation of thinking in a field. Work that is brilliant but impenetrable is, from the perspective of actual impact, nearly equivalent to work that was never done.

Hamming identified three forms of communication that matter and that require deliberate practice: clear writing (the ability to make complex ideas legible on the page), formal talks (the ability to structure an argument for a live audience), and spontaneous in-meeting advocacy (the ability to make a compelling case without preparation, when the moment presents itself). All three are learnable skills, and all three reward consistent investment.

A practical principle Hamming offered for all three: lead with the broad picture before diving into details. Most technical communicators invert this order — they build up from the details to the conclusion, because that mirrors how they arrived at the result. The audience, however, cannot evaluate details without context. Leading with the big picture first lets listeners orient themselves and follow the argument, rather than accumulating facts they cannot yet place.

The strategic framing point is related: when you solve a problem, resist the temptation to solve only the specific case in front of you. Reframe the problem so that your solution generalizes. A solution to one instance is a deliverable; a solution to a class of instances is a contribution. The generalized version is also more communicable, because it gives the audience something they can apply to their own situations.

Hamming summarized the relationship between preparation and opportunity with a phrase he attributed to Pasteur: "Luck favors the prepared mind." The open-questions list, the Friday afternoon practice, and the investment in communication are all forms of preparation — ways of being ready to recognize and act on the opportunities that appear unpredictably but reward whoever is positioned to exploit them.
