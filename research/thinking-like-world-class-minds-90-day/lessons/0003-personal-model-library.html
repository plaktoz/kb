Your Personal Model Library — Feynman Meets Munger

Feynman's Toolbox: Why One Technique Went So Far

Richard Feynman's edge wasn't raw intelligence — it was having a different toolbox. While his peers worked through the standard curriculum, Feynman self-taught from advanced texts outside it. He discovered a calculus technique — differentiating under the integral sign — that most physicists had never learned. Because he was the only one in the room who knew it, problems that stumped everyone else collapsed quickly for him.

The lesson is not that Feynman found a secret trick. The lesson is his method: master one thing deeply, then apply it as broadly as possible. He didn't spread himself thin across dozens of half-learned techniques. He went deep on a small set and practiced using them across wildly different problems until the application became instinctive.

This is the same move Charlie Munger recommends when building a latticework of mental models. Start with a core set — roughly 80 to 90 models from biology, economics, physics, and psychology. Learn each one well enough to explain it simply. Then practice applying it to problems far outside its original domain.

Building Your Personal Model Library

A personal model library is not a reading list. It is a curated set of thinking tools you have actually internalized — models you can reach for without looking them up.

The place to start is not with the most impressive models. Start with the ones you already use, even informally. You probably reason about incentives when you wonder why someone is behaving strangely. You probably use inversion when you ask "what could go wrong?" before committing to a plan. Naming these moves and writing them down is the first step toward owning them deliberately.

Once you have named a model, practice it. Pick one — inversion is a good starting point — and spend a full week applying it to everything: a career decision, a coding problem, a relationship conflict. This is depth before breadth. The goal is to reach a point where the model runs automatically, not where you have memorized its definition.

Diverse mental models combined with Feynman-style simplification produce clearer thinking and better decisions. The combination matters: breadth of models without simplification becomes noise; simplification without breadth becomes a hammer in search of every nail.

Cross-Pollination: The Multi-Lens Question

Once you have a small library of internalized models, a new move becomes available: cross-pollination. Instead of attacking a problem with the first model that comes to mind, you deliberately ask the same question through multiple lenses simultaneously.

A practical version: when you are stuck on a hard problem at work, pause and ask three questions in sequence.

  • How would a biologist see this? What are the feedback loops? What is adapting, and to what?
  • How would an economist see this? What are the incentives? Who bears the cost; who captures the benefit?
  • How would a physicist see this? What is the system? Where is the energy or momentum, and what is conserved?

The first alien perspective — the one that comes from a field completely unlike your own — often breaks the deadlock. This is because your default framing carries invisible assumptions. A lens from outside your domain strips those assumptions away.

The underlying principle: all perspectives hold some truth; none contain the complete truth. The goal is not to find the one correct lens. The goal is to let multiple lenses illuminate different faces of the same problem.

Collecting mental models is not the same as owning them. Reading a list of 100 models and being able to name them is trivia, not thinking. Feynman's method is the opposite: pick fewer models, apply each one deliberately and repeatedly until it becomes a reflex. Breadth without depth produces a library you can catalog but not use.

Liquid Knowledge: Making Models Flow Between Fields

The endpoint of this practice is what James Clear calls liquid knowledge — understanding that flows freely between fields rather than staying locked in the domain where you first encountered it. A model becomes liquid when you can apply it without consciously remembering which discipline it came from.

Getting there requires two things: studying outside the standard curriculum, and practicing transfer deliberately. The first means reading foundational texts from unfamiliar disciplines — not summaries, but the primary sources where the core ideas were first worked out. The second means noticing, every time you apply a model, whether you are using it in its home domain or carrying it somewhere new.

Feynman's technique for testing whether knowledge had become liquid was to try to explain it simply — without jargon, without appeal to authority, using only the clearest language you could find. If you could not do that, you did not yet own the idea. The simplification step is not optional. It is the test.

Check your understanding

What does Feynman's approach to building a personal model library suggest you do first?

Start your personal model library. Write 3 models you already use — even informally. For each one: give it a name, write a one-sentence definition, and give one real example from your own life. This is the seed of your latticework.

Use this format for each entry:
Model name — One-sentence definition. Example: [something from your life].

Want to go deeper? Ask your tutor: "Help me apply inversion to a real decision I'm facing right now" — or share your three models above and ask for a cross-pollination challenge.
