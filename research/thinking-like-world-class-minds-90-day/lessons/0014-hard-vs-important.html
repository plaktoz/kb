Hard vs. Important — Don't Confuse the Two

~20 min Module 4: Deciding What to Do Next
Primary source Ben Kuhn — benkuhn.net/hard/
Key result: difficulty and importance are separate dimensions that often don't overlap. Education trains people to optimize for puzzle-solving difficulty, producing a flawed mental model for real-world impact. Exceptional thinkers redirect their intelligence toward problem selection and toward building systems that solve problems at scale.

The Education Trap: Training for Difficulty

School is a closed system. It rewards you for solving problems that have clear, verifiable answers — and it calibrates those problems on a difficulty scale. The harder the problem you can solve, the higher your grade. This creates a strong and largely invisible incentive: to seek out difficult challenges as a proxy for doing something worthwhile.

The incentive makes sense inside the system. In school, choosing an easy problem when you could have solved a harder one genuinely does waste your ability. The performance ceiling is low and fixed. Every student gets the same problem set. Under those conditions, difficulty and quality of effort are closely linked.

But the mental model this produces — that difficulty signals importance — travels poorly into real life. It is one of the more consequential mismatches between how education prepares people to think and how effective work actually operates.

The most common misconception is that a hard problem is, by virtue of its difficulty, worth solving. In reality, hardness and importance are independent dimensions. A task can be technically demanding and completely inconsequential. A task can be straightforward and genuinely transformative. Difficulty is a property of the solution path. Importance is a property of the outcome. They do not measure the same thing.

Reality Has Orders of Magnitude More Range

Outside school, the performance ceiling disappears. Kuhn's central observation is that real-world outcomes vary by orders of magnitude — from negligible to potentially transformative global impact. The best possible outcome in school is a perfect grade. The best possible outcome in reality has no fixed upper bound.

This means the question "am I solving this problem well?" matters far less than the question "am I working on the right problem?" In a high-ceiling environment, your problem selection determines your impact range before your execution quality has any say.

This is why the examples of exceptional thinkers Kuhn cites are not people who tackled the hardest problems available to them — they are people who chose problems that turned out to matter far more than others expected:

  • Linus Torvalds invested serious effort in version control tooling — git — that many engineers dismissed as a solved and unremarkable problem. The leverage turned out to be enormous.
  • Donald Knuth spent decades on TeX, a typesetting system that most considered a peripheral concern. It became foundational infrastructure for scientific communication.
  • Andrew Ng chose to democratize machine learning education rather than continue publishing research papers. The decision to work on access rather than novelty reached vastly more people.

In each case, the leverage came from the problem chosen, not from the intrinsic difficulty of the work. Some of these problems even appeared beneath the person's technical ability — which is exactly what makes them instructive.

Redirecting Cleverness: The One Diagnostic Question

Kuhn offers a practical test for evaluating how you are spending your intellectual effort: "Is this where I want to spend my cleverness?"

The word "cleverness" is deliberate. It names the finite resource you are allocating — not time in the abstract, but the specific quality of focused intelligence. The question is not whether the task is hard enough to deserve your attention. It is whether this particular expenditure of mental effort is aimed at the right target.

In the real world, intelligence can be redirected in ways school never permits. You can apply it to faster execution of a simpler solution. You can apply it to better problem selection upstream. You can apply it to building systems and tools that eliminate the problem at scale rather than solving it once. None of those redirection options exist inside a fixed problem set with a locked-in grade.

"Don't look for hard problems — important ones are ultimately more fun." The real leverage comes from choosing what to work on, not from maximizing the difficulty of what you have already agreed to work on. Intelligence directed at problem selection compounds differently than intelligence directed at execution.

Applying the Distinction: Work, Career, and Daily Decisions

The hard-vs-important distinction has two practical applications: at the task level and at the career level.

At the task level, Kuhn's test is direct: list your most time-consuming tasks and score each one independently on difficulty and on importance. Hard-but-not-important tasks are candidates for delegation, simplification, or elimination. A complex solution built over months can be outperformed by a simpler solution built quickly — provided the simpler solution addresses the right problem. Complex solutions carry hidden costs: they are harder to maintain, slower to change, and more expensive to explain. Simplicity aimed at the right target usually wins.

At the career level, the trap runs deeper. The temptation is to optimize skills within your current role — to become more technically masterful at whatever you already do. Technical mastery does compound. But it only compounds if it is aimed at the right problem. The prior question — "is this role pointed at an important problem?" — has to come before the execution question. Accumulating skill in the wrong direction is not neutral; it is opportunity cost measured in years.

Catching yourself mid-task with "is this where I want to spend my cleverness?" is the practical habit. It does not require a career overhaul. It requires a moment of deliberate reorientation before committing to how a unit of effort gets used.

List your 5 most time-consuming tasks this week. For each one, assign two independent ratings:

  • Hard (1–5): How much cognitive effort or skill does this require?
  • Important (1–5): How much does the outcome of this task actually matter?

Once you have your ratings, identify the task with the highest hardness score and the lowest importance score. That is your candidate. Write below: what would happen if you stopped doing it, delegated it to someone else, or found a substantially simpler way to do it? What is the real cost of each option?
