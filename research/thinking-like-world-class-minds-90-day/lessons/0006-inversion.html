Inversion — Solving Problems Backwards

⌛ ~20 min Module 2: Signal vs. Noise
Primary Source: Inversion — Farnam Street fs.blog/inversion — Inversion means asking "what would guarantee failure?" and then avoiding those things. Carl Jacobi and Charlie Munger both discovered that many hard problems yield when attacked backwards rather than forwards.

The Logic of Thinking Backwards

Most of us attack problems in the same direction: we define what we want and then ask how to get there. That forward approach feels natural, but it has a blind spot — it keeps your attention fixed on the path to success while leaving failure modes invisible. Inversion corrects that blind spot by reversing the question entirely.

The mathematician Carl Jacobi recognized this pattern and made it a working rule: invert, always invert. He found that many problems that resisted frontal attack crumbled when he asked what the opposite outcome would require. The same logic transfers from mathematics to business to daily life. Instead of only asking "How do I achieve X?", you also ask "What would guarantee the opposite of X?" — and then you avoid those things.

Inversion does not replace forward thinking. It supplements it. The power comes from running both directions simultaneously: you build toward the goal while systematically clearing away the conditions that would sink it. The result is a more complete picture of the problem than either direction alone could produce.

Munger and the Art of Avoiding Stupidity

Charlie Munger, Berkshire Hathaway's vice chairman and one of the most rigorous thinkers in modern business, made inversion a cornerstone of his worldview. His formulation is direct: "All I want to know is where I'm going to die, so I'll never go there."

That line is not a joke. It captures a genuine asymmetry that Munger exploited throughout his career: avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance. Trying to engineer success requires correctly predicting what will work, which is hard. Identifying the reliable causes of failure — overconfidence, ignoring incentives, fragile single points of failure — and staying clear of them is comparatively straightforward.

Applied to strategy, the inversion looks like this. Forward thinking asks: "How do we foster innovation?" Inverted thinking asks: "What kills innovation in organizations?" — and then those conditions get eliminated. Inversion uncovers hidden assumptions you would never notice thinking only forward, because it forces you to examine the structure of failure rather than the structure of success.

"Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance." Munger's insight is that negative knowledge — knowing what destroys value, what causes projects to fail, what kills trust — is often more actionable than positive knowledge about how to succeed. Remove the failure conditions first; success becomes the path of least resistance.

The Pre-Mortem: Inversion Before It Matters

A pre-mortem is inversion applied to planning. Before a project starts — not after it ends — you imagine that it has already failed. You project yourself six months into the future and ask: "This project is over and it failed. What went wrong?"

The pre-mortem was developed as a countermeasure to a specific cognitive trap: the planning fallacy. When we plan, we tend to reason forward from best-case assumptions, which makes us systematically overconfident. The pre-mortem forces a shift to a different perspective. By treating failure as a given rather than a possibility, it removes the social pressure to be optimistic and makes it psychologically safe to voice concerns that would otherwise go unspoken.

Ray Dalio obsessively stress-tests strategies against worst-case scenarios as a standard practice. Linus Torvalds designs systems for failure modes first, treating the failure analysis as the primary design constraint. In both cases, the logic is the same: the pre-mortem surfaces problems while they are still cheap to fix, rather than discovering them after the cost of failure has already been paid.

The practical structure is simple. Before launch: spend ten minutes imagining the project has failed six months from now. Write the most plausible reasons it failed. For each reason, identify one thing you can do now to reduce that risk. The whole exercise takes less time than most status meetings, and its return — decisions made with fuller information about downside risk — is disproportionately high.

Subtractive Reasoning: Why Removing Harm Beats Adding Good

Inversion points toward a broader principle: subtractive reasoning. Adding things — features, steps, commitments, complexity — carries hidden costs that are easy to underestimate. Removing bad things is often safer, more durable, and more effective than adding good things.

This asymmetry shows up everywhere. In diet, eliminating processed food produces more reliable results than adding supplements. In code, removing unnecessary complexity reduces bugs more reliably than adding error-handling. In decisions, eliminating the worst options often clarifies the choice more than analyzing the best ones. Harm is easier to identify and avoid than benefit is to engineer and sustain.

Inversion operationalizes this. When you ask "what would make this definitely fail?", you are doing subtractive reasoning: you are looking for the conditions to eliminate rather than the conditions to create. It forces genuine understanding by requiring you to examine multiple perspectives — the perspective of what success needs, and the perspective of what failure needs — and the second perspective is usually more legible.

Inversion is sometimes mistaken for pessimism — a bias toward expecting failure. It is the opposite. Inversion is a tool for being more realistic about risk so that you can act more confidently. Running a pre-mortem does not mean you expect the project to fail; it means you are taking success seriously enough to identify and eliminate the conditions that would prevent it. The pessimist avoids starting. The inversion thinker starts better.
