Learn by Reproducing — Karpathy's Method

~20 min Module 3: Structured Thinking & Communication
Primary source Andrej Karpathy — karpathy.github.io/2022/03/14/lecun1989/
Key result: re-implementing a 1989 deep learning paper from scratch and isolating one variable at a time revealed that simple modern improvements (cross-entropy loss, AdamW, dropout) cut error rates ~60% without any architectural overhaul — and that the macro-level principles of deep learning have remained surprisingly stable across three decades.

The Experiment: Re-implementing 33 Years of Progress

In 2022, Andrej Karpathy took a canonical 1989 deep learning paper and rebuilt it from scratch. His stated goal was to use the project as "a case study on the nature of progress in deep learning." By reconstructing what was known at the time — the original architecture, loss function, optimizer, and training procedure — he created a controlled baseline he could then interrogate systematically.

The result was clarifying in a way that reading dozens of survey papers would not have been. When you rebuild something yourself, every assumption the original authors left undocumented becomes a decision you are forced to make consciously. Gaps in your understanding cannot hide behind fluency with the vocabulary.

Karpathy's observation that "not much has changed on the macro level" across thirty-three years is only visible from this vantage point. Without the reconstruction, the surface-level complexity of modern deep learning makes it easy to mistake novelty for fundamental change. The rebuild strips that away.

"Deep understanding comes from doing — rebuilding, measuring, and projecting — not just reading about results." Active reconstruction forces your gaps into the open. Reading about a result keeps them hidden.

Variable Isolation: Changing One Thing at a Time

Once Karpathy had a working baseline, he did not apply every modern improvement at once. He swapped components one at a time — loss function, then optimizer, then data augmentation, then regularization — and measured the impact of each change independently.

This is the same discipline used in controlled experiments: isolate the variable, hold everything else constant, and observe the outcome before moving on. The findings were concrete. Simple substitutions produced outsized gains:

  • Replacing the original loss function with cross-entropy produced a measurable improvement on its own.
  • Switching to AdamW as the optimizer added further gains.
  • Adding dropout as a regularizer contributed additional error reduction.
  • Together, these straightforward changes cut errors roughly 60% — without touching the underlying architecture.

This finding carries a practical principle: more data often beats more architectural complexity at the same inference cost. The compulsion to reach for structural novelty frequently obscures the return available from simpler, well-understood improvements.

A common misconception is that re-implementing something is only useful if you are trying to extend or improve it. In fact, the value comes before any improvement is made — in the act of reconstruction itself. You are not trying to build something better. You are trying to find out what you do not actually know.

History as a Mirror: Benchmarking Against the Past

By working with a 1989 baseline, Karpathy transformed the past into a measuring instrument. The question was not simply "what works today?" — it was "what changed, and why does it matter?" That second question is far harder to answer without a concrete historical anchor.

This is the principle that Karpathy calls "think in trajectories." Rather than taking a snapshot of the current state of a field and treating it as the ground truth, a trajectory view asks: where did this come from, what has been consistent across time, and where does the trend line point? The 1989 re-implementation made the trajectory visible in a way that a literature review of current papers would not.

The same habit appears across high-caliber thinkers. Donald Knuth re-derives algorithms from first principles rather than citing them by name. Richard Feynman famously refused to accept a result he could not re-derive himself. Jeff Dean builds intuition for large-scale systems by reconstructing their behavior from primitive components. The pattern is consistent: the reconstruction is the learning.

Applying Active Reconstruction Beyond Research

Karpathy's method is not confined to machine learning. The logic applies wherever you have acquired knowledge by reading or watching rather than by building.

  • Learning a new tool or framework: Do not just follow a tutorial. Complete the tutorial once, then close the documentation and build the same output from scratch. The places where you get stuck are exactly the places where you understood the words but not the underlying logic.
  • Inheriting a system or process at work: Before making any changes, try to reproduce the system's logic from scratch on paper. This will surface assumptions the original author treated as obvious and never documented — the same gaps that trip up every subsequent maintainer.
  • Auditing a career skill: Pick one skill you use regularly and believe you know well — writing a performance review, building a financial model, designing a presentation structure. Rebuild it entirely from memory, without your notes or templates. Where you reach for a reference is where your actual understanding ends.

In each case, the exercise produces a map of real gaps — not the gaps you thought you had, but the ones that exist regardless of how confident you feel when explaining the topic to others.

Check your understanding

What does Karpathy's 1989 paper re-implementation demonstrate about gaining deep understanding of a subject?

Identify one skill you "know" but have never rebuilt from scratch — a process, a model, a framework, a tool you use regularly. It could be writing a performance review, building a spreadsheet model, explaining a technical architecture, or constructing an argument from evidence.

Spend 30 minutes rebuilding it entirely from memory, without your notes, templates, or documentation. Do not look anything up until the time is up.

Afterward, write down exactly where you got stuck. Those are your real gaps — the difference between fluency with the vocabulary and genuine structural understanding.
