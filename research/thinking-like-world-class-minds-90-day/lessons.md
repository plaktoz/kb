## Module 1 — The Foundation: First Principles & Mental Models
*This module comes first because everything else in the system builds on one shared premise: most people reason by analogy, inheriting assumptions. These three lessons replace that default with a deliberate practice of stripping problems to bedrock and building up from there.*

### Lesson 1: First Principles — Strip Every Problem to Bedrock
**File:** `lessons/0001-first-principles.html`
**Key concepts:** first-principles thinking · Socratic questioning · Five Whys · assumption mapping
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/first-principles/ — First-principles thinking separates verified truths from inherited assumptions; Musk used it to found SpaceX by asking what rockets are *actually made of* (materials = ~2% of market price)
**Skill:** Write a problem you're currently facing. List every assumption underneath it. Circle the ones you haven't verified. Write what you'd do differently if those circled assumptions were false.
**Reference doc:** `reference/first-principles-techniques.html`

---

### Lesson 2: Munger's Latticework — Thinking Across Disciplines
**File:** `lessons/0002-mental-models-latticework.html`
**Key concepts:** mental models · latticework · cross-domain thinking · inertia · bottlenecks · trade-offs · Red Queen Effect
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/mental-models/ — Munger's framework: pull the strongest concepts from multiple disciplines, weave them together; power emerges from combination, not mastery of a single field
**Skill:** Pick one mental model from the lesson (Inertia, Bottleneck, Trade-off, or Red Queen). Write one paragraph applying it to a real current situation in your work or life. Make the model do actual work, not just describe it.
**Reference doc:** `reference/mental-models-library.html`

---

### Lesson 3: Your Personal Model Library — Feynman Meets Munger
**File:** `lessons/0003-personal-model-library.html`
**Key concepts:** personal model library · cross-pollination · liquid knowledge · depth-before-breadth · multi-lens questioning
**Source paper:** https://jamesclear.com/feynman-mental-models — Feynman's edge was a different toolbox: he mastered one calculus technique and applied it broadly; combined with Munger's latticework, the result is 80–90 core models applied with Feynman-style simplification
**Skill:** Start your personal model library. Write 3 models you already use (even informally). For each: name it, write one-sentence definition, give one real example from your life. This is the seed of your latticework.
**Reference doc:** *(extend existing mental-models-library.html)*

## Module 2 — Separating Signal from Noise
*This module comes second because a good thinking system is useless if it's fed bad inputs. These three lessons build the filtering layer: what to let in, where your edge is, and how to think about failure before it happens.*

### Lesson 4: The Frequency Problem — Why More Info Makes You Dumber
**File:** `lessons/0004-signal-vs-noise.html`
**Key concepts:** signal vs. noise · frequency bias · information overload · deliberate rationing · reactive thinking
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/noise-and-signal-nassim-taleb/ — Daily data checks yield ~95% noise; hourly is ~99.5% noise. Overexposure transforms signal-focused thinkers into reactive people who mistake randomness for meaningful patterns.
**Skill:** Track your information check-ins for one day. Write: how many times did you check Slack/email/news? For each check, rate: was it signal (changed what you did) or noise (didn't change anything)? Calculate your personal signal-to-noise ratio.
**Reference doc:** `reference/signal-noise-filter.html`

---

### Lesson 5: Circle of Competence — Know Your Edge
**File:** `lessons/0005-circle-of-competence.html`
**Key concepts:** circle of competence · earned knowledge · confidence calibration · selective competition · intellectual honesty
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/circle-of-competence/ — Confidence erodes slower than competence when you leave your edge. Munger and Buffett skipped pharmaceuticals entirely; Rose Blumkin built an empire by mastering only furniture and real estate.
**Skill:** Draw your circle. Three columns: "I know deeply" / "I know a little" / "I'm guessing." Fill in at least 5 items per column for your professional domain. Then identify one area where you've been acting from "I know a little" as if it were "I know deeply."
**Reference doc:** `reference/competence-circle-template.html`

---

### Lesson 6: Inversion — Solving Problems Backwards
**File:** `lessons/0006-inversion.html`
**Key concepts:** inversion · pre-mortem · failure-mode thinking · subtractive reasoning · avoiding stupidity
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/inversion/ — Inversion: ask "what would guarantee failure?" then avoid those things. Munger: "Avoiding stupidity is easier than seeking brilliance." Dalio stress-tests every strategy against worst-case scenarios; Linus designs for failure modes first.
**Skill:** Pick a goal or project you're working on. Run a 10-minute pre-mortem: imagine it's 6 months from now and it failed. Write the 5 most likely reasons it failed. For each reason, write one thing you can do now to prevent it.
**Reference doc:** *(extend first-principles-techniques.html)*

## Module 3 — Structured Thinking and Communication
*This module comes third because now that you have clean inputs and a model library, you need reliable techniques for reasoning through hard problems and transmitting the results clearly. These five lessons are the engine room.*

### Lesson 7: The Feynman Technique — 4 Steps to Deep Understanding
**File:** `lessons/0007-feynman-technique.html`
**Key concepts:** Feynman technique · simplification · gap detection · teaching as testing · jargon as disguise
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/feynman-technique/ — Feynman's 4 steps: map what you know, explain it to a 12-year-old, identify gaps where explanation breaks down, test by teaching an actual person. "Anyone can make a subject complicated but only someone who understands can make it simple."
**Skill:** Pick a concept you use at work but have never tried to explain simply. Write a 150-word explanation for a smart 12-year-old. Identify the first sentence where you felt stuck or vague — that's your real learning target for this week.
**Reference doc:** `reference/feynman-technique-guide.html`

---

### Lesson 8: Learn by Reproducing — Karpathy's Method
**File:** `lessons/0008-learn-by-reproducing.html`
**Key concepts:** active reconstruction · variable isolation · benchmarking against history · projecting from patterns · doing over reading
**Source paper:** https://karpathy.github.io/2022/03/14/lecun1989/ — Karpathy re-implemented a 1989 deep learning paper from scratch to extract timeless principles. Method: swap one component at a time, measure independently. "Deep understanding comes from doing — rebuilding, measuring, and projecting — not just reading about results."
**Skill:** Identify one skill you "know" but have never rebuilt from scratch (a process, a model, a framework). Spend 30 minutes rebuilding it from memory without your notes or documentation. Write down exactly where you got stuck — those are your real gaps.
**Reference doc:** `reference/feynman-technique-guide.html` *(extend)*

---

### Lesson 9: Paradigm Shifts as Design Problems — Karpathy's Reasoning Framework
**File:** `lessons/0009-paradigm-shifts-design-problems.html`
**Key concepts:** analogy-driven abstraction · paradigm mapping · cross-domain pattern detection · forward extrapolation · limitation acknowledgment
**Source paper:** https://karpathy.medium.com/software-2-0-a64152b37c35 — Karpathy builds understanding through structural analogies. Key move: "What does this paradigm shift demand of everything surrounding it?" — turns observations into design problems. He surveys multiple domains to let empirical breadth build the case.
**Skill:** Identify one change happening in your field right now (a new tool, a new process, a new expectation). Apply Karpathy's question: "What does this demand of everything surrounding it?" Write 3 specific things this change demands that don't exist yet — in your team, your skills, or your industry.
**Reference doc:** `reference/signal-noise-filter.html` *(extend)*

---

### Lesson 10: Writing IS Thinking — The Paul Graham Principle
**File:** `lessons/0010-writing-is-thinking.html`
**Key concepts:** writing as idea generation · drafts as thinking · imperfect starts · revision as reasoning · intellectual output
**Source paper:** https://paulgraham.com/writing44.html — Graham: "Writing doesn't just communicate ideas; it generates them." ~80% of ideas in an essay happen after you start writing. Perfectionism blocks the process. The act of revision IS the thinking — not polish applied after.
**Skill:** Write a 200-word rough draft on any problem you're currently thinking about. No editing allowed until you reach 200 words. Then read it back and underline the sentence that surprised you most — that's the idea your thinking generated that you didn't have before you started.
**Reference doc:** `reference/writing-thinking-system.html`

---

### Lesson 11: Independent Thinking & Forming Strong Opinions
**File:** `lessons/0011-independent-thinking-strong-opinions.html`
**Key concepts:** intellectual independence · opinion formation · Munger standard · unfashionable ideas · calibrated certainty · Darwin's method
**Source paper:** https://www.paulgraham.com/think.html + https://fs.blog/the-work-required-to-have-an-opinion/ — Graham's 3 components of independent thinking: truth-fastidiousness, resistance to consensus, curiosity. Munger standard: "I never allow myself to have an opinion unless I know the other side's argument better than they do."
**Skill:** Pick a position you hold at work or in your field. Write the strongest possible argument *against* your position — from the perspective of a smart person who disagrees. If you can't write that argument convincingly, you don't yet understand your own position well enough to hold it.
**Reference doc:** `reference/writing-thinking-system.html` *(extend)*

## Module 4 — Deciding What to Do Next & The Daily System
*This module comes last because everything converges here: how to choose what to work on, how to think about consequences, and how to make this entire system a daily habit that compounds. This is where the 90-day progression becomes a permanent operating system.*

### Lesson 12: Work on Important Problems — The Hamming Framework
**File:** `lessons/0012-important-problems-hamming.html`
**Key concepts:** important vs. tractable problems · 10 open questions · Friday thinking time · compound productivity · communication as 50% of the work
**Source paper:** https://www.cs.virginia.edu/~robins/YouAndYourResearch.html — Hamming: "It is not the consequence that makes a problem important, it is that you have a reasonable attack." Maintain 10–20 open questions. Reserve time for big-picture thinking. "Knowledge and productivity are like compound interest."
**Skill:** Write your 10 most important open questions — problems in your career, field, or life you'd most like to solve. These should feel slightly uncomfortable to write because they're real. Then: for each question, write one sentence on your "reasonable attack" — what angle you'd take if you started on it today.
**Reference doc:** `reference/open-questions-template.html`

---

### Lesson 13: Second-Order Thinking — "And Then What?"
**File:** `lessons/0013-second-order-thinking.html`
**Key concepts:** second-order consequences · "and then what?" · 10-10-10 projection · first-level vs. second-level thinking · hidden tradeoffs
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/second-order-thinking/ — Howard Marks: first-level thinking is "fast, simple, superficial"; second-level asks "what are others missing?" Dalio: "Failing to consider second- and third-order consequences is the cause of a lot of painfully bad decisions." Many opportunities look first-order negative but second-order positive.
**Skill:** Take a decision you made in the last month. Apply second-order thinking: what did you expect to happen (first-order)? What actually happened? What will happen next (second-order)? Write the full chain. Then apply it to one upcoming decision: run "And then what?" at least 3 times.
**Reference doc:** `reference/decision-journal-template.html`

---

### Lesson 14: Hard vs. Important — Don't Confuse the Two
**File:** `lessons/0014-hard-vs-important.html`
**Key concepts:** difficulty vs. importance · problem selection · leverage · cleverness allocation · school vs. real-world mindset
**Source paper:** https://www.benkuhn.net/hard/ — "Don't look for hard problems — important ones are ultimately more fun." Education trains people to optimize for difficulty. Exceptional thinkers redirect intelligence toward problem *selection* and building systems that solve problems at scale. Test: "Is this where I want to spend my cleverness?"
**Skill:** List your 5 most time-consuming tasks this week. For each: rate it Hard (1–5) and Important (1–5) independently. Plot them mentally. Identify your hardest-but-least-important task — what would happen if you stopped doing it, delegated it, or simplified it?
**Reference doc:** `reference/open-questions-template.html` *(extend)*

---

### Lesson 15: The 90-Day Daily Loop — Your Cognitive Operating System
**File:** `lessons/0015-daily-loop-system.html`
**Key concepts:** deliberate practice · decision journal · Eisenhower matrix · prediction tracking · compounding habits · daily loop
**Source paper:** https://fs.blog/deliberate-practice/ + https://fs.blog/eisenhower-matrix/ — Deliberate practice: target specific cognitive sub-skills, work in the learning zone, measure outcomes. Eisenhower: "What is important is seldom urgent." Daily structure: 20-min morning (model + prediction), 10-min midday (bias check), 15-min evening (review + journal).
**Skill:** Design your personal daily loop. Write the exact 3-part structure for tomorrow: (1) What model or concept will you review in the morning, and what prediction will you write? (2) What decision will you reflect on at midday? (3) What will you log in the evening? Run it tomorrow and write what happened.
**Reference doc:** `reference/decision-journal-template.html` *(extend)*

## Suggested Teaching Order

Follow lessons 1–15 sequentially. Each module layer adds on the previous: you need a model library (Module 1) before you can filter what to load into it (Module 2); you need clean inputs before structured reasoning pays off (Module 3); and you need the reasoning techniques before the prioritization and daily system make sense (Module 4). Do not skip ahead — the compounding logic is the curriculum.
