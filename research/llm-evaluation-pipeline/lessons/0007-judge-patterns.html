Judge Patterns — Direct, Pairwise, and Reference-Based

Three Approaches to LLM-as-a-Judge

When you ask an LLM to evaluate a generated output, you face an immediate structural choice: evaluate one response on its own, compare two responses head-to-head, or check a response against a known-good answer. Each approach has a distinct performance profile and is better suited to different task types. Understanding the tradeoffs prevents you from applying the wrong tool and misreading the resulting scores.

LLM-human agreement currently sits at roughly 0.3–0.6 correlation, still well below human-human agreement of 0.8–0.9. LLM judges also show high sensitivity for consistent content but low recall — only 30–60% — when detecting actual defects in outputs. Selecting the right structural approach is the first lever for narrowing that gap.

Direct Scoring

Direct scoring presents the judge with a single response and asks it to assign a numeric rating, usually on a defined scale. It is the simplest and fastest pattern — one prompt, one call, one score.

Direct scoring works best for objective tasks such as factuality checking and toxicity detection, where the criteria can be stated unambiguously and the judge does not need to weigh one answer against another to form a judgment. When aggregating results, use Cohen's kappa (κ) for binary or categorical outcomes and Spearman's ρ or Kendall's τ for ordinal or ranking data. Cohen's κ adjusts for chance agreement, making it more conservative than correlation-based metrics; Spearman's ρ and Kendall's τ are higher but carry no chance adjustment. For production use, Recall and Precision tend to be the most actionable metrics.

Chain-of-Thought (CoT) prompting improves direct scoring accuracy, especially for larger models, by forcing the judge to reason through criteria before committing to a score.

Pairwise Comparison

Pairwise comparison presents two candidate responses side by side and asks the judge to choose the better one — or declare a tie. Because the judge is anchored by a concrete alternative, it is more reliable for subjective qualities such as tone, helpfulness, and persuasiveness, where no single rubric captures the difference cleanly.

Research confirms that pairwise comparison generally outperforms direct scoring for subjective tasks. The preferred agreement metric is Cohen's κ, which accounts for the chance that the judge would pick a winner at random.

The main cost of pairwise comparison is volume: evaluating n responses requires O(n²) comparisons for a full tournament. In practice, you can reduce this with a round-robin bracket or by limiting comparisons to close pairs.

Reference-Based Evaluation

Reference-based evaluation compares a model output against a gold-standard answer using sophisticated fuzzy-matching rather than exact string comparison. Unlike BLEU or ROUGE, which count token overlaps, reference-based LLM evaluation can handle paraphrase, synonym substitution, and structural variation while still assessing fidelity to the ground truth.

This pattern requires a curated reference set — an upfront cost that pays dividends in objectivity. It is particularly useful for closed-book QA, summarization faithfulness, and code correctness where an authoritative answer exists. The tradeoff is that gold standards can go stale and may not cover the long tail of real queries.

A practical note: finetuned evaluators excel on in-domain tasks but suffer catastrophic performance drops when applied to different evaluation schemes. GPT-4 offers better generalizability across diverse evaluation types, making it preferable when you need a single evaluator to cover multiple rubrics.

Bias Taxonomy

All three patterns are vulnerable to systematic biases that inflate or deflate scores in predictable ways. Knowing the taxonomy lets you design prompts and pipelines that mitigate each bias before it contaminates your results.

  • Position bias — LLMs tend to favor responses placed in certain positions (often first or last) during pairwise comparison, regardless of actual quality. Mitigation: swap positions and average the two judgments.
  • Verbosity bias — Models show a preference for longer responses regardless of quality. A longer but vague answer can outscore a shorter but precise one. Mitigation: instruct the judge to evaluate substance, not length.
  • Self-enhancement bias — Models favor outputs generated by themselves, giving their own completions a 10–25% higher win rate on average. Mitigation: use a different model family as judge, or cross-evaluate with multiple judges.

Cost Optimization: PoLL

GPT-4-class models are accurate LLM judges, but their cost makes large-scale evaluation prohibitive. The Panel of LLM Judges (PoLL) framework answers this directly: a panel of smaller models, aggregated by majority vote or averaging, can match GPT-4 judgment quality at roughly one-seventh the cost.

PoLL also has a side benefit for bias reduction. Because self-enhancement bias is model-specific, a multi-model panel partially cancels out individual model preferences. No single model dominates the final verdict.

The practical implication: reserve GPT-4 for calibration and edge cases, and run PoLL for high-volume production evaluation.

Decision Framework

The four-cell framework below maps task type and deployment phase to the recommended evaluator pattern and metric.

Scenario Recommended Pattern Recommended Metric
Objective task (factuality, toxicity) Direct scoring Classification metrics (Precision, Recall, F1)
Subjective task (tone, persuasiveness) Pairwise comparison Cohen's κ
Development / offline eval Prompt LLM API with CoT + n-shot Spearman's ρ or Kendall's τ
Production guardrail (low latency) Finetune a specialized classifier Recall / Precision at threshold

One caveat: finetuned classifiers outperform general-purpose LLMs on the specific task they were trained for, but degrade sharply when the evaluation scheme changes. Build them last, once the rubric is stable.

Pairwise comparison is more reliable than direct scoring for subjective qualities, but the right structural choice depends entirely on whether your task is objective or subjective — applying direct scoring to tone evaluation or pairwise comparison to factuality checking will give you noisy, misleading results.
A higher LLM-human correlation score does not mean the evaluator is catching defects reliably. LLM judges show high sensitivity for consistent content but only 30–60% recall on actual defects. An evaluator can look well-calibrated on aggregate while missing the specific failure modes that matter most in production.
