Semantic Metrics — BERTScore, ROUGE/BLEU, and Their Limits

🕑 ~13 min Module 2 Lesson 6 of 12

In the previous lesson we saw how execution-based evals let running code be the judge. But most LLM outputs are not executable: summaries, replies, explanations. How do you measure quality when there is no test suite to run and no single correct answer?

Semantic metrics occupy the middle tier of the evaluation stack. They sit above pure string matching but below the cost and complexity of an LLM judge. Used well, they give fast, reproducible signal without requiring a human reviewer for every output. Used carelessly, they produce numbers that correlate poorly with what humans actually care about.

Embedding-Based Scorers: BERTScore and MoverScore

The core limitation of n-gram methods (covered below) is that they are purely lexical. If the model writes “decline” where the reference says “reject,” every unigram comparison penalizes the output even though the intent is identical. Embedding-based scorers solve this by replacing character-level or word-level equality with a semantic similarity check using vector representations.

BERTScore

BERTScore encodes both the candidate and reference texts using a BERT-like pretrained language model, producing one contextual embedding per token. It then computes the maximum cosine similarity between each token in the candidate and every token in the reference, and aggregates those similarities into precision, recall, and F1 scores.

Because the embeddings are contextual — the vector for “bank” differs depending on whether it appears next to “river” or “money” — BERTScore captures whether two tokens carry equivalent intent even when the surface forms differ. This makes it meaningfully better than ROUGE or BLEU at handling paraphrase.

Cosine Similarity and Embedding Similarity More Broadly

A simpler cousin of BERTScore is plain embedding similarity: encode both texts as single dense vectors (via a sentence encoder) and compute the cosine similarity between the two vectors. This is faster and easier to implement, but loses the token-level alignment that gives BERTScore its advantage on longer texts.

MoverScore extends the idea further by using Earth Mover’s Distance across the full token embedding distributions, penalizing not just mismatch but also the “transport cost” of moving probability mass between tokens. In practice BERTScore and MoverScore are used interchangeably in most pipelines; the choice depends on tooling and latency budget rather than a strong empirical difference.

Embedding-based methods inherit the biases of the pretrained model used to produce their embeddings. If the underlying model encodes certain linguistic or cultural patterns as semantically closer than they are in your domain, scores will be skewed. This contextual bias is the primary vulnerability of all embedding-based scorers.

N-Gram Metrics: ROUGE and BLEU

Before contextual embeddings became cheap, the dominant evaluation approach was to count how many n-grams in the candidate text also appear in the reference. ROUGE and BLEU remain the most-cited metrics in NLP papers and are still appropriate for narrow, well-defined tasks — but only those tasks.

ROUGE

ROUGE stands for Recall-Oriented Understudy for Gisting Evaluation. It measures the overlap between generated and reference text, with recall (how much of the reference is captured) as the primary lens. Three variants matter in practice:

  • ROUGE-1 — unigram overlap; fastest and most common; misses phrase-level patterns.
  • ROUGE-2 — bigram overlap; penalizes any change in adjacent word order; more sensitive than ROUGE-1 but also more brittle.
  • ROUGE-L — longest common subsequence; does not require consecutive matches, so it is more robust to word-order variation than ROUGE-2.

All three variants favor lexical overlap over semantic meaning. A response that paraphrases every sentence in the reference while conveying identical information will score near zero. For RAG tasks in particular, ROUGE and BLEU show poor correlation with human judgment.

BLEU

BLEU measures n-gram precision: out of all the n-grams the model produced, how many also appear in the reference? It was originally designed for machine translation, where a single sentence typically has only a handful of acceptable wordings. In that narrow context — short outputs, tight reference sets, mostly substitution-free language — BLEU is reasonable.

Applied to open-ended generation (summaries, explanations, chat replies), BLEU is a poor fit. Its precision framing rewards brevity and safe lexical choices over genuine quality, and it provides no mechanism for giving partial credit to semantically equivalent expressions.

The Reference-Dependence Problem

Both BLEU and ROUGE are reference-dependent: the quality of the metric is constrained by the quality of the reference texts. A weak or incomplete reference will penalize a correct and fluent response. A reference with idiosyncratic wording will reward responses that mimic that style over responses that communicate more clearly. This means that investing in high-quality reference sets is just as important as selecting the metric itself.

NLI Classifiers and the Middle-Tier Pattern

NLI-Based Classifiers

A third class of semantic evaluator treats the relationship between two texts as a classification problem. Natural Language Inference (NLI) classifiers are fine-tuned to label text pairs as entailment, neutral, or contradiction. This makes them well-suited for one specific task: checking whether a generated summary contradicts the document it was drawn from.

The main weakness is length. NLI models struggle with lengthy texts, both because input window limits force chunking and because long-range logical consistency is harder to classify. They work best on short, self-contained propositions.

In RAG pipelines, NLI classifiers (or the related concept of context faithfulness) check whether the generated text derives strictly from the retrieved chunks. A response that introduces facts not present in the context will be flagged, even if those facts are factually correct.

The Middle-Tier Pattern

Semantic metrics are most useful when exact match is too strict but an LLM judge would be overkill. A concrete example: you have a known-good answer for a classification-style question and want to check whether the model’s response conveys the same meaning, allowing for minor wording variation. Running a full LLM judge on thousands of such examples is expensive. Running ROUGE will penalize any legitimate paraphrase. An embedding similarity check or BERTScore gives a fast, cheap proxy that is good enough for this middle ground.

Use traditional n-gram metrics (BLEU, ROUGE) cautiously — only for translation or summarization overlap tasks where lexical fidelity genuinely matters. For everything else, prefer embedding-based similarity or move directly to an LLM judge.

Metric Comparison at a Glance

Metric Mechanism Best Use Case Key Limitation
BLEU N-gram precision vs. reference Machine translation with tight reference sets Poor fit for open-ended generation; penalizes all paraphrase
ROUGE-1/2 Unigram / bigram overlap vs. reference Extractive summarization overlap Misses paraphrases; poor correlation with human judgment on RAG tasks
ROUGE-L Longest common subsequence Summarization with flexible word order Still lexical; reference quality constrains score ceiling
Embedding similarity Cosine similarity of sentence vectors Quick semantic match check against a known-good answer Single vector loses token-level nuance; inherits pretrained model bias
BERTScore Token-level contextual embedding similarity (precision, recall, F1) Semantic similarity where paraphrase is acceptable Contextual bias from underlying model; slower than n-gram methods
MoverScore Earth Mover’s Distance over token embeddings Same as BERTScore; alternative with transport-cost penalty Same vulnerability to pretrained model bias
NLI classifier Entailment / contradiction classification Faithfulness check: does summary contradict its source? Struggles with long texts; requires chunking for RAG
Semantic metrics occupy the middle tier of the eval stack: faster and cheaper than an LLM judge, more meaning-aware than string matching. The single most important decision is recognizing which tier you actually need — reach for BERTScore or embedding similarity when paraphrase matters, use ROUGE/BLEU only when strict lexical fidelity is the actual goal, and escalate to an LLM judge when nuanced reasoning is required.
ROUGE is routinely applied to RAG answers, chatbot replies, and open-ended explanations — tasks for which it was not designed and where it shows poor correlation with human judgment. The metric was built for extractive summarization, where the expected output is a subset of the source text. Outside that narrow window, a low ROUGE score can mean the model paraphrased elegantly, not that it failed.

Metric Selection for Three Output Types

You are evaluating three different LLM outputs. For each one, choose the single semantic metric that fits best, then write one or two sentences explaining why the other candidates would be weaker choices.

  1. One-word classification — The model must output exactly one label from a fixed set (e.g., “positive”, “negative”, “neutral”). There are reference labels for every example.
  2. Paragraph summary — The model produces a 3–5 sentence summary of a news article. Reference summaries exist but were written by different annotators and vary in phrasing.
  3. Customer support reply — The model writes a free-form reply to a customer complaint. No single reference answer exists; instead, you have a set of known-good replies from senior agents.

Consider: exact match, ROUGE-1, ROUGE-L, BLEU, embedding similarity, BERTScore, and NLI classifier. You do not have to use every option.
